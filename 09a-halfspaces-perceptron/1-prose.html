The filter that listens to one word

Monday morning, and the complaints are polite but pointed. The facilities team sent out “Free lunch at the meeting, meeting room 4” and half the office never saw it: the filter put it in junk. Meanwhile a message that said “Claim your free prize” sailed straight through for some people and got caught for others, depending on how the spammer spelled things that week.

You open the filter and find the culprit quickly. Over the last few lessons the team has grown fond of rules that look at a single feature, because they are cheap to learn and easy to explain. The one in production says: junk exactly when the word “free” appears at least once. A rule like that, which looks at one feature and compares it with a threshold, is called a single-feature rule. It is fast, it is readable, and it is deaf to everything except one word.

The team has already done the first half of the fix. Instead of the yes/no features of lesson 1, every message is now turned into a vector of word counts: pick a vocabulary of ddd words, and let xjx_jxj​ be the number of times word jjj appears in the message. With a vocabulary of two words, “free” and “meeting”, the message “free trial, book a meeting” becomes the list (2,1)(2, 1)(2,1): two of the first word, one of the second. A message becomes a point x∈Rd\mathbf x \in \mathbb R^dx∈Rd. Bold letters like x\mathbf xx are whole lists of numbers, called vectors, and plain xjx_jxj​ is entry jjj of that list; R\mathbb RR is the set of real numbers, Rd\mathbb R^dRd is the set of all lists of ddd real numbers, and ∈\in∈ reads “is in”. So x∈Rd\mathbf x \in \mathbb R^dx∈Rd just says the message is a list of ddd numbers, and (2,1)∈R2(2, 1) \in \mathbb R^2(2,1)∈R2. What the team wants now is a filter that weighs the evidence: a lot of “free” pushes towards junk, a lot of “meeting” pushes towards keep, and the verdict comes from the balance, not from whichever word happens to be loudest.

So you write down the obvious thing. Give every word a weight, add up the weighted counts, add a constant, and call the message junk when the total is positive. Then you ask the question this course has trained you to ask: how does the learner find the best weights? And here the brute-force instinct from lesson 2, “try every hypothesis in the class and keep the one with the smallest empirical risk”, runs into a wall. The weights are real numbers, so the class is infinite. Even if you settle for 64-bit floating-point weights, a vocabulary of ddd words plus the constant gives 264(d+1)2^{64(d+1)}264(d+1) hypotheses. Read that from the inside out. There are d+1d + 1d+1 numbers to choose, one weight per word and the constant; each is stored in 64 bits, so together they use 64(d+1)64(d+1)64(d+1) bits; and each bit can be 0 or 1, so the number of different settings is 2 multiplied by itself once per bit, which is what the exponent in 264(d+1)2^{64(d+1)}264(d+1) means. In one sentence: the number of filters you could store is 2 raised to the total number of bits in the weights. With thirty words that is 64⋅31=198464 \cdot 31 = 198464⋅31=1984 bits, so 219842^{1984}21984 filters, a number with 598 digits:

This lesson is about the class you just wrote down, the halfspaces, and about why learning it is not hopeless at all. In the right conditions, finding a consistent halfspace is a linear program (a standard optimization problem that off-the-shelf solvers handle, defined below), or a five-line loop with a guarantee on how long it runs. At the end the team will ask for two more things, a junk score and a probability of junk, and lesson 9b will answer both with the same linear idea.

Why brute force is hopeless, and what saves us

So the hypothesis class is HSdHS_dHSd​, and we would like to run ERM on it. As a reminder from lesson 2, ERM, empirical risk minimization, means: return a hypothesis with the smallest empirical risk, the fraction of labelled examples it gets wrong. Counting plain mistakes like that is the 0-1 loss: a mistake costs 1, a correct answer costs 0. The production rule, wrong on E and G, has empirical risk 2/8=0.252/8 = 0.252/8=0.25; our weighted score has 0. Two separate worries stand in the way.

The first is statistical. HSdHS_dHSd​ is infinite, so lesson 2’s finite-class bound says nothing. But lesson 6 gave us the tool for infinite classes, the VC dimension, the size of the largest set of points a class can label in every possible way, and later in this lesson we will prove that the VC dimension of HSdHS_dHSd​ is d+1d + 1d+1. Recall the two dials of PAC learning: ε\varepsilonε is the accuracy you ask for (true risk at most ε\varepsilonε), and δ\deltaδ is the chance of failure you tolerate (the guarantee holds with probability at least 1−δ1 - \delta1−δ). By the fundamental theorem of lesson 6, ERM over halfspaces is then a PAC learner, and the number of examples it needs grows with the number of words, not with the 219842^{1984}21984 settings of the weights: for thirty words, δ=0.05\delta = 0.05δ=0.05 and ε=0.1\varepsilon = 0.1ε=0.1, it is a few hundred times a constant, as we will compute at the end of the VC section. So statistically, halfspaces are fine.

The second worry is computational, and it is the one from the opening: how do you find the empirical risk minimizer among infinitely many halfspaces? In general, when no halfspace gets every example right (the agnostic case of lesson 3), this is known to be computationally hard. The usual way around it is to minimize a different, friendlier loss instead of the 0-1 loss; logistic regression, in lesson 9b, is one example, and lesson 12 studies the idea in depth.

But there is an important case where ERM is easy. Suppose some halfspace gets every training example right. That is the realizability assumption of lesson 2, and for halfspaces it has its own name: the training sequence is separable, because a hyperplane separates all the junk from all the keep. Our eight messages are separable; the score 2x1−2x2−12x_1 - 2x_2 - 12x1​−2x2​−1 proves it.

In the separable case the minimum empirical risk is 0, so an ERM hypothesis is exactly a halfspace with no mistakes on SSS. In homogeneous form, we are looking for a vector w∈Rd\mathbf w \in \mathbb R^dw∈Rd with

max⁡w∈Rd  ⟨u,w⟩subject toAw≥v,\max_{\mathbf w \in \mathbb R^d} \; \langle \mathbf u, \mathbf w \rangle \quad \text{subject to} \quad A \mathbf w \ge \mathbf v,w∈Rdmax​⟨u,w⟩subject toAw≥v,

Read the pieces one at a time.

  • A matrix AAA is a table of numbers; m×dm \times dm×d means mmm rows and ddd columns. In the puzzle, the rows are (3,0)(3, 0)(3,0) and (−1,−2)(-1, -2)(−1,−2), one per condition.

  • ≥\ge≥ between two lists compares them entry by entry, so Aw≥vA \mathbf w \ge \mathbf vAw≥v is all the conditions at once, with v=(1,1)\mathbf v = (1, 1)v=(1,1) holding the right-hand sides.

  • max⁡w∈Rd\max_{\mathbf w \in \mathbb R^d}maxw∈Rd​ says: over all feasible weight vectors, make the value that follows as large as possible. The subscript names what you are allowed to vary.

In plain words: among all weight vectors that pass every “at least” test, find one where a chosen weighted sum is largest. The candidate w=(1,−1)\mathbf w = (1, -1)w=(1,−1) gives Aw=(3,1)A \mathbf w = (3, 1)Aw=(3,1), which is at least (1,1)(1, 1)(1,1) entry by entry, so it is feasible, with objective value 1:

Our constraints yi⟨w,xi⟩>0y_i \langle \mathbf w, \mathbf x_i \rangle > 0yi​⟨w,xi​⟩>0 are almost of that form, except that they are strict, and an LP wants ≥\ge≥. The Perceptron

The LP is a perfectly good answer, but it hands the work to a solver. There is a much older algorithm, Rosenblatt’s Perceptron from 1958, that you can write in five lines, and that comes with a beautiful guarantee.

The idea, in words first: start with all weights at zero; find a message the current weights get wrong; if it is junk, add its vector to the weights, and if it is keep, subtract it; repeat until nothing is wrong. Starting from zero weights, every score is 0, so junk message A is wrong, and adding its vector (1,3,0)(1, 3, 0)(1,3,0) gives the weights (1,3,0)(1, 3, 0)(1,3,0).

Now the notation. The weights change from step to step, so we label them by step: w(t)\mathbf w^{(t)}w(t) means the value of w\mathbf ww at step ttt, with the ttt in brackets so it is not mistaken for a power. Start with w(1)=0\mathbf w^{(1)} = \mathbf 0w(1)=0, the all-zeros vector, which labels nothing correctly (every score is 0). At each step ttt, look for an example that w(t)\mathbf w^{(t)}w(t) gets wrong, that is, with yi⟨w(t),xi⟩≤0y_i \langle \mathbf w^{(t)}, \mathbf x_i \rangle \le 0yi​⟨w(t),xi​⟩≤0, a signed score that is not positive. If there is one, update by adding the message, scaled by its label:

w(t+1)=w(t)+yixi.\mathbf w^{(t+1)} = \mathbf w^{(t)} + y_i \mathbf x_i.w(t+1)=w(t)+yi​xi​.

If there is none, stop and output w(t)\mathbf w^{(t)}w(t). Read the update inside out: xi\mathbf x_ixi​ is the message’s vector, yixiy_i \mathbf x_iyi​xi​ multiplies every entry by the label (so it is the vector itself for junk, its negative for keep), and adding that to w(t)\mathbf w^{(t)}w(t) entry by entry gives the next weights w(t+1)\mathbf w^{(t+1)}w(t+1). In plain words: on a mistake, add the message to the weights if it is junk and subtract it if it is keep. Here is that first step:

It prints 0, the signed score that makes A a mistake, then [1, 3, 0].

How many updates? The mistake bound

Three updates on eight messages is quick. Is it always quick? Could the Perceptron chase its tail forever, fixing one example and breaking another? On separable data, no, and the proof is one of the loveliest in the subject. It needs two quantities.

The first is how far the messages reach. Among our eight, with the 1 glued on, the longest is C, (1,4,2)(1, 4, 2)(1,4,2), with squared length 1+16+4=211 + 16 + 4 = 211+16+4=21 and length 21≈4.58\sqrt{21} \approx 4.5821​≈4.58. In plain words: RRR is the length of the longest message vector.

The second is how short a separator can be while still scoring every example at least 1. Our (−1,2,−2)(-1, 2, -2)(−1,2,−2) does that (its smallest signed score is exactly 1), and its length is 1+4+4=3\sqrt{1 + 4 + 4} = 31+4+4​=3. Could a shorter vector pass the same test? The best such length is

### Radius and margin

That gap between 189 and 3 is worth understanding, and the cleanest way is to rewrite BBB as a picture.

The idea is room: how far the closest message sits from the separating hyperplane. Scores measure that distance once the weights have length 1. Halfspaces as code

Here are the two learners for separable data. Each takes mmm examples whose vectors already include the constant 1, so they live in Rn\mathbb R^nRn with n=d+1n = d + 1n=d+1.

HalfspaceLP(S):
  for i = 1..m:
    for j = 1..n:
      A[i][j] ← y_i · x_{i,j}
  v ← (1, ..., 1) in R^m
  u ← (0, ..., 0) in R^n
  w ← LPSolve(maximize ⟨u, w⟩ subject to A w ≥ v)
  return h(x) = sign(⟨w, x⟩)

Perceptron(S):
  w ← (0, ..., 0) in R^n
  loop:
    if some i has y_i · ⟨w, x_i⟩ ≤ 0:
      w ← w + y_i · x_i
    else:
      return h(x) = sign(⟨w, x⟩)

HalfspaceLP spends O(mn)O(mn)O(mn) time and space building AAA (as in lesson 8, O(mn)O(mn)O(mn) reads “at most a constant times mmm times nnn”; eight messages with three entries each is 24 multiplications), and then calls a solver whose running time is polynomial in mmm, nnn and the number of bits in the input. Which polynomial depends on the solver.

Perceptron keeps one vector, so it needs O(n)O(n)O(n) space beyond the data. Each pass through the loop scans for a mistake in O(mn)O(mn)O(mn) time, and by the mistake bound there are at most (RB)2(RB)^2(RB)2 updates, so the total is O(mn(RB)2)O(mn (RB)^2)O(mn(RB)2) time on separable data. On data that is not separable it never stops, so a real implementation also takes a cap on the number of updates. Predicting a new message costs O(n)O(n)O(n) for either learner.

What people get wrong

“The Perceptron needs about (RB)2(RB)^2(RB)2 updates.” It needs at most that many. The bound is a worst case over every order of the examples, and on our eight messages it promised 142 while the Perceptron used 3. What the bound buys is a guarantee you can write down before training; it is not a forecast.

“Any separator gives the bound (RB)2(RB)^2(RB)2.” A separator u\mathbf uu that you happen to know certifies (R/γu)2(R / \gamma_{\mathbf u})^2(R/γu​)2, which is at least (RB)2(RB)^2(RB)2 and can be much larger when u\mathbf uu barely separates. Only the widest separator gives (RB)2(RB)^2(RB)2 itself. That is the whole game of the lab’s second act.

“Halfspaces are easy to learn.” In the separable case, yes: a linear program, or the Perceptron. Without separability, finding a halfspace with the smallest empirical risk under the 0-1 loss is computationally hard. That is why the practical answer for messy data is to change the loss, as the logistic regression of lesson 9b does.

“The bias term is the inductive bias.” Same word, different ideas. The bias term bbb is one more weight, the score of a message with no evidence. The inductive bias is the decision to search halfspaces at all, made before seeing any data.
