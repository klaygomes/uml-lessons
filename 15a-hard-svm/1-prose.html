The filter that grazes

Your colleague’s complaint from the end of lesson 14 comes with an example. On that quiet week the labelled mail could be split perfectly by a line, and on separable mail the retraining job takes the shortcut of lesson 9a: it runs the Perceptron, which stops at the first line that makes no training mistakes, and operations likes it because the mistake bound says in advance how long the job can take. The example comes from the facilities team, who write in again, confused this time rather than annoyed.

On Monday, “free lunch at the meeting, meeting room 4” arrived in everyone’s inbox, exactly as it should. On Tuesday the same invitation went out with one small edit, “free lunch and free parking at the meeting, meeting room 4”, and the filter sent it to junk. One extra word. The message is obviously still a lunch invitation, and a filter that weighs the evidence should not change its mind over a single “free”.

You pull up the hypothesis the Perceptron returned and the training messages it was given, and the picture explains everything. The separating line does separate the training messages, every one of them. But it passes a hair’s breadth from the lunch invitation. Monday’s message sat on the keep side by a sliver; Tuesday’s extra “free” nudged it a short distance, and that was enough to cross.

So the training sequence was fine and the learner was correct by the rules we gave it. It found a consistent halfspace, and lesson 9a never asked for more. The trouble is that there are infinitely many consistent halfspaces, and the Perceptron returns whichever one its updates happen to land on. Some of them leave plenty of room around every message. Some graze. Which one should a learner return? And while we are at it, the team has a second worry: next month’s labels will contain a few messages that no halfspace can get right, because people disagree about what is junk. What does the learner do when the mail is only almost separable?

This lesson answers the first question, and lesson 15b answers the second. Together the answers have a name you have probably heard, the support vector machine, and the theory in this lesson already delivers something lesson 9a could not: a number of labels that does not grow with the size of the vocabulary.

The fix is not to stop using halfspaces. It is to stop accepting just any consistent halfspace, and to ask for the one that leaves the most room. To say that precisely we need a way to measure room, which means measuring the distance from a point to a hyperplane.

The distance to a hyperplane

First, the notation we will use for the whole lesson, read once on the Perceptron’s line. That line weighs “free” by 4, “meeting” by −1-1−1, adds −3-3−3, and calls the message junk when the total is positive. A halfspace is named by a pair (w,b)(\mathbf w, b)(w,b): it labels x\mathbf xx with sign⁡(⟨w,x⟩+b)\operatorname{sign}(\langle \mathbf w, \mathbf x \rangle + b)sign(⟨w,x⟩+b), and its boundary is the hyperplane {v:⟨w,v⟩+b=0}\{\mathbf v : \langle \mathbf w, \mathbf v \rangle + b = 0\}{v:⟨w,v⟩+b=0}.

  • w=(w1,…,wd)\mathbf w = (w_1, \dots, w_d)w=(w1​,…,wd​) is the weight vector, one weight per word, and bbb is the bias term, a number added at the end. Here w=(4,−1)\mathbf w = (4, -1)w=(4,−1) and b=−3b = -3b=−3.
  • ⟨w,x⟩=w1x1+⋯+wdxd\langle \mathbf w, \mathbf x \rangle = w_1 x_1 + \dots + w_d x_d⟨w,x⟩=w1​x1​+⋯+wd​xd​ is the inner product of lesson 9a: multiply matching coordinates and add.
  • ⟨w,x⟩+b\langle \mathbf w, \mathbf x \rangle + b⟨w,x⟩+b is the score; sign⁡\operatorname{sign}sign turns a positive score into +1+1+1 (junk) and a negative one into −1-1−1 (keep).
  • In {v:… }\{\mathbf v : \dots\}{v:…} the braces collect points and the colon reads “such that”: the set of points v\mathbf vv whose score is exactly 0. That set is the line you have been looking at.
  • ∥w∥=w12+⋯+wd2\|\mathbf w\| = \sqrt{w_1^2 + \dots + w_d^2}∥w∥=w12​+⋯+wd2​​ is the length of w\mathbf ww.

In plain words: a halfspace scores a message by weighing its counts and adding a constant, and labels it by the sign of the score; the hyperplane is where the score is zero. For G, (1,2)(1, 2)(1,2): ⟨w,x⟩=4−2=2\langle \mathbf w, \mathbf x \rangle = 4 - 2 = 2⟨w,x⟩=4−2=2, the score is 2−3=−12 - 3 = -12−3=−1, the sign says keep, and ∥w∥=17≈4.123\|\mathbf w\| = \sqrt{17} \approx 4.123∥w∥=17​≈4.123.

The distance between a point x\mathbf xx and the hyperplane is the length of the shortest gap between x\mathbf xx and a point on it:

dist⁡(x,(w,b))=min⁡{∥x−v∥  :  ⟨w,v⟩+b=0}.\operatorname{dist}(\mathbf x, (\mathbf w, b)) = \min \{ \|\mathbf x - \mathbf v\| \; : \; \langle \mathbf w, \mathbf v \rangle + b = 0 \}.dist(x,(w,b))=min{∥x−v∥:⟨w,v⟩+b=0}.

From the inside out: x−v\mathbf x - \mathbf vx−v is the arrow from a point v\mathbf vv to the message, and ∥x−v∥\|\mathbf x - \mathbf v\|∥x−v∥ is its length. The colon restricts v\mathbf vv to the hyperplane. The braces collect one length per such point, and min⁡\minmin picks the smallest. In plain words: the distance is how far the message is from the nearest point of the line. For G and the Perceptron’s line, the points of the line are (v1,4v1−3)(v_1, 4v_1 - 3)(v1​,4v1​−3); try them all on a fine grid and keep the shortest gap:

The margin, and the widest separator

Now the measure of room. For a training sequence S=((x1,y1),…,(xm,ym))S = ((\mathbf x_1, y_1), \dots, (\mathbf x_m, y_m))S=((x1​,y1​),…,(xm​,ym​)) with xi∈Rd\mathbf x_i \in \mathbb R^dxi​∈Rd and yi∈{±1}y_i \in \{\pm 1\}yi​∈{±1}, recall from lesson 9a that SSS is separable when some halfspace gets every example right, that is, some (w,b)(\mathbf w, b)(w,b) has

yi(⟨w,xi⟩+b)>0for all i∈[m].y_i (\langle \mathbf w, \mathbf x_i \rangle + b) > 0 \quad \text{for all } i \in [m].yi​(⟨w,xi​⟩+b)>0for all i∈[m].

Here [m][m][m] is the list of positions {1,…,m}\{1, \dots, m\}{1,…,m}, and yiy_iyi​ is example iii’s label, +1+1+1 or −1-1−1. Multiplying the score by the label gives the signed score: positive exactly when the score has the label’s sign, that is, when the halfspace gets example iii right. In plain words: some halfspace gives every training message a positive signed score. For G under the Perceptron’s line, the score is −1-1−1 and the label −1-1−1, so the signed score is +1+1+1: right. The first snippet’s loop tests exactly this, y * score(w, x) <= 0, and stops when no message fails it.

Every such halfspace has empirical risk 0 (the fraction of training messages it gets wrong, lesson 1), so every one of them is a valid output of ERM, the learner of lesson 2 that returns a hypothesis with the fewest training mistakes. The margin of a hyperplane with respect to SSS is the smallest distance between a training message and the hyperplane. By the claim, for a separating (w,b)(\mathbf w, b)(w,b) with ∥w∥=1\|\mathbf w\| = 1∥w∥=1, it is min⁡i∣⟨w,xi⟩+b∣\min_i |\langle \mathbf w, \mathbf x_i \rangle + b|mini​∣⟨w,xi​⟩+b∣, and since every signed score is positive, that is the same as min⁡iyi(⟨w,xi⟩+b)\min_i y_i (\langle \mathbf w, \mathbf x_i \rangle + b)mini​yi​(⟨w,xi​⟩+b). The subscript in min⁡i\min_imini​ says what varies: compute the expression for each example iii and keep the smallest. In plain words: the margin is the signed score of the worst-placed message, once w\mathbf ww is a unit vector. The Perceptron’s hyperplane has margin 0.243 on our eight messages, set by G: the smallest entry of the first snippet’s second line, which divides by ∥w∥\|\mathbf w\|∥w∥ to make the vector unit.

Why care about the margin? Because a hyperplane with a large margin still separates the training messages after you perturb each one a little: every message can move by anything less than the margin, in any direction, without crossing. A grazing hyperplane survives almost no perturbation at all. Later in this lesson we will also see that a large margin buys a small true risk, regardless of the dimension. That is the real prize.

Definition (hard-SVM). Hard-SVM is the learning rule that returns, among all halfspaces that separate SSS, one with the largest margin:

argmax⁡(w,b):∥w∥=1  min⁡i∈[m]∣⟨w,xi⟩+b∣subject toyi(⟨w,xi⟩+b)>0 for all i.\operatorname*{argmax}_{(\mathbf w, b) : \|\mathbf w\| = 1} \; \min_{i \in [m]} |\langle \mathbf w, \mathbf x_i \rangle + b| \quad \text{subject to} \quad y_i (\langle \mathbf w, \mathbf x_i \rangle + b) > 0 \text{ for all } i.(w,b):∥w∥=1argmax​i∈[m]min​∣⟨w,xi​⟩+b∣subject toyi​(⟨w,xi​⟩+b)>0 for all i.

When SSS is separable, this is the same as dropping the constraint and maximizing the smallest signed score directly,

argmax⁡(w,b):∥w∥=1  min⁡i∈[m]yi(⟨w,xi⟩+b),\operatorname*{argmax}_{(\mathbf w, b) : \|\mathbf w\| = 1} \; \min_{i \in [m]} y_i (\langle \mathbf w, \mathbf x_i \rangle + b),(w,b):∥w∥=1argmax​i∈[m]min​yi​(⟨w,xi​⟩+b),

and proving that the two agree is this lesson’s problem at the end. We will work with the second form.

Read it from the inside out. The inner part, min⁡i∈[m]yi(⟨w,xi⟩+b)\min_{i \in [m]} y_i (\langle \mathbf w, \mathbf x_i \rangle + b)mini∈[m]​yi​(⟨w,xi​⟩+b), is the margin of one candidate, as above. The subscript under argmax⁡\operatorname{argmax}argmax says which candidates compete: every pair (w,b)(\mathbf w, b)(w,b) such that w\mathbf ww is a unit vector. And argmax⁡\operatorname{argmax}argmax returns not the largest value but the candidate that achieves it. In plain words: among all unit halfspaces, return the one whose worst-placed message is as far as possible. The Perceptron’s unit pair, (4,−1,−3)/17(4, -1, -3)/\sqrt{17}(4,−1,−3)/17​, scores 0.243 in this contest. The snippet after the next lemma runs the whole contest by brute force.

“Maximize a minimum over a sphere” is an awkward thing to hand a computer. The standard trick is to fix the scale differently: instead of fixing ∥w∥=1\|\mathbf w\| = 1∥w∥=1 and maximizing the smallest score, fix the smallest score at 1 and minimize ∥w∥\|\mathbf w\|∥w∥. A quadratic program is an optimization problem whose objective is a convex quadratic function and whose constraints are linear inequalities; solvers for them are standard and run in polynomial time.

Hard-SVM(S):
  solve the quadratic program
    (w0, b0) = argmin ||w||^2
               over (w, b)
               subject to y_i (⟨w, x_i⟩ + b) ≥ 1 for all i
  return ŵ = w0 / ||w0||,  b̂ = b0 / ||w0||

The program reads: among all pairs (w,b)(\mathbf w, b)(w,b) such that every signed score is at least 1, find one with the smallest squared length ∥w∥2\|\mathbf w\|^2∥w∥2 (squaring removes the square root and changes nothing about which pair is shortest); then rescale it to unit length, the hats on w^,b^\hat{\mathbf w}, \hat bw^,b^ marking the returned pair. For the pair w0=(23,−13)\mathbf w_0 = (\tfrac23, -\tfrac13)w0​=(32​,−31​), b0=−1b_0 = -1b0​=−1 that we will meet shortly, ∥w0∥2=49+19=59\|\mathbf w_0\|^2 = \tfrac49 + \tfrac19 = \tfrac59∥w0​∥2=94​+91​=95​.

Why should the shortest w\mathbf ww give the widest margin? A message’s score is its distance to the hyperplane times ∥w∥\|\mathbf w\|∥w∥, so a short w\mathbf ww can only give every message a score of 1 if every message is far away.

Lemma (hard-SVM finds the widest separator). Suppose SSS is separable and contains both labels, and let (w0,b0)(\mathbf w_0, b_0)(w0​,b0​) solve the quadratic program. Then for every (w,b)(\mathbf w, b)(w,b) with ∥w∥=1\|\mathbf w\| = 1∥w∥=1 and γ=min⁡iyi(⟨w,xi⟩+b)>0\gamma = \min_i y_i (\langle \mathbf w, \mathbf x_i \rangle + b) > 0γ=mini​yi​(⟨w,xi​⟩+b)>0,

Here γ\gammaγ (gamma) is the margin of a competitor, and the left side is the margin of hard-SVM’s output. In plain words: no separating unit halfspace has a wider margin than the one hard-SVM returns. With the Perceptron as the competitor, γ=0.243\gamma = 0.243γ=0.243, and the left side will turn out to be 1.342. So (w^,b^)(\hat{\mathbf w}, \hat b)(w^,b^) has the largest margin of any separating halfspace: it solves the max-min problem.

Proof. First, w0≠0\mathbf w_0 \ne \mathbf 0w0​=0, so the output is defined: if w0=0\mathbf w_0 = \mathbf 0w0​=0, the constraints would read yib0≥1y_i b_0 \ge 1yi​b0​≥1 for all iii, forcing every yiy_iyi​ to have the sign of b0b_0b0​, but SSS contains both labels.

Without the bias term

It is often more convenient to work with homogeneous halfspaces, those with no bias term, x↦sign⁡(⟨w,x⟩)\mathbf x \mapsto \operatorname{sign}(\langle \mathbf w, \mathbf x \rangle)x↦sign(⟨w,x⟩), whose hyperplane passes through the origin. Hard-SVM for homogeneous halfspaces is the program

min⁡w∥w∥2subject toyi⟨w,xi⟩≥1 for all i.\min_{\mathbf w} \|\mathbf w\|^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad y_i \langle \mathbf w, \mathbf x_i \rangle \ge 1 \text{ for all } i.wmin​∥w∥2subject toyi​⟨w,xi​⟩≥1 for all i.

It is the program you just read with bbb gone: the shortest weight vector that gives every message a signed score of at least 1.

As in lesson 9a, a halfspace with a bias term becomes a homogeneous one after appending a constant 1 to every message: (1,x)(1, \mathbf x)(1,x) in Rd+1\mathbb R^{d+1}Rd+1, with the bias term as the first weight. But there is one difference worth noticing. The program with a bias term minimizes ∥w∥2\|\mathbf w\|^2∥w∥2 and leaves bbb free, while the homogeneous program on the lifted vectors minimizes b2+∥w∥2b^2 + \|\mathbf w\|^2b2+∥w∥2: it regularizes the bias term too, preferring small bbb. In general the two programs can return different halfspaces. In practice, regularizing bbb usually makes no significant difference to the number of examples needed, and the homogeneous form is much easier to analyze, so from here on the theory is homogeneous.

On our eight messages the two even agree: the homogeneous program on the lifted vectors returns the same (b,w1,w2)=(−1,23,−13)(b, w_1, w_2) = (-1, \tfrac23, -\tfrac13)(b,w1​,w2​)=(−1,32​,−31​), as the solver of lesson 15b will confirm. The margin is now measured in R3\mathbb R^3R3, where the length includes the bias term, so it is 1/1+49+19=3/14≈0.8021/\sqrt{1 + \tfrac49 + \tfrac19} = 3/\sqrt{14} \approx 0.8021/1+94​+91​​=3/14​≈0.802 rather than 1.342. Same hyperplane in the plane, different ruler.

Why a wide margin needs fewer labels

Here is the deeper reason to want a wide margin, and it is the reason this lesson exists.

Lesson 9a showed that homogeneous halfspaces in Rd\mathbb R^dRd (the space of vectors with ddd coordinates, one per word) have VC dimension ddd: they can label some ddd messages in every possible way, and no d+1d + 1d+1. By the fundamental theorem of lesson 6a, the number of examples needed to learn them grows with ddd, and, worse, for an accuracy target ε\varepsilonε (epsilon, the true risk you are willing to tolerate), if the number of examples is much smaller than d/εd/\varepsilond/ε, then no learner can guarantee true risk at most ε\varepsilonε for every distribution. For a filter with two words that is nothing. For a filter that tracks fifty thousand words it is a disaster: the label budget would have to grow with the vocabulary.

The way out is to assume something more about the distribution. There is no contradiction with the lower bound here: the lower bound says that some distributions need that many examples, and we are about to rule those distributions out with an extra assumption. The assumption is that the data are separable with a margin. But there is a catch to deal with first.

Suppose SSS is separable with margin γ\gammaγ. Multiply every message by 10. The same hyperplane (if it is homogeneous) still separates, and every distance is now ten times bigger, so the new training sequence is separable with margin 10γ10\gamma10γ. By scaling the data, you can make the margin as large as you like, and a measure of difficulty that changes when you change units is useless. The fix is to measure the margin relative to the size of the messages.

import math

S = [((5, 2), 1), ((3, 0), 1), ((4, 1), 1), ((4, 0), 1),
     ((0, 0), -1), ((0, 1), -1), ((1, 2), -1), ((1, 3), -1)]
X = [(1,) + x for x, _ in S]
w0 = (-1, 2 / 3, -1 / 3)
dot = lambda u, v: sum(a * b for a, b in zip(u, v))
for alpha in (1, 10, 0.1):
    Xa = [[alpha * c for c in x] for x in X]
    gamma = min(y * dot(w0, x) for x, (_, y) in zip(Xa, S))
    gamma /= math.sqrt(dot(w0, w0))
    rho = max(math.sqrt(dot(x, x)) for x in Xa)
    print(alpha, round(gamma, 4), round(rho, 4),
          round((rho / gamma) ** 2, 2))

Definition (separability with a (γ,ρ)(\gamma, \rho)(γ,ρ)-margin). Let D\mathcal DD be a distribution over Rd×{±1}\mathbb R^d \times \{\pm 1\}Rd×{±1}. We say D\mathcal DD is separable with a (γ,ρ)(\gamma, \rho)(γ,ρ)-margin if there is (w⋆,b⋆)(\mathbf w^\star, b^\star)(w⋆,b⋆) with ∥w⋆∥=1\|\mathbf w^\star\| = 1∥w⋆∥=1 such that, with probability 1 over (x,y)∼D(\mathbf x, y) \sim \mathcal D(x,y)∼D, both y(⟨w⋆,x⟩+b⋆)≥γy (\langle \mathbf w^\star, \mathbf x \rangle + b^\star) \ge \gammay(⟨w⋆,x⟩+b⋆)≥γ and ∥x∥≤ρ\|\mathbf x\| \le \rho∥x∥≤ρ. Reading it piece by piece: D\mathcal DD is the unknown source of labelled mail, and (x,y)∼D(\mathbf x, y) \sim \mathcal D(x,y)∼D reads “a message and its label drawn from D\mathcal DD”. The star on w⋆\mathbf w^\starw⋆ marks one fixed, ideal separator that we never get to see. “With probability 1” means the two inequalities hold for every message that can actually arrive. The first says that message’s signed score under the ideal separator is at least γ\gammaγ (gamma), so it sits at least γ\gammaγ from the hyperplane; the second says its length is at most ρ\rhoρ (rho). In plain words: some unit separator keeps every possible message at least γ\gammaγ from the boundary, and no message is longer than ρ\rhoρ. For the uniform distribution on our eight lifted messages, the scaling snippet’s first line gives γ=0.8018\gamma = 0.8018γ=0.8018 and ρ=5.4772\rho = 5.4772ρ=5.4772 (that is 30\sqrt{30}30​, the length of A’s lifted vector (1,5,2)(1, 5, 2)(1,5,2)).

The number ρ\rhoρ is the radius: every message lies in the ball of radius ρ\rhoρ. (Lesson 9a called the radius of a training sequence RRR; we switch to ρ\rhoρ here, because it will soon play a second role.) Scaling the data multiplies both γ\gammaγ and ρ\rhoρ, so the ratio ρ/γ\rho / \gammaρ/γ is the honest measure of difficulty.

Theorem (hard-SVM’s sample complexity depends on the margin, not the dimension). Let D\mathcal DD be a distribution over Rd×{±1}\mathbb R^d \times \{\pm 1\}Rd×{±1} that is separable with a (γ,ρ)(\gamma, \rho)(γ,ρ)-margin using a homogeneous halfspace. Then, with probability at least 1−δ1 - \delta1−δ over a training sequence S∼DmS \sim \mathcal D^mS∼Dm, the homogeneous hard-SVM hypothesis has 0-1 true risk at most

4(ρ/γ)2m+2log⁡(2/δ)m.\sqrt{\frac{4 (\rho / \gamma)^2}{m}} + \sqrt{\frac{2 \log(2/\delta)}{m}}.m4(ρ/γ)2​​+m2log(2/δ)​​.

First the notation. S∼DmS \sim \mathcal D^mS∼Dm reads “SSS is mmm examples drawn independently from D\mathcal DD”, and δ\deltaδ (delta) is the small chance we allow the training sequence to be unlucky, as in every probably approximately correct guarantee since lesson 2. The 0-1 true risk is the chance that the returned halfspace mislabels a fresh message from D\mathcal DD. Inside the first square root sits the difficulty ratio ρ/γ\rho/\gammaρ/γ, squared, divided by the number of labels; inside the second, log⁡(2/δ)\log(2/\delta)log(2/δ), which grows only slowly as you demand more confidence. In plain words: with high confidence, hard-SVM’s error rate on new mail is at most a difficulty term plus a confidence term, and both shrink like one over the square root of the number of labels. With ρ/γ=10\rho/\gamma = 10ρ/γ=10, m=1000m = 1000m=1000 and δ=0.05\delta = 0.05δ=0.05: 400/1000≈0.632\sqrt{400/1000} \approx 0.632400/1000​≈0.632, plus 2log⁡40/1000≈0.086\sqrt{2 \log 40 / 1000} \approx 0.0862log40/1000​≈0.086, about 0.718, the first line hard_svm_bound prints below.

This is the heart of the lesson: the dimension ddd appears nowhere, so a filter over fifty thousand words gets the same guarantee as one over two words with the same ratio ρ/γ\rho/\gammaρ/γ. Setting the bound equal to ε\varepsilonε, the number of labels needed grows like (ρ/γ)2/ε2(\rho/\gamma)^2/\varepsilon^2(ρ/γ)2/ε2, with no ddd. (Here log⁡\loglog is the natural logarithm, as everywhere in this course.)

The proof needs a tool we have not built yet, the Rademacher complexity of a class, and lesson 26 proves this theorem in full. Here is the bound as the label count grows, still with ρ/γ=10\rho/\gamma = 10ρ/γ=10 (the closest message a tenth of the radius from the best separator) and δ=0.05\delta = 0.05δ=0.05:

import math

def hard_svm_bound(rho_over_gamma, m, delta):
    estimation = math.sqrt(4 * rho_over_gamma ** 2 / m)
    confidence = math.sqrt(2 * math.log(2 / delta) / m)
    return estimation + confidence

for m in (1_000, 10_000, 100_000):
    print(m, round(hard_svm_bound(10, m, 0.05), 4))

The ratio ρ/γ\rho/\gammaρ/γ has met you before. Lesson 9a proved that on separable data the Perceptron makes at most (R/γ⋆)2(R/\gamma^\star)^2(R/γ⋆)2 updates, where γ⋆\gamma^\starγ⋆ is the best margin of a homogeneous separator. In this lesson’s letters that is (ρ/γ)2(\rho/\gamma)^2(ρ/γ)2, the very quantity that controls hard-SVM’s sample complexity. For our lifted eight messages it is 46.67 (from the scaling snippet), and the Perceptron used 5. The same ratio governs how fast the Perceptron finishes and how many examples a large-margin separator needs. That is not a coincidence: both are statements that wide, well-scaled margins make halfspaces easy.

A margin is a safety distance

Back to Tuesday’s lunch invitation. We have been saying, loosely, that a wide margin lets every message move a little without crossing. Before we lean on that to fix the bug, let’s make it a claim with a proof, because it is exactly the promise the facilities team is about to ask for.

Picture an edit as an arrow e\mathbf ee added to a message: adding one “free” to G is the arrow e=(1,0)\mathbf e = (1, 0)e=(1,0), which carries (1,2)(1, 2)(1,2) to (2,2)(2, 2)(2,2). Its length ∥e∥\|\mathbf e\|∥e∥ measures how big the edit is, and any edit that adds or removes a single word has length exactly 1. The question is how far the edit can push the signed score down. Here is the answer.

Claim (a margin is a safety distance). Let (w,b)(\mathbf w, b)(w,b) with ∥w∥=1\|\mathbf w\| = 1∥w∥=1 separate SSS with margin γ>0\gamma > 0γ>0. Then for every example iii and every edit e\mathbf ee,

yi(⟨w,xi+e⟩+b)≥γ−∥e∥.y_i (\langle \mathbf w, \mathbf x_i + \mathbf e \rangle + b) \ge \gamma - \|\mathbf e\|.yi​(⟨w,xi​+e⟩+b)≥γ−∥e∥.

So every edit shorter than γ\gammaγ leaves every training message strictly on its own side.

Read it from the inside out. xi+e\mathbf x_i + \mathbf exi​+e is the edited message. ⟨w,xi+e⟩+b\langle \mathbf w, \mathbf x_i + \mathbf e \rangle + b⟨w,xi​+e⟩+b is its score under the unit halfspace, and multiplying by the label yiy_iyi​ gives its signed score, positive when the halfspace still gets it right. On the right, γ\gammaγ is the margin, the smallest signed score before any edit, and ∥e∥\|\mathbf e\|∥e∥ is the length of the edit. In plain words: an edit can lower a message’s signed score by at most its own length, so a message that started at least γ\gammaγ from the boundary is still at least γ−∥e∥\gamma - \|\mathbf e\|γ−∥e∥ from it afterwards. For G under hard-SVM’s unit line, γ=1.342\gamma = 1.342γ=1.342 and the one-word edit has length 1, so the edited G keeps a signed score of at least 0.3420.3420.342.

Proof. The inner product is linear, so the edited signed score splits into the old one plus the edit’s share:

yi(⟨w,xi+e⟩+b)=yi(⟨w,xi⟩+b)+yi⟨w,e⟩.y_i (\langle \mathbf w, \mathbf x_i + \mathbf e \rangle + b) = y_i (\langle \mathbf w, \mathbf x_i \rangle + b) + y_i \langle \mathbf w, \mathbf e \rangle.yi​(⟨w,xi​+e⟩+b)=yi​(⟨w,xi​⟩+b)+yi​⟨w,e⟩.

The first term is at least γ\gammaγ, because γ\gammaγ is the smallest signed score over the training sequence. For the second, ∣yi∣=1|y_i| = 1∣yi​∣=1, and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality of lesson 9a, ∣⟨a,c⟩∣≤∥a∥ ∥c∥|\langle \mathbf a, \mathbf c \rangle| \le \|\mathbf a\| \, \|\mathbf c\|∣⟨a,c⟩∣≤∥a∥∥c∥, gives ∣⟨w,e⟩∣≤∥w∥ ∥e∥=∥e∥|\langle \mathbf w, \mathbf e \rangle| \le \|\mathbf w\| \, \|\mathbf e\| = \|\mathbf e\|∣⟨w,e⟩∣≤∥w∥∥e∥=∥e∥, since w\mathbf ww is a unit vector. So the second term is at least −∥e∥-\|\mathbf e\|−∥e∥, and the sum is at least γ−∥e∥\gamma - \|\mathbf e\|γ−∥e∥. When ∥e∥<γ\|\mathbf e\| < \gamma∥e∥<γ, that is positive. ■\blacksquare■

Let’s push G through it term by term: its signed score before the edit, the edit’s share y⟨w,e⟩y \langle \mathbf w, \mathbf e \rangley⟨w,e⟩, the signed score after, and the floor γ−∥e∥\gamma - \|\mathbf e\|γ−∥e∥ the claim promises.

import math

w, b = (2 / math.sqrt(5), -1 / math.sqrt(5)), -3 / math.sqrt(5)
x, y, e = (1, 2), -1, (1, 0)

The edit’s share is less than its full length because the arrow (1,0)(1, 0)(1,0) does not point straight at the line; an edit aimed exactly along −yiw-y_i \mathbf w−yi​w would use its whole length, and an edit of length γ\gammaγ aimed that way lands exactly on the hyperplane. Now all eight messages and all four one-word edits (one more or one fewer “free”, one more or one fewer “meeting”), under hard-SVM’s line and under the Perceptron’s. The function returns the margin and the smallest signed score after any single edit:

import math

S = [((5, 2), 1), ((3, 0), 1), ((4, 1), 1), ((4, 0), 1),
     ((0, 0), -1), ((0, 1), -1), ((1, 2), -1), ((1, 3), -1)]
edits = [(1, 0), (-1, 0), (0, 1), (0, -1)]

def worst_after_edit(w, b):
    n = math.hypot(*w)
    s = lambda x: (w[0] * x[0] + w[1] * x[1] + b) / n
    gamma = min(y * s(x) for x, y in S)
    after = min(y * s((x[0] + e[0], x[1] + e[1]))
                for x, y in S for e in edits)
    return round(gamma, 3), round(after, 3)

print(worst_after_edit((2, -1), -3))
print(worst_after_edit((4, -1), -3))

Fix the bug

Time to ship. The facilities team and operations sit down together and agree on a written promise for the separator that goes into the nightly job: every labelled message sits more than one word from the boundary. By the claim, that settles Tuesday once and for all. A one-word edit has length 1, so if the margin is more than 1, no message in the training sequence can be pushed across by adding or removing a single word.

Why write the promise as a distance, instead of just trying the edits? In the production filter a message has fifty thousand words it could gain or lose, the closest direction to the boundary is usually some mix of many words, and next week’s complaint will be about an edit nobody thought to test. The margin is one number that answers for every edit of length below it, in every direction at once. And a wide margin is exactly what the sample-complexity theorem rewards, so asking for one costs nothing on new mail.

In the lab below, each round brings one week of labelled mail on the two words, so you can see it, starting with our eight messages. You steer the separator with two controls: its direction, the angle of w\mathbf ww, and its offset, the bias term bbb of the unit pair. The lab draws a circle of radius 1, one word, around every message, computes the margin exactly, and names the message that sets it. A round passes only when the line separates and its margin is more than 1. Trying edits does not count: a line can dodge every edit you draw and still pass closer than one word to a message, and the promise is about distance. Three weeks in a row, and the filter ships.

[lab-act2: built on the lesson page]

What you just did by hand is what hard-SVM does by solving its quadratic program. By the lemma, its line has the largest margin of any separator, so whenever some line can keep the promise, hard-SVM’s line keeps it, with nothing to steer. On our eight messages it leaves 1.342, and the one-word promise holds with room to spare.

What people get wrong

“Every consistent halfspace is as good as any other.” They all have empirical risk 0, but they are not equally robust, and they do not carry the same guarantee. The Perceptron’s line on our eight messages left 0.243; hard-SVM’s left 1.342. The three keep messages that a one-word nudge sends to junk under the Perceptron’s line all stay keep under hard-SVM.

“A bigger margin is always better, so rescale the data.” Multiplying every message by 10 multiplies the margin by 10 and changes nothing real. The meaningful quantity is ρ/γ\rho/\gammaρ/γ, which scaling leaves alone. That is why the radius is part of every margin bound.

“If none of the edits I tried flips a message, the line is safe.” A line can dodge every edit on your list and still pass closer than one word to a message, because the direction straight toward the boundary is rarely the direction of a single word. On our eight messages, point the weight vector 50 degrees below the “free” axis and centre the line between the two groups: none of the four one-word edits of any message crosses it, yet A and E sit only 0.841 from it. The safety distance is the margin, and only the margin covers the edits you did not think of.

Recap and the next bug

  1. The margin of a separating hyperplane is the distance from it to the closest training message; for a unit w\mathbf ww that distance is ∣⟨w,x⟩+b∣|\langle \mathbf w, \mathbf x \rangle + b|∣⟨w,x⟩+b∣. An edit can lower a signed score by at most its own length, so every training message survives every edit shorter than the margin.
  2. Hard-SVM returns the separating halfspace with the largest margin. It is the quadratic program “minimize ∥w∥2\|\mathbf w\|^2∥w∥2 subject to every signed score being at least 1”, and the largest margin is 1/∥w0∥1/\|\mathbf w_0\|1/∥w0​∥.
  3. Margins only mean something relative to the radius ρ\rhoρ of the messages. If the distribution is separable with a (γ,ρ)(\gamma, \rho)(γ,ρ)-margin, hard-SVM needs a number of examples that grows with (ρ/γ)2(\rho/\gamma)^2(ρ/γ)2, with no dependence on the dimension.

The filter ships with its promise, and the facilities team gets its lunch invitations. A month later the nightly job runs hard-SVM on the new labels and, in the morning, there is no new filter, only a line in the log: the quadratic program has no feasible point. Among the new labels is a free consultation with exactly the counts of the lunch invitation, which your users called junk, and a farewell party full of free cake, which they called keep. No line separates the month’s mail, so hard-SVM has nothing to return. Real labels are noisy and people disagree, so this will happen again every month. What does a learner that loves wide margins do when the mail is only almost separable, and does its guarantee survive? That is lesson 15b.

  1. A colleague multiplies every lifted message vector (including the constant 1) by 10, “to make the margin bigger”. For homogeneous hard-SVM on our eight messages, what happens to the margin, to the radius ρ\rhoρ, to the hypothesis returned (as a labelling of messages), and to the bound of the hard-SVM sample-complexity theorem? Then explain why the definition of separability with a (γ,ρ)(\gamma, \rho)(γ,ρ)-margin needs ρ\rhoρ at all.
  2. Hard-SVM’s line on the eight messages has margin 1.342. Does the safety-distance claim promise that B, “FREE gift, FREE delivery, FREE”, stays junk if one “FREE” is swapped for a “meeting”? Work out the edited message’s score. What does this tell you about how much a margin can promise?
  3. The lemma that hard-SVM finds the widest separator assumes that SSS contains both labels. Give a separable training sequence with junk messages only, say what the quadratic program returns, and explain why hard-SVM’s output is then undefined. Where else does the proof need the competitor’s margin γ\gammaγ to be positive?
