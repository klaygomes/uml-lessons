# AdaBoost

Objective: after this, you can define decision stumps, weighted risk and \(\gamma\)-weak learnability (and say why weak learning is statistically as hard as strong learning but may be computationally easier), prove that ERM over stumps is a \(\tfrac1{12}\)-weak learner for three-piece hypotheses on the line (and has an edge of \(\tfrac16\) on every weighting of a finite sample), implement ERM over stumps in \(O(dm)\) per call after sorting, run AdaBoost by hand and in code, prove the bound \(L_S(h_s) \le e^{-2\gamma^2 T}\) step by step (and its sharper product form), and turn a guaranteed edge into a number of rounds that promises zero empirical risk on every training sequence.

Key words: message, word count (a feature; not "frequency", "term count"), hour (the hour a message was sent, 0 to 24, minutes as a fraction; act 2 only), junk \(+1\) / keep \(-1\) (not spam / ham), example, training sequence, hypothesis (not "classifier", "model"; "rule" only in the story's opening and in "rules of thumb"), decision stump (short form "stump"; not "split", "threshold rule"), base class \(B\) (not "weak class"), weak learner, strong learning (the lesson's name for PAC learning), edge \(\gamma\) (not "advantage" in formal text), distribution over the examples \(D\), weight \(D_i\) (belongs to an example), vote \(w_t\) (belongs to a hypothesis; never "weight" for \(w_t\)), weighted risk \(L_D(h)\) (not "weighted error"), round (not "iteration"), empirical risk (not "training error"), true risk, \(Z_t\) (no nickname), ceiling (the theorem's \(e^{-2\gamma^2 T}\); act 2's \(e^{-T/18}\)), schedule (the number of rounds fixed before any night runs), the desk (the support desk's shared inbox). Accuracy is \(\varepsilon\), confidence \(\delta\), sample size \(m\); AdaBoost's weighted risks are \(\varepsilon_t\) (same symbol family, as in the book).

Terms:
- decision stump: \(h_{j,\theta,b}(x) = b \cdot \operatorname{sign}(x_j - \theta)\), with \(\operatorname{sign}(u) = +1\) iff \(u > 0\).
- weighted risk: \(L_D(h) = \sum_i D_i \mathbb 1[h(x_i) \ne y_i]\) for a distribution \(D\) over the \(m\) examples; equals \(L_S\) when \(D\) is uniform.
- \(\gamma\)-weak learner (Definition 10.1): like PAC but with accuracy fixed at \(\tfrac12 - \gamma\) and \(m_{\mathcal H}: (0,1) \to \mathbb N\) depending on \(\delta\) only.
- three-piece hypothesis: \(h_{\theta_1,\theta_2,b}\), label \(b\) outside \([\theta_1, \theta_2]\), \(-b\) inside.
- AdaBoost: rounds \(t = 1..T\); \(h_t = \mathrm{WL}(D^{(t)}, S)\), \(\varepsilon_t = L_{D^{(t)}}(h_t)\), \(w_t = \tfrac12\log(1/\varepsilon_t - 1)\), \(D^{(t+1)} \propto D^{(t)} e^{-w_t y h_t}\), output \(\operatorname{sign}(\sum w_t h_t)\).
- \(Z_t = \frac1m\sum_i e^{-y_i f_t(x_i)}\), \(f_t = \sum_{p \le t} w_p h_p\); \(Z_T = \prod_t 2\sqrt{\varepsilon_t(1-\varepsilon_t)}\).

Misconception: "AdaBoost's empirical risk goes down every round". Why wrong: on the ten messages it stays at 0.2 after round 2; on the desk's night 0 it is 0 after round 3 and 0.16 after round 4; only \(Z_t\) is guaranteed to fall.
Second misconception: "a weak learner is a weak assumption". Why wrong: the edge must hold on every reweighting, which is what each round of the proof uses (and what the desk's schedule relies on).
Third misconception: "tonight's run was perfect after three rounds, so three rounds is a schedule". Why wrong: round 4 undoes it on 41% of simulated nights; simulated nights are not every night; tonight's \(Z_T < 1/300\) certifies tonight only after the run; only the ceiling \(e^{-T/18}\) is known in advance for every night (103 rounds).

Derivation:
- Weak learnability and its statistical equivalence to strong learning via the lower bound of Theorem 6.8 with \(\varepsilon = \tfrac12 - \gamma\).
- Example 10.1 with the \(\eta\) repair (see Assumptions), \(\varepsilon = \eta = 1/24\).
- ERM for stumps: \(\Theta_j\), update, both signs via \(1 - F\).
- Theorem 10.2: Step 1 \(\mathbb 1 \le e^{-yf}\); Step 2 telescoping, \(Z_0 = 1\); Step 3 induction for \(D^{(t+1)}\); Step 4 ratio \(= 2\sqrt{\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon)}\); Step 5 monotonicity of \(a(1-a)\), \(1 - a \le e^{-a}\). Plus: the vote minimizes the ratio; product form \(Z_T = \prod 2\sqrt{\varepsilon_t(1-\varepsilon_t)} \le e^{-2\sum\gamma_t^2}\).
- Remark 10.2: union bound, \(1 - \delta T\).
- The schedule (ours): finite-sample Step 1 of Example 10.1 for any weights gives \(\varepsilon_t \le \tfrac13\), \(\gamma = \tfrac16\), \(2\gamma^2 = \tfrac1{18}\); \(L_S \le e^{-T/18} < 1/m\) iff \(T > 18\log m\); \(m = 300\): \(18 \log 300 = 102.67\), \(T = 103\).

Algorithms: ERM-Stumps (mirror of the book's pseudocode, see Assumptions): \(O(dm\log m)\) per call with sorting, \(O(dm)\) per call with presorted indices (\(O(dm)\) space). AdaBoost: \(O(T(m + c_{WL}))\); with stumps \(O(dm\log m + Tdm)\) time, \(O(dm + m + T)\) space, \(O(T)\) per prediction. Build-it part 1: `erm_stump` (asserts: (0.2, (0, 0.5, 1)) on uniform weights; (0.125, (0, 2.5, 1)) on round 2's weights), brute-force comparison on 300 random examples, timing. Part 2: `adaboost`, `predict` (asserts: votes [0.693, 0.973, 1.282], all ten correct), \(Z_t\) printout, the lab mail experiment. All asserts run against the reference `boost.py` (`buildit_check.py`, `buildit_stubs_check.py`: pass). The desk's night job: one ERM-Stumps call on one feature is \(O(m)\) after a single sort, so 103 rounds on 300 messages cost about \(103 \cdot 300\) steps plus one \(O(m \log m)\) sort.

Concept check, answers (never shown to the learner; critique against these):
1. \(\varepsilon_t = 0.7\): \(w_t = \tfrac12\log(1/0.7 - 1) = \tfrac12\log(3/7) = -0.424\). A negative vote means the final vote uses \(-h_t\), whose weighted risk is 0.3, with vote 0.424. The ratio is \(2\sqrt{0.7 \cdot 0.3} = 0.917 < 1\), so \(Z\) still shrinks: Step 4 never used \(\varepsilon \le \tfrac12\); only Step 5 did, and \(2\sqrt{\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon)}\) is symmetric about \(\tfrac12\). \(\varepsilon_t = 0.5\): \(w_t = 0\), ratio 1, weights unchanged (multiplied by \(e^0\)), so no progress, and a deterministic weak learner returns the same hypothesis forever. \(\varepsilon_t = 0\): \(w_t\) is infinite; stop and return \(h_t\), which has empirical risk 0. Look for: the symmetry, and that a 30%-right hypothesis is as useful as a 70%-right one.
2. Step 4, the last two lines (plugging \(e^{\pm w}\)). With \(w = 1\), \(\varepsilon = 0.25\): \(e^{-1}(0.75) + e^{1}(0.25) = 0.9555\); AdaBoost's vote: \(2\sqrt{0.25 \cdot 0.75} = 0.8660\). The ratio \(e^{-w}(1-\varepsilon) + e^{w}\varepsilon\) is a strictly convex function of \(w\) minimized exactly at AdaBoost's \(w\) (derivative zero at \(e^{2w} = (1-\varepsilon)/\varepsilon\)), so any other vote gives a ratio at least as large in that round. Accept: "greedy per round", not "globally optimal over all rounds".
3. \(e^{-T/18} < 1/1000\) iff \(T > 18 \log 1000 = 124.34\), so \(T = 125\) (22 more than for 300: the rounds grow only with \(\log m\)). Independence: Step 1 of the three-piece argument holds for every position of the two cuts and every weighting of the examples, so every round of every night has \(\varepsilon_t \le \tfrac13\); the theorem needs nothing else about the night (it is a statement about any training sequence on which every round has the edge). Look for: naming the "any weights" part as what AdaBoost's reweighting needs, and noting realizability (the truth must really be three-piece; a night with label noise breaks the edge argument).

Assumptions:
- Orientation and sign convention are ours: stumps are \(b \cdot \operatorname{sign}(x_j - \theta)\) with \(\operatorname{sign}(0) = -1\), so \(b = +1\) says junk above the threshold. The book's ERM section writes \(\operatorname{sign}(\theta - x_i) \cdot b\) and fixes \(b = 1\), predicting \(+1\) when \(x \le \theta\), with update \(F - y_i D_i\); our pseudocode is its mirror image (update \(F + y_i D_i\), start at the total keep weight) and handles both signs at once via \(1 - F\), which the book leaves implicit.
- Example 10.1 repair: the book says a stump agrees with any pair of the three regions; for the two outer rays no stump on \(\mathbb R\) does exactly (stumps are never constant on \(\mathbb R\)), so the prose uses "within \(\eta\)" by continuity of probability and sets \(\varepsilon = \eta = 1/24\) (the book uses \(\varepsilon = 1/12\) and a sample size \(\Omega(\log(1/\delta)/\varepsilon^2)\)). VCdim 2 for stumps on the line is the book's claim; the prose leaves the check to the reader.
- Not in the book: the product form \(L_S \le \prod 2\sqrt{\varepsilon_t(1-\varepsilon_t)} \le e^{-2\sum\gamma_t^2}\) (immediate from the book's proof); the vote as the per-round minimizer of the ratio; the observation that \(\varepsilon_t > \tfrac12\) gives a negative vote and still shrinks \(Z\); the stopping rule for \(\varepsilon_t = 0\); the presorting trick's \(O(dm)\) per call across rounds (the book says \(O(dm)\) after a preprocessing sort).
- The desk's night job is ours: the hour feature, the moving window, 300 labels a night, the schedule framing, the finite-sample edge \(\tfrac16\) (Example 10.1's Step 1 with exact ERM and no \(\eta\), valid for every weighting), and its combination with Theorem 10.2 into \(T > 18 \log m\). The simulated nights are a model of the desk, not data; the prose says so.
- The mail world (word counts capped at 5, the ten words, the sum-at-least-21 truth, 20,000 labels) and the ten messages are invented for the lesson. The bug's "63%" and "five times in eight" come from that world, not from the book.
- History: Kearns and Valiant 1988, Schapire 1990 ("then a graduate student at MIT"), Freund and Schapire 1995, from pp. 130 to 131 and 141; the hardness reading of boosting from p. 141.
- Labels switch to \(\{\pm 1\}\) for this lesson, as the book does in Chapter 10; earlier lessons used \(\{0, 1\}\).
