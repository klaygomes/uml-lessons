Overview

Objective: after this, you can define the margin of a separating hyperplane and compute it (distance to a hyperplane), state hard-SVM in its max-min and quadratic-program forms and prove they agree, explain why margins are only meaningful relative to the radius and state the dimension-free sample complexity of hard-SVM, derive soft-SVM as hinge-loss regularized loss minimization and prove its norm-based, dimension-free guarantee (including the choice of λ\lambdaλ), compare margin and VC bounds, explain the ramp loss and why SVMs use the hinge, prove that the hard-SVM solution lies in the span of the support vectors, derive the dual, and implement soft-SVM with SGD in O(Td)O(Td)O(Td) time.

Misconception: “every consistent halfspace is as good as any other”. Why wrong: they differ in margin, hence in robustness to perturbation and in the dimension-free guarantee; Perceptron 0.243 vs hard-SVM 1.342 on the lesson’s eight messages.

Second misconception: “scale the data to get a bigger margin”. Why wrong: margin and radius scale together; only ρ/γ\rho/\gammaρ/γ (or ρB\rho BρB) matters (Definition 15.3 and Remark 15.2).

Third misconception: “the margin bound always beats the VC bound”. Why wrong: it wins when ρ2B2≪d\rho^2B^2 \ll dρ2B2≪d; the large-margin preference is an inductive bias that can raise approximation error, and the hinge approximation term can exceed the 0-1 one (p. 209).

Fourth: “every message on the margin has a nonzero coefficient”. Why wrong: in S10 at λ=0.1\lambda = 0.1λ=0.1, A has signed score exactly 1 and dual coefficient 0.
