# Clustering

Objective: after this, you can explain why clustering is ill-defined (non-transitive similarity, no ground truth); define a distance function, a clustering (partition), soft clusterings and dendrograms; run single, average and max linkage with the three stopping rules and prove single linkage is Kruskal's algorithm; define the k-means, k-medoids, k-median, center-based and SOD costs; prove the mean minimizes squared spread and that each k-means iteration does not increase the cost; explain cut, RatioCut, the unnormalized Laplacian, prove \(v^\top L v = \frac12\sum W_{r,s}(v_r - v_s)^2\) and RatioCut \(= \operatorname{trace}(H^\top L H)\), and state the spectral relaxation; compute mutual information and the information-bottleneck objective; state scale invariance, richness and consistency and prove that no clustering function has all three.

Terms:
- distance function: \(d: \mathcal X \times \mathcal X \to \mathbb R_+\), symmetric, \(d(x,x) = 0\); often the triangle inequality.
- clustering: a partition \((C_1, \dots, C_k)\) of \(\mathcal X\).
- dendrogram: tree of subsets, singletons at the leaves, \(\mathcal X\) at the root.
- k-means cost: \(\sum_i \sum_{x \in C_i} \|x - \mu(C_i)\|^2\), \(\mu(C_i)\) the mean.
- RatioCut: \(\sum_i \frac{1}{|C_i|}\sum_{r \in C_i, s \notin C_i} W_{r,s}\).
- unnormalized Laplacian: \(L = D - W\), \(D\) diagonal with row sums of \(W\).
- mutual information \(I(a;b) = \sum p(\alpha,\beta)\log_2\frac{p(\alpha,\beta)}{p(\alpha)p(\beta)}\); information bottleneck \(\min_{p(C|x)} I(x;C) - \beta I(C;y)\).
- scale invariance, richness, consistency: as in the prose (Kleinberg's three properties).

Misconception: "k-means finds the best clustering." Why wrong: it reaches a fixed point; on the twelve messages, start (0,0),(0,2) stops at 35 against the optimum 20; 1,000 random starts end 692 at 20, 208 at 23, 100 at 35.
Second misconception: "looks right on the plot means the method is right." Why wrong: average linkage drew the four kinds (purity > 0.95) on 25 of 30 lab batches but optimizes no stated cost; nothing checkable.
Third misconception: "single linkage is the natural choice." Why wrong: chaining; on the lab mail with \(k = 4\), mean purity 0.595 (min 0.28), sizes like [1, 1, 25, 77] (seed 0) and [1, 2, 2, 99] (seed 2).
Fourth misconception: "there is a correct clustering." Why wrong: no ground truth even with full knowledge of \(\mathcal D\); the impossibility theorem.

Derivation (all proofs in the prose):
- Single linkage = Kruskal (p. 311 says "it is also possible to show"): induction on rounds, ours; Kruskal's correctness cited as a standard algorithms fact, not proved.
- Mean minimizes squared spread: the book says "note that" (p. 314); the decomposition proof is ours.
- Lemma 22.1 (each iteration does not increase the cost): the book's two inequalities (22.4) and (22.5), with the per-message argument and the empty-cluster remark spelled out (ours).
- Termination with ties kept in the current cluster, and the "stop if \(G\) fails to drop strictly" safeguard: ours. The book says only that there is no guarantee on the number of iterations (p. 314).
- Quadratic-form identity and Lemma 22.3: the book's, with the "standard algebraic manipulations" written out.
- "Separate pieces are free" proposition (zero RatioCut, columns are 0-eigenvectors): ours, formalizing the book's intuition on p. 317. Nonnegative eigenvalues: ours, one line.
- Relaxation solved by the \(k\) smallest eigenvectors: stated, proof deferred to lesson 23a as the book does (Theorem 23.2).
- Theorem 22.4: the book's proof, with the positivity requirement made explicit and \(\alpha = \max d_1/d_2\) given explicitly.

Algorithms:
- Linkage (pp. 310 to 311): pseudocode ours; \(O(m^3)\) time, \(O(m^2)\) space with the cluster-distance table and Lance-Williams-style updates (min, max, size-weighted average); \(O(m^2 n)\) for distances; single linkage \(O(m^2)\) via Prim. Build-it asserts pass with `sol_linkage.py` (also prints max heights [2, 2, 7, 13] and "mst True" over 200 random 9-point sets).
- k-means (p. 313): \(O(mkn)\) per iteration, \(O(mn + kn)\) space, \(O(RTmkn)\) total. Build-it asserts pass with `sol_kmeans.py` (smaller-index ties, keep empty centre, stop when unchanged or \(G\) not strictly lower); its internal assertions of both lemma inequalities never fired on 1,000 random 15-point sets with \(k = 3\).
- Unnormalized spectral clustering (p. 317): \(O(m^2 n)\) to build \(W\), \(O(m^3)\) eigendecomposition, \(O(mk^2)\) per k-means iteration, \(O(m^2)\) space. No separate build-it (its k-means step reuses the k-means build-it; the power-iteration snippet computes the Fiedler vector).
- Information bottleneck: no algorithm in the book (hard; EM-like methods, lesson 24b). None built.

End-of-chapter problems (Section 22.8, PDF pp. 320 to 322). In the prose: Problem 1 = Exercise 2, Problem 2 = Exercise 3, Problem 3 = Exercise 5. Hints one rung at a time; never the solution.
- Exercise 2 (k-means need not stop at a local minimum).
  1. Run it: 1 and 2 go to centre 2; 3 is at distance 1 from both centres, a tie, so it goes to index 1; 4 goes to centre 4. New centres: mean of {1, 2, 3} = 2, and 4. Nothing changes: a fixed point, cost \(1 + 0 + 1 + 0 = 2\).
  2. View the cost as \(f(\mu_1, \mu_2) = \sum_x \min_j (x - \mu_j)^2\). What happens to point 3's term if \(\mu_2\) moves slightly toward it?
  3. Take \(\mu_1 = 2\), \(\mu_2 = 4 - \delta\) for small \(\delta > 0\): 3 now goes to \(\mu_2\), and \(f = 1 + 0 + (1 - \delta)^2 + \delta^2 = 2 - 2\delta + 2\delta^2 < 2\) for \(0 < \delta < 1\). So arbitrarily close points have lower cost. (The clustering {1, 2}, {3, 4} costs 1.)
- Exercise 3 (farthest-first is a 2-approximation for k-diameter).
  1. Let \(\mu_{k+1}\) be the next centre you would pick and \(r = \min_{j \le k} d(\mu_j, \mu_{k+1})\). Why is every point within \(r\) of its nearest centre?
  2. Upper bound: two points in one cluster \(\hat C_j\) are each within \(r\) of \(\mu_j\); triangle inequality gives diameter \(\le 2r\).
  3. Lower bound: the \(k + 1\) points \(\mu_1, \dots, \mu_{k+1}\) are pairwise at least \(r\) apart (each was farthest when chosen, and distances to chosen centres only shrink). Pigeonhole: any \(k\)-clustering puts two of them together, so its k-diameter is at least \(r\). Note: the book's statement writes \(\max_{j \in [d]}\); read \(j \in [k]\). If \(|\mathcal X| \le k\) the optimum is 0 and farthest-first also gets 0 (each point its own centre); handle as an edge case.
- Exercise 5 (single linkage and the three properties).
  1. Fixed \(k\): scaling preserves the order of distances, so the same merges (SI holds). Never outputs a partition with \(\ne k\) parts, so richness fails (for \(|\mathcal X| > k\) or any \(\mathcal X\) with at least two partition sizes). Consistency holds: with all distances distinct, the clusters are the components after the \(m - k\) lightest Kruskal edges; every between-cluster distance exceeds the last accepted weight \(\lambda\); after a consistent change, within-cluster forest edges stay \(\le \lambda\) and between edges stay \(> \lambda\), so Kruskal's first \(m - k\) acceptances are within clusters. (Kleinberg 2002; ties need a fixed tie rule.)
  2. Fixed bound \(r\): output = connected components of the graph with edges \(d \le r\). Consistency holds (within edges stay \(\le r\), between stay \(> r\)); richness holds (within \(r/2\), between \(2r\)); SI fails (two points at distance \(r\): one cluster; scale by 2: two).
  3. Pairs: fixed \(k\): SI + Co; fixed \(r\): Ri + Co; scaled bound \(\alpha \max d\): SI + Ri (richness needs \(|\mathcal X| \ge 3\) for the one-cluster partition, or a non-metric \(d\); with \(|\mathcal X| = 2\) the scaled rule always gives two singletons), Co fails (worked example 5 in the prose is a counterexample, which gives this part away; accept it as evidence and ask for the SI and Ri proofs).
  Note: the lab's property panel shows these empirically; it hints, it does not prove.
