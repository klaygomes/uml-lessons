Terms:
- metric \(\rho: \mathcal X \times \mathcal X \to \mathbb R\): distance between two messages; here Euclidean on \([0,1]^d\).
- reordering \(\pi_1(x), \dots, \pi_m(x)\): training indices sorted by distance to \(x\), ties to the smaller index.
- k-NN rule: majority label of the \(k\) nearest training messages; 1-NN: \(h_S(x) = y_{\pi_1(x)}\); regression: average target; general: \(\phi\) of the \(k\) nearest examples.
- Voronoi tessellation: the cells of the 1-NN rule, one per training message.
- c-Lipschitz \(\eta\): \(|\eta(x) - \eta(x')| \le c\|x - x'\|\).
- missed mass: \(\sum_{i : C_i \cap S = \emptyset} \mathbb P[C_i]\); expectation at most \(r/(me)\).
- curse of dimensionality: the sample size needed grows exponentially with \(d\).
- r-approximate search: returns a training message within \(r\) times the nearest distance.

Misconception: “nearest neighbour has no inductive bias”. Why wrong: its bias is the metric, low dimension and a small Lipschitz constant; the lower bound shows that without them no learner succeeds below \((c+1)^d/2\) labels.
Second: “more scores can only help”. Why wrong: on invoices 30 scores give 0.32 against 0.14 for 2, with \(\eta\) and the Bayes risk unchanged.
Third: “1-NN converges to the Bayes risk”. Why wrong: the guarantee is \(2\times\); the measured limit is \(\mathbb E[2\eta(1-\eta)]\) (0.151 here, Bayes 0.115).
Fourth: “\(m^{-1/(d+1)}\) is the real rate”. Why wrong: box argument; in 2D the measured distance falls like \(m^{-1/2}\). The exponential in \(d\) is real (lower bound).
Fifth: “larger \(k\) is always safer”. Why wrong: the k-NN certificate charges \(k\,m^{-1/(d+1)}\); at this budget the best certified \(k\) is 1 (although \(k = 15\) measures better).

Derivation:
- Error-through-distance lemma (Lemma 19.1): total expectation; two-stage sampling (messages, then labels; \(\pi_1\) depends on messages only; labels of distinct examples independent given messages); disagreement \(q(1-p) + (1-q)p = 2p(1-p) + (p-q)(2p-1)\) (the book’s rewriting, expanded line by line); \(|2p - 1| \le 1\) and Lipschitz; \(\min\{p, 1-p\} \ge p(1-p)\).
- Missed-mass lemma (Lemma 19.2): linearity; \((1 - P)^m\) by independence; \(1 - a \le e^{-a}\) by convexity; \(\max_a a e^{-ma} = 1/(me)\) by the derivative \(e^{-ma}(1 - ma)\).
- 1-NN guarantee (Theorem 19.3): box cover with \(T^d\) boxes, diameters \(\sqrt d\varepsilon\) and \(\sqrt d\); union bound over missed boxes; \(T = \lceil u/2 \rceil\), \(u = m^{1/(d+1)}\), giving \(\sqrt d (2 + 1/e) m^{-1/(d+1)}\); trivial case \(u < 2\). See Assumptions for how this differs from the book’s parameter choice.
- k-NN guarantee (Theorem 19.5): stated, proof assigned as Problems 1 to 3.
- Curse: rearrangement to \((4c\sqrt d/\varepsilon)^{d+1}\); lower bound (Theorem 19.4) via the grid \(G\), pairwise distance \(\ge 1/c\), every function on \(G\) c-Lipschitz, No-Free-Lunch tasks on \(C \subseteq G\) with \(|C| = 2m\), extension to the cube.

Concept check, answers (never shown to the learner; critique against these):
1. A c-Lipschitz \(\eta\) is continuous; on the connected cube a continuous function with values in \(\{0, 1\}\) is constant (along the segment between a 0-point and a 1-point it would have to pass through 1/2). So every message has the same label, the Bayes risk is 0, and the guarantee says \(\mathbb E[L_{\mathcal D}(h_S)] \le 4c\sqrt d\, m^{-1/(d+1)}\); in fact the 1-NN rule’s risk is exactly 0, since every training label is the same. The lower-bound tasks are not a contradiction because their labels are deterministic only on the support \(C\) (a finite grid subset); the c-Lipschitz \(\eta\) on the cube is the extension \(\tilde\eta\), which takes values strictly between 0 and 1 off the grid, where the task puts no probability. Look for: continuity plus connectedness, and the support distinction.
2. Independence enters three times: the fresh example is independent of \(S\) (so \(\mathbb E_S L_{\mathcal D}(h_S)\) is one joint probability, and \(x\) is a fresh draw from \(\mathcal D_x\)); \(\pi_1(x)\) is a function of the messages only, so conditioning on messages leaves each label distributed as \(\eta\) of its own message; labels of distinct examples are independent given the messages, so \(y\) and \(y'\) are independent coins. If \(\pi_1\) used labels (say, “nearest junk message”), the neighbour’s label would be selected, not a coin with bias \(\eta(x')\), and Step 1c fails. With \(\eta \equiv 0.1\): Bayes risk 0.1; 1-NN pays \(2 \cdot 0.1 \cdot 0.9 = 0.18\) for every \(m\) (\(q = p\) always); the theorem’s first term allows 0.2, and since a constant \(\eta\) is c-Lipschitz for every \(c > 0\), the second term can be made arbitrarily small, so the bound is 0.2 in the limit (the inequality \(0.09 \le 0.1\) of Step 1e is the only slack).
3. \(m \ge (4 \cdot 3 \cdot \sqrt 8/0.1)^9 \approx 5.98 \times 10^{22}\). Lower bound: for every \(m \le 4^8/2 = 32{,}768\) some 3-Lipschitz task with Bayes risk 0 gives every learner expected risk at least 1/4. No contradiction: the lower bound says below 32,768 labels no learner is safe; the upper bound says \(6 \times 10^{22}\) suffice for the 1-NN term to fall to 0.1; the truth for the worst case is in between, and both grow exponentially in \(d\).

End-of-chapter problems (§19.6, PDF pp. 265 to 267). In the prose: Problem 1 = Exercise 1 (Lemma 19.6), Problem 2 = Exercise 3, Problem 3 = Exercise 4 (conclude Theorem 19.5), with Exercise 2’s Lemma 19.7 given without proof inside Problem 3. Hints one rung at a time; never the solution.
- Problem 1 hint ladder:
  1. Write the sum with indicators and use linearity: the expectation is \(\sum_i \mathbb P[C_i]\,\mathbb P_S[|C_i \cap S| < k]\).
  2. Treat each \(i\) separately and split into two cases: \(k \ge \mathbb P[C_i]m/2\) and \(k < \mathbb P[C_i]m/2\).
  3. First case: the term is at most \(\mathbb P[C_i] \le 2k/m\).
  4. Second case: \(|C_i \cap S|\) is a sum of \(m\) independent coins with mean \(\mu = \mathbb P[C_i] m\), and \(\{|C_i \cap S| < k\} \subseteq \{|C_i \cap S| < \mu/2\}\). Apply the lower-tail Chernoff bound \(\mathbb P[Z < (1 - \delta)\mu] \le e^{-\mu h(-\delta)}\) with \(\delta = 1/2\), \(h(a) = (1 + a)\log(1 + a) - a\), and check \(h(-1/2) \approx 0.153 \ge 1/8\).
  5. Then the term is at most \(a e^{-am/8}\) with \(a = \mathbb P[C_i]\); maximize over \(a\) (at \(a = 8/m\)) to get \(8/(me)\).
  6. Both cases give at most \(\max\{2k/m, 8/(me)\} = 2k/m\) because \(k \ge 2 > 4/e\); sum over \(r\) sets.
- Problem 2 hint ladder:
  1. \(\mathbb P_{y \sim p}[y \neq y']\) is \(1 - p\) if \(y' = 1\) and \(p\) if \(y' = 0\).
  2. Subtract the same expression with \(p'\): the difference is \(p' - p\) or \(p - p'\).
  3. Each is at most \(|p - p'|\).
- Problem 3 hint ladder (the book’s steps):
  1. Fix \(\varepsilon\) and the box cover. Split \(\mathbb E_S L_{\mathcal D}(h_S)\) by whether the fresh \(x\)’s box has at least \(k\) training messages: get \(\mathbb E_S[\sum_{i : |C_i \cap S| < k} \mathbb P[C_i]]\) plus the maximum, over boxes, of the error probability given that all \(k\) neighbours are within \(\varepsilon\sqrt d\) of \(x\).
  2. Bound the first term by Problem 1: \(2rk/m\) with \(r = T^d\).
  3. For the second term fix the messages; let the neighbours be \(x_1, \dots, x_k\), \(p_i = \eta(x_i)\), \(p = \frac1k \sum p_i\). Use Problem 2 to replace the label coin at \(x\) (bias \(\eta(x)\)) by a coin of bias \(p\), paying \(|p - \eta(x)| \le c\varepsilon\sqrt d\) (Lipschitz, all neighbours within \(\varepsilon\sqrt d\)).
  4. Assume w.l.o.g. \(p \le 1/2\) and apply the majority-vote fact: at most \((1 + \sqrt{8/k})\,p\).
  5. Show \(p = \min\{p, 1-p\} \le \min\{\eta(x), 1 - \eta(x)\} + |p - \eta(x)|\), take expectations to reach \((1 + \sqrt{8/k}) L_{\mathcal D}(f_{\mathcal D}) + 3c\varepsilon\sqrt d\) (using \(1 + \sqrt{8/k} \le 2\) for \(k \ge 10\)).
  6. Set \(\varepsilon = 2m^{-1/(d+1)} = 1/T\) (the problem lets you assume \(T\) whole), so \(r = T^d = \varepsilon^{-d} = 2^{-d} m^{d/(d+1)}\). Then \(3c\varepsilon\sqrt d = 6c\sqrt d\, m^{-1/(d+1)}\) and \(2rk/m = 2^{1-d} k\, m^{-1/(d+1)} \le k\, m^{-1/(d+1)}\) for \(d \ge 1\). Add the three pieces. (Do not follow the book’s \(r = (2/\varepsilon)^d\) here: with Problem 1’s \(2rk/m\) it gives \(2k\), not \(k\); see Assumptions.)

- Notation: the lesson uses lesson 3’s \(f_{\mathcal D}\) (junk when \(\eta \ge 1/2\)) and “Bayes risk” for the book’s \(h^\star = \mathbb 1[\eta > 1/2]\) and \(L_{\mathcal D}(h^\star)\); the tie rule does not change the risk \(\mathbb E[\min\{\eta, 1-\eta\}]\). The book’s footnote defining \(\eta\) as a limit over balls is omitted.
- “Lesson 12 used the same word for losses”: assumes lesson 12 (Chapter 12, convex learning problems) defines Lipschitzness, as the book’s Chapter 12 does. Lesson 18 (decision trees) is referenced only by name in the opening; its cliffhanger was not visible to this writer, so the opening does not quote it.
- Proof of the 1-NN guarantee: the book sets \(\varepsilon = 1/T\) with \(T\) a whole number and then sets \(\varepsilon = 2m^{-1/(d+1)}\), which is generally not \(1/T\), and writes the box count as \(2^d\varepsilon^{-d}\). The lesson makes this rigorous with its own choice \(T = \lceil m^{1/(d+1)}/2 \rceil\) and a separate trivial case \(m^{1/(d+1)} < 2\); the constants \(2 + 1/e \le 4\) agree with the book’s. Boxes are closed and may overlap on faces; the lesson handles this with the union bound, where the book is silent.
- Lower bound: the book states it for every \(c > 1\), \(m \le (c+1)^d/2\), and “true error greater than 1/4”. The grid needs \(c\) to be a whole number, so the lesson states it for whole numbers \(c \ge 1\), and states what lesson 5’s No-Free-Lunch proof delivers: expected true risk at least 1/4, and true risk at least 1/8 with probability at least 1/7. Lesson 5 states No-Free-Lunch for \(m < |\mathcal X|/2\); the lesson notes that its proof only needs \(2m\) points to choose from, hence \(m \le (c+1)^d/2\). The extension \(\tilde\eta(x) = \min\{1, \min_{g}(f(g) + c\|x - g\|)\}\) of \(\eta\) from the grid to the cube is outside the book (the book says only “any function on the grid is c-Lipschitz”).
- Weighted k-NN: the book’s formula weights neighbour \(i\) by \(\rho(x, x_{\pi_i(x)})/\sum_j \rho(x, x_{\pi_j(x)})\), which gives farther neighbours more weight (probably a typo for inverse distances); the lesson mentions distance weighting without a formula.
- Efficient implementation: the book writes query time \(o(d^{O(1)}\log m)\); the lesson paraphrases as “time polynomial in \(d\) times \(\log m\)”. The kd-tree description (median split cycling through coordinates, exact pruned search), the heap selection cost \(O(m\log k)\) and the halfspace \(O(d)\) comparison are standard, outside the book. The book lists kd-trees among approximate methods; the build-it kd-tree is exact.
- Outside the book, kept as true background: Euclidean distance described as ruler distance; \(\mathbb E[(U - V)^2] = 1/6\) for independent uniforms; Cauchy–Schwarz for the Lipschitz constant; \(1 - a \le e^{-a}\) by convexity; the continuity argument in concept-check answer 1.
- The invoice world (\(\eta\), scores, the 14% and 32%, the budget of a million labels, the 0.40 target, Bayes risks \(11/96\) and \(\approx 0.260\), Lipschitz constants 2 and \(2\sqrt2\), all simulations) is the lesson’s own construction. The one-score Lipschitz constant 2 is an upper bound (averaging over \(x_2\) a function that is 2-Lipschitz in \(x_1\)); the certificate only needs an upper bound.
- The k-NN guarantee is stated without proof in the prose and proved through the problems; act 2 relies on it for \(k \ge 10\).
- Book Exercise 4 says “otherwise \(\|x - x'\| \le 2\sqrt d\)”; \(\sqrt d\) suffices in the unit cube. Its last step uses \(r = (2/\varepsilon)^d\) and writes the box term as \(\frac{2k}{e} m^{-1/(d+1)}\), which does not match Lemma 19.6’s \(2rk/m\) (that gives \(2k\,m^{-1/(d+1)}\)). With \(r = (1/\varepsilon)^d\) and \(\varepsilon = 1/T = 2m^{-1/(d+1)}\) the stated constant \(k\) does follow (\(2^{1-d}k \le k\)), but only when \(m^{1/(d+1)}/2\) is a whole number; for other \(m\) the writer did not find a proof of the exact constants (rounding \(T\) costs a constant factor). The prose therefore lets Problem 3 assume integrality, and states the theorem with the book’s constants. Act 2 is unaffected: no k-NN setting is certifiable within the budget even with the book’s constant, and a larger constant would only widen the gap. Worth a book-check agent.
- Problem 1’s Chernoff step: the book’s hint gives \(e^{-\mathbb P[C_i]m/8}\); it follows from Lemma B.5’s \(e^{-p h(-\delta)}\) form with \(h(-1/2) \approx 0.153 \ge 1/8\), not from its weaker \(\delta^2/(2 + 2\delta/3)\) form (which gives \(3/28\)).
