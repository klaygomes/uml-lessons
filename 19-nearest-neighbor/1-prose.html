Mail that matches no rule

Every filter you have built so far began the same way: before seeing a single label, you fixed a hypothesis class, and the learner searched it. Halfspaces in lesson 9a, boosted votes of stumps in lesson 10a, decision trees in lesson 18. Each class was a bet about what junk looks like, placed in advance.

This month the bets stopped paying. New kinds of mail keep arriving that match no rule anyone wrote and no hypothesis anyone trained: parcel-delivery notices that are half genuine and half phishing, and supplier invoices that are half real and half fraud. The filters shrug at them. So a colleague suggested the oldest trick there is. When a new message arrives, find the labelled messages that look most like it, and give it their label. No class, no search, no training. Just “this one looks like those, and those were junk.”

By now every message in the mail room is described by thirty numbers, which the team calls scores, each scaled to lie between 0 and 1. Two of them matter for this story. The payment score says how much a message talks about paying (0 for a lunch invitation, near 1 for “settle the attached invoice today”), and the sender score says how unfamiliar the sender is (0 for your manager, near 1 for an address nobody has ever written to). The other twenty-eight scores cover everything else the team ever measured: link share, word counts, the hour it was sent, and so on.

For parcel notices, the team compared messages on the two scores the parcel dashboard happens to show, payment and sender. For invoices, a different engineer compared them on all thirty scores, on the reasonable-sounding principle that more information can’t hurt. A month later the numbers came in. On parcel notices, the copy-your-neighbours filter was wrong on 14% of messages. On invoices it was wrong on 32%, more than twice as often, and closer to a coin than anyone was comfortable with.

Here is the part that should bother you. When we dig into the data later in this lesson, it will turn out that both kinds of mail follow exactly the same pattern of junk and keep. The only difference is how many scores the comparison used. So why would more information make the filter worse? And how many labelled messages would you need before “label it like its neighbours” comes with a guarantee?

This lesson answers both. The method is called nearest neighbour, and it is the first learner in this course that does not search a hypothesis class at all. That makes it a strange and interesting test of everything lesson 1 told you about bias.

Similar messages, similar labels

To say “the most similar message” we need a way to measure how far apart two messages are. Take a new invoice with payment score 0.70 and sender score 0.70, and a labelled one with 0.65 and 0.65 (you will meet both again in a moment). Draw them as two dots on a square whose sides are the two scores: they are 0.05 apart along each side, and the ruler between them reads about 0.071.

That ruler, made general, is a metric. Recall that the domain X\mathcal XX is the set of all messages that could ever arrive, and R\mathbb RR is the set of real numbers. A metric on X\mathcal XX is a function ρ:X×X→R\rho: \mathcal X \times \mathcal X \to \mathbb Rρ:X×X→R (the Greek letter rho) that takes a pair of messages and returns the distance between them; X×X\mathcal X \times \mathcal XX×X is the set of all such pairs. When each message is a list of ddd scores, a point of Rd\mathbb R^dRd, the natural choice is the Euclidean distance,

ρ(x,x′)=∥x−x′∥=∑i=1d(xi−xi′)2.\rho(x, x') = \|x - x'\| = \sqrt{\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^d (x_i - x'_i)^2}.ρ(x,x′)=∥x−x′∥=∑i=1d​(xi​−xi′​)2​.

Read it from the inside out.

  • xix_ixi​ is score number iii of message xxx, and xi′x'_ixi′​ (read “x prime i”) is the same score of the other message x′x'x′.
  • xi−xi′x_i - x'_ixi​−xi′​ is the gap on that one score, and squaring it turns every gap into a size, whatever its sign.

No class to search, but still a bias

Pause on what just happened, because it is new. Every learner since lesson 2 chose a hypothesis from a class H\mathcal HH fixed in advance: ERM picked the one with the fewest training mistakes, structural risk minimization traded those mistakes against the size of the class, and boosting combined stumps from a fixed family. The k-NN rule picks a label for any new message without searching any predefined class of functions. It stores the training sequence and, at prediction time, looks things up.

That should ring an alarm. Lesson 1’s memorizer also stored the training sequence and looked things up, and it learned nothing. What is different? The memorizer answered only for messages it had seen exactly, and fell back on a blind “keep” for everything else. The nearest neighbour rule answers for unseen messages by borrowing from seen ones that are close. That borrowing is generalization, and it rests on an assumption: that the scores describing a message are relevant to its label in such a way that nearby messages are likely to have the same label.

So the bias has not disappeared. It has moved out of a hypothesis class and into the distance. If the scores you compare on are the wrong ones, or if junk changes abruptly between two messages that look nearly identical, nearest neighbour is the pigeon of lesson 1 all over again. The rest of this lesson makes “nearby messages have similar labels” precise, proves what it buys, and shows what it costs.

Where it works and where it fails

Let’s reproduce the bug. Here is the secret behind both kinds of mail, the one I promised in the opening. A message is a point x∈[0,1]30x \in [0, 1]^{30}x∈[0,1]30, thirty scores, with every score drawn independently and uniformly between 0 and 1. Labels that change slowly

To prove anything, we have to say what “nearby messages have similar labels” means. Recall from lesson 3 that the labels need not be a function of the message: users disagree. Examples come from a distribution D\mathcal DD over X×Y\mathcal X \times \mathcal YX×Y, with Y={0,1}\mathcal Y = \{0, 1\}Y={0,1}: a rule saying how likely each (message, label) pair is. Now the assumption. In the invoice world, nudge the message at (0.6, 0.5) to (0.62, 0.52). The distance moved is 0.022+0.022≈0.028\sqrt{0.02^2 + 0.02^2} \approx 0.0280.022+0.022​≈0.028, and η\etaη moves from 0.70 to 0.78, a change of 0.08: about 2.83 times the distance. Close messages, close probabilities of junk, with a fixed exchange rate between the two. That exchange rate is the constant in the definition. A function η:X→[0,1]\eta: \mathcal X \to [0, 1]η:X→[0,1] is c-Lipschitz, for a constant c>0c > 0c>0, if for all x,x′∈Xx, x' \in \mathcal Xx,x′∈X,

The single bars ∣⋅∣|\cdot|∣⋅∣ are the absolute value, the size of a change ignoring its sign; the double bars are the distance from before. In plain words: if two messages are close, their probabilities of junk are close: moving a distance rrr through the domain changes η\etaη by at most crcrcr. A small ccc means junk changes gently from message to message; a large ccc means a tiny change in the scores can flip the odds. In one dimension, the nearest of a thousand messages is a thousand times closer than a typical one. And notice the subtler point. In the invoice world η\etaη depends on two scores and its Lipschitz constant is 222\sqrt 222​ in thirty dimensions just as in two. Nothing about the labels got harder. What got harder is the space the neighbours live in. The nearest neighbour rule searches no hypothesis class, yet it carries prior knowledge all the same: its success depends on the assumption that the dimension and the Lipschitz constant of η\etaη are not too high. Choosing which scores to compare on is choosing that dimension. This is why, in practice, nearest neighbour usually runs after a step that reduces the number of scores; lesson 23a is about how to do that well.

Nearest neighbours as code

Here is the k-NN rule as pseudocode. The training sequence holds mmm messages of ddd scores each.

KNN-Train(S):
  store S

KNN-Predict(S, x, k):
  for i = 1..m:
    dist_i ← ρ(x, x_i)
  N ← the indices of the k smallest dist_i
      (ties broken by smaller index)
  return the majority label among {y_i : i ∈ N}

For regression, replace the last line by the average of {yi:i∈N}\{y_i : i \in N\}{yi​:i∈N}, or by any ϕ\phiϕ of the kkk neighbours.

Cost. Recall from lesson 8 that O(⋅)O(\cdot)O(⋅) means “at most a constant times”; its two-sided cousin Θ(⋅)\Theta(\cdot)Θ(⋅) means “a constant times, up to and down to”. Training is nothing more than storing the training sequence: O(md)O(md)O(md) space, and no time beyond copying. Nearest neighbour is a learning-by-memorization rule, and the whole training sequence must be kept. Prediction is where the work is: computing all mmm distances costs Θ(md)\Theta(md)Θ(md), selecting the kkk smallest with a heap of size kkk costs O(mlog⁡k)O(m \log k)O(mlogk), and the vote costs O(k)O(k)O(k), so one prediction is O(md+mlog⁡k)O(md + m\log k)O(md+mlogk) time and O(k)O(k)O(k) extra space. For the 1-NN rule it is Θ(md)\Theta(md)Θ(md). Compare that with a halfspace from lesson 9a, which predicts in O(d)O(d)O(d) no matter how many examples trained it. Nearest neighbour moves all the cost from training to prediction, and every single prediction pays it.

Faster search. When ddd is small, data structures from computational geometry can answer a nearest-neighbour query in time polynomial in ddd times log⁡m\log mlogm, instead of scanning. The catch is space: roughly mO(d)m^{O(d)}mO(d), which is impractical once ddd is more than a handful. The way out is to ask for less. An r-approximate search is a procedure guaranteed to return a training message within distance at most rrr times the distance to the true nearest neighbour. Three popular methods are kd-trees, ball trees and locality-sensitive hashing. A kd-tree, the one you will build below, splits the training messages in half by one score at a time, cycling through the scores, and at query time skips any half that cannot contain anything closer than the best message found so far. In low dimension the skipping saves most of the work. In high dimension, by the curse you just met, almost nothing can be skipped, because almost everything is about equally far.

What people get wrong

“Nearest neighbour has no inductive bias, because there is no hypothesis class.” The bias moved into the distance. The rule works when nearby messages have similar labels, in a space whose dimension is small enough for neighbours to be near. The curse-is-real theorem shows that without such assumptions no rule, nearest neighbour included, can learn from fewer than exponentially many labels.

“More scores can only help.” On invoices, adding twenty-eight irrelevant scores more than doubled the error, from 0.14 to 0.32, while leaving η\etaη and the Bayes risk untouched. Irrelevant scores add distance without adding information, and the training sequence needed grows exponentially with every score you keep.

“The 1-NN rule converges to the Bayes risk.” The guarantee is at most twice the Bayes risk as mmm grows. Getting all the way down needs a vote of many neighbours, which is what the k-NN guarantee’s multiplier 1+8/k1 + \sqrt{8/k}1+8/k​ describes.

“The bound m−1/(d+1)m^{-1/(d+1)}m−1/(d+1) is how fast the error really falls.” It is an upper bound from a box argument, and in the simulations the real distances fell faster. What is not an artefact is the exponential dependence on ddd: the lower bound proves it for every learner.

“A bigger kkk is always safer.” Larger kkk shrinks the multiplier on the Bayes risk but reaches further for its neighbours, and the k-NN guarantee charges k m−1/(d+1)k\, m^{-1/(d+1)}km−1/(d+1) for that. With a fixed budget there is a best kkk, and it can be 1.
