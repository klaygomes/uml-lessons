The number that went wrong

Lesson 10a gave the mail room a boosted filter. Each round of boosting adds one decision stump, a tiny hypothesis that looks at a single feature, and the filter after TTT rounds is a weighted vote of TTT stumps. Lesson 10b showed that TTT is a dial on the bias-complexity tradeoff: too few rounds and the vote is too crude to describe junk, too many and it starts to describe the accidents of the training sequence. The obvious question is the one it left hanging. How do you set TTT?

Your team answered it the way most teams would. Users had labelled 300 messages. The team trained on 100 of them and put the other 200 aside as a test set, examples the learner never sees during training. They ran boosting for 60 rounds, which gives sixty filters at once, one after each round, and measured every one of them on the 200 test messages. The filter after 11 rounds made the fewest mistakes: 33 out of 200, a test error of 16.5%. The report said “16.5% error on held-out data”, everyone nodded, and the filter shipped.

A month later the complaints came in, and they added up to a filter that was wrong on about 21% of incoming mail. Not a disaster. But not 16.5% either, and the difference is far too large to wave away.

Here is the strange part. Nobody cheated. No test message was ever used to train a stump. The 200 messages were set aside honestly, at random, before training began. So where did four and a half points of error hide?

This lesson is about that question, and its name is model selection: choosing among learners, or among settings of one learner (such as the number of rounds), using the data itself. By the end you will know exactly what the team did wrong, how much their number could be trusted, how to split a labelling budget so that the reported number is honest, and what to do when the honest number turns out to be bad.

Was this test set unlucky? We can check, because the simulation can draw as many test sets as we like. Keep the same training sequence and the same sixty filters, draw 2,000 fresh test sets of 200 messages, and let the team’s procedure pick a winner on each. On average the reported test error is 0.188 while the true risk of the winner is 0.210, and the true risk is above the reported number on 80% of the draws. The gap is not bad luck. It is built into the procedure.

You may recognise the pattern from lesson 4, where the best-looking rule on a dashboard was the most flattered one: the winner’s curse. To see how strong it can be, strip the problem to its bones. Suppose sixty candidates all have true risk exactly 0.2, and their mistakes on the 200 test messages are independent. Each test error is then a binomial count divided by 200 (a binomial count is the number of hits in 200 independent tries, each a hit with the same chance, here 0.2), and the chance that the smallest of sixty counts is at least kkk is the chance that one count is at least kkk, raised to the sixtieth power. So the team’s number was optimistic because the test set did two jobs at once: it chose the filter, and it graded the filter it chose. There are two ways forward. One is to stop measuring and bound the true risk with a theorem, choosing TTT to make the bound small. The other is to keep measuring, but on examples that played no part in any choice. We’ll take them in that order.

Choosing by a bound: structural risk minimization

Lesson 7a met a learner that faces a whole sequence of hypothesis classes, H1,H2,H3,…\mathcal H_1, \mathcal H_2, \mathcal H_3, \dotsH1​,H2​,H3​,…, and does not want to commit to one of them in advance. To compare classes we need to know how much each one can be trusted on mmm examples, where mmm is the length of the training sequence. Recall from lesson 4 the idea of uniform convergence: once the training sequence is long enough, every hypothesis in the class has an empirical risk close to its true risk, all of them at the same time, except on a rare unlucky draw. Measuring instead: the hold-out set

Here is the sharper tool, and it is almost embarrassingly simple. To learn the true risk of one hypothesis hhh, show it messages it has never had any influence on, and count. Had the team fixed h11h_{11}h11​ before looking at the test set, its 33 mistakes on 200 messages, 0.165, would have been exactly this kind of count.

The tool for the proof is Hoeffding’s inequality, which lesson 4 introduced. The idea: an average of many independent, bounded numbers rarely strays far from its expected value, and the chance of straying shrinks exponentially as you add more numbers. Take a filter of true risk 0.2 on 200 test messages. Its test error could in principle be anything from 0 to 1, but the chance that it lands more than 0.1 away from 0.2 is at most about 0.037. Validation for model selection

Choosing was not the mistake. Validation is a natural tool for choosing. Train several learners, or one learner with several settings, on SSS, and collect their outputs in a candidate set H={h1,…,hr}H = \{h_1, \dots, h_r\}H={h1​,…,hr​}. For the boosted filter, hTh_ThT​ is the vote after TTT rounds. Then draw a fresh validation set and pick the candidate with the smallest validation risk. In other words, run ERM over HHH, on VVV.

This is learning a finite class, the problem lesson 2 solved, with one twist: HHH is not fixed in advance, because it depends on SSS. But VVV is independent of SSS, and so independent of HHH, and that is enough for the finite-class argument to go through. The only mistake the team made was charging for one hypothesis when they had compared sixty. Charging properly means splitting the failure budget δ\deltaδ among the sixty, each getting δ/60\delta/60δ/60, and on 200 messages that widens the margin from 0.096 to 0.139.

k-fold cross validation

Validation assumes you can afford to set examples aside. When labels are scarce, holding out a fifth of them for validation means training on a fifth less. k-fold cross validation is designed to give a good estimate of the true risk without wasting so much data.

Partition the training sequence SSS of mmm examples into kkk parts, called folds, S1,…,SkS_1, \dots, S_kS1​,…,Sk​, each of size m/km/km/k (assume kkk divides mmm). For each fold iii, train on the other k−1k - 1k−1 folds, S∖SiS \setminus S_iS∖Si​, and measure the result on fold iii. The average of these kkk errors is the estimate of the true risk. The special case k=mk = mk=m, where each fold is a single example, is called leave-one-out.

For model selection, do this for every parameter value θ\thetaθ in a set Θ\ThetaΘ (capital theta; this θ\thetaθ is a setting such as the number of rounds, unrelated to Hoeffding’s θi\theta_iθi​), pick the value with the smallest average, and then retrain with that value on all of SSS. Write A(S′;θ)A(S'; \theta)A(S′;θ) for the learner run on S′S'S′ with parameter θ\thetaθ:

KFoldSelect(S, Θ, A, k):
  partition S into S_1, S_2, ..., S_k
  for each θ in Θ:
    for i = 1..k:
      h_{i,θ} ← A(S \ S_i; θ)
    error(θ) ← (1/k) · Σ_{i=1..k} L_{S_i}(h_{i,θ})
  θ* ← argmin_{θ in Θ} error(θ)
  return θ*, h_{θ*} = A(S; θ*)

Reading the notation: S∖SiS \setminus S_iS∖Si​ is “SSS without fold iii”, LSi(hi,θ)L_{S_i}(h_{i,\theta})LSi​​(hi,θ​) is the empirical risk of the fold-iii hypothesis on the fold it never saw, so error(θ) is the average of the kkk held-out errors, and argmin⁡θ∈Θ\operatorname{argmin}_{\theta \in \Theta}argminθ∈Θ​ returns the parameter value, not the error, at which error(θ) is smallest. With k=5k = 5k=5 and 100 examples, each fold has 20 messages and each training run uses 80. The snippet below mirrors it: rest is S∖SiS \setminus S_iS∖Si​, risk(A(rest), f) is LSi(hi,θ)L_{S_i}(h_{i,\theta})LSi​​(hi,θ​), and sum(errs) / k is error(θ).

Let’s watch it choose between two learners on 100 examples from a plain mail room, where label = feature 1 with 10% of labels flipped: ERM over the twenty one-feature hypotheses of lesson 1, and lesson 1’s memorizer.

    x = tuple(rng.randint(0, 1) for _ in range(20))
    S.append((x, x[0] ^ (rng.random() < 0.1)))
def risk(h, T):
    return sum(h(x) != y for x, y in T) / len(T)
def one_feature(T):
    j = min(range(20), key=lambda j: risk(lambda x: x[j], T))
    return lambda x: x[j]

    return lambda x, table=dict(T): table.get(x, 0)

k = 5

for A in (one_feature, memorize):
    errs = []
    for f in folds:
        rest = [z for g in folds if g is not f for z in g]
        errs.append(risk(A(rest), f))
    print(A.__name__, [round(e, 2) for e in errs],
          round(sum(errs) / k, 2))

Is there a guarantee like the hold-out theorem? Each single term LSi(hi,θ)L_{S_i}(h_{i,\theta})LSi​​(hi,θ​) is an honest hold-out estimate, because hi,θh_{i,\theta}hi,θ​ never saw fold iii. Cross validation often works very well in practice, yet in general it can fail, and the first problem at the end of this lesson builds a learner for which the leave-one-out estimate is wrong by exactly 1/21/21/2 every time. Exactly when it works is still not fully understood. It is known to give good estimates for local rules such as nearest neighbours (lesson 19), a result of Rogers and Wagner, and for stable learners, whose output barely moves when one example changes (lesson 13a).

Train, validation, test

Now the fix assembles itself. In most practical work you split the labelled examples into three parts:

  1. a training sequence SSS, used to train every candidate;
  2. a validation set VVV, used to choose among the candidates;

What to do if learning fails

Now suppose you did everything right: a hypothesis class, a learner, parameters tuned on a validation set, a number from an untouched test set. And the number is bad. What went wrong, and what should you do next?

There are many things you could change:

  • get a larger training sequence;
  • change the hypothesis class: enlarge it, reduce it, replace it entirely, or change the parameters you consider;
  • change the features that describe each message;
  • change the optimization algorithm that carries out your learning rule.

Which one helps depends on why the hypothesis is bad, and lesson 5 gave us the language for “why”. The error of what you ship has two sources. Part of it is the class’s fault: even the best hypothesis in the class makes mistakes. The rest is the data’s fault: the learner, working from finitely many examples, picked something worse than that best one. On the plain mail room with the twenty one-feature hypotheses, the best is feature 1, with true risk 0.10, and every other feature is a coin flip with true risk 0.5. If the learner picked feature 3, the class is to blame for 0.10 and the data for the remaining 0.4.

In general, as long as the approximation error is above zero, expect the empirical risk to rise with the number of examples, since more examples are harder to explain all at once, and the validation risk to fall. When the VC dimension is finite and the number of examples grows without end, both converge to the approximation error. So by extrapolating the two curves you can guess the approximation error, or at least an interval that contains it.

That gives the remedy for a small empirical risk with a large validation risk. Either way, the training sequence is too small for this class. If the validation curve has started to fall, more examples are the best cure, if you can afford them; a simpler class is the other reasonable move. If the validation curve sits near 1/21/21/2, you have no evidence that the approximation error is small, and more examples may not help at all. More data can still settle it, since eventually you would see the validation risk fall or the empirical risk rise. But if labels are expensive, try reducing the complexity of the class first.

Put together, this is the procedure:

  1. If learning involves parameters, plot the model-selection curve and make sure you tuned them in the right regime.
  2. If the empirical risk is too large, enlarge the class, replace it, or change the features.
  3. If the empirical risk is small, plot learning curves and decide whether the problem is estimation error or approximation error.
  4. If the approximation error looks small enough, get more data; if you can’t, reduce the complexity of the class.
  5. If the approximation error looks large too, change the class or the features entirely.

What people get wrong

“The test set is clean, because we never trained on it.” Choosing is a kind of training. Once a set has been used to pick among candidates, the winner’s score on it is biased low, and the single-hypothesis guarantee no longer applies. Either report with the margin for every candidate you compared, or report from a set nobody looked at.

“Trying more candidates can only help.” Each extra candidate costs a little margin, log⁡(2r/δ)\log(2r/\delta)log(2r/δ) grows with rrr, and a large enough search overfits the validation set itself. More candidates help only while the validation set is big enough to tell them apart.

“The cross-validation error is the true risk of the hypothesis I ship.” Each fold’s error is an honest estimate for a hypothesis trained on fewer examples, not for the final one. Usually the two are close; in general there is no guarantee, and the first problem below shows the estimate can be off by 1/21/21/2.

“The empirical risk is small, so the class is rich enough.” A class that can label any small training sequence perfectly gets empirical risk 0 whatever its approximation error. Only the learning curves, or more data, tell the two apart.

Recap and the next bug

  1. Model selection chooses among learners, or settings of one learner, from the data. Choosing by the smallest empirical risk always prefers the richest candidate, so something else must decide.
  2. Structural risk minimization chooses by minimizing a bound, empirical risk plus a complexity term that grows with the class. It is sound, but often far too pessimistic.
  3. A validation set drawn independently of a hypothesis estimates its true risk within log⁡(2/δ)/(2mv)\sqrt{\log(2/\delta)/(2m_v)}log(2/δ)/(2mv​)​, whatever the learner. The independence is the whole point.
  4. Choosing among rrr candidates on a validation set costs a union bound: every candidate is within log⁡(2r/δ)/(2mv)\sqrt{\log(2r/\delta)/(2m_v)}log(2r/δ)/(2mv​)​, and the winner is within twice that of the best candidate.
  5. k-fold cross validation reuses every example for both training and validation; it usually works, but carries no general guarantee. A final test set, touched once, gives the reported number its guarantee.
  6. When learning fails, split the error into pieces you can measure. A large empirical risk proves a large approximation error (for ERM); a small one is ambiguous, and learning curves resolve it.
