The loop that made things worse

Last lesson’s certificate came with a condition that was easy to miss: every message in it carried its label. Your users labelled 600 messages of each kind, and the theorems did the rest. But labels are the expensive part of the whole business. Meanwhile the product team has been saving something else, an archive of a million messages that nobody ever labelled, and they have a hunch: two kinds of message are hiding in it, newsletters and personal notes, with different length scores. (A length score, remember, is the natural logarithm of a message’s number of characters.) If a filter could learn from the archive alone, labels would stop being a bottleneck.

Ines takes the hunch seriously and builds the obvious loop. Start with a guess for the two kinds: where each one is centred on the length score, and what share of the archive it makes up. Then repeat two steps. First, give every message to whichever kind finds it more probable. Second, refit: move each kind’s centre to the average score of the messages it just received, and set its share to the fraction of messages it received. She keeps one number on her screen, the archive’s log-likelihood: how likely the current model finds all the messages together, measured in logs, as in lesson 24a. Higher is better, and a loop that keeps refitting to the data ought to push it up.

She tries it on a test archive of 200 messages, 140 personal notes centred on a length score of 1 and 60 newsletters centred on 4, with the kinds hidden from the loop. Before the first round the log-likelihood is −711.7-711.7−711.7. After round one it is −422.2-422.2−422.2, a huge improvement. Then the dashboard turns red. After round two it is −423.2-423.2−423.2, then −424.3-424.3−424.3, then −426.5-426.5−426.5, and by round forty it has sunk to −433.7-433.7−433.7. The model gets worse every round it is given. Look at where it ends up: the centres are at −1.1-1.1−1.1 and 2.042.042.04, nowhere near the 1 and 4 that made the archive, and one of the two kinds claims 98% of all messages.

A colleague shows her a second loop that differs from the first by one small change. On the very same archive it climbs every single round, to −364.0-364.0−364.0, and lands on centres 1.18 and 3.98. There is a third oddity, too: when Ines starts both centres at the same score, her loop never pulls them apart.

Why does one sensible-sounding loop go downhill while its cousin never does? What is the one change that makes the cousin safe, and what can you promise about it? To find out you need two ideas. The first is how a generative model describes measured scores that move together, which gives a classifier you will recognise. The second is how it describes a kind of message that nobody ever observes.

What you need from lesson 24a

This lesson leans on a few ideas from the first half. Here they are, one line each, so you can check them against what you remember.

  • A generative model describes how each kind of message is produced by a few numbers, its parameters, and learning means estimating them from the training sequence.
  • The log-likelihood of a training sequence under parameters θ\thetaθ adds up, over the examples, the log of the probability (for measured values, the density) the model gave each one. The maximum likelihood estimator is the θ\thetaθ that makes it largest. For a Gaussian, the best centre is the average of the scores and the best width is the root of the average squared distance from that average.
  • Maximum likelihood is ERM (empirical risk minimization, from lesson 3) with the log loss −log⁡Pθ[x]-\log P_\theta[x]−logPθ​[x], the model’s surprise at what it saw. The extra surprise caused by a wrong model is the relative entropy, which is never negative; its proof rested on the inequality log⁡t≤t−1\log t \le t - 1logt≤t−1, which we use again below.
  • Bayes’ rule turns “how likely is this message under each kind” into “how likely is each kind given this message”: a kind’s share times its chance of writing the message, divided by the total over the kinds. The Bayes optimal predictor gives each message whichever label that makes more probable.
  • Naive Bayes assumes the words are independent given the label, which cuts 2d2^d2d parameters to 2d+12d + 12d+1.

Another assumption: Gaussian clouds

Naive Bayes, from lesson 24a, is one generative assumption. Here is a second, for measured features, and it leads somewhere you have been before.

Hidden causes: latent variables

So far every example came with its label. Now look at the unlabelled archive: a million messages nobody ever clicked on. The product team suspects there are two kinds of message in it, newsletters and personal notes, with different length scores, but no message says which kind it is. The kind is a latent variable: part of the story of how the message was produced, but never observed.

A model with a latent variable tells the production story in two steps. First, choose the kind: kind yyy with probability cyc_ycy​, for yyy in {1,…,k}\{1, \dots, k\}{1,…,k}. Second, write a message of that kind: draw xxx from a Gaussian with centre μy\mu_yμy​ and covariance Σy\Sigma_yΣy​. With two kinds, a newsletter share of c2=0.3c_2 = 0.3c2​=0.3, and centres 1 and 4 on the length score, you get a two-humped density. The probability (density) of a message adds over the kinds that could have produced it:

P[X=x]=∑y=1kP[Y=y] P[X=x∣Y=y]=∑y=1kcy 1(2π)d/2∣Σy∣1/2exp⁡(−12(x−μy)⊤Σy−1(x−μy)).\mathbb P[X = x] = \sum_{y=1}^{k}\mathbb P[Y = y]\,\mathbb P[X = x \mid Y = y] = \sum_{y=1}^{k} c_y\,\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}|\Sigma_y|^{1/2}}\exp\left(-\frac12(x - \mu_y)^\top\Sigma_y^{-1}(x - \mu_y)\right).P[X=x]=y=1∑k​P[Y=y]P[X=x∣Y=y]=y=1∑k​cy​(2π)d/2∣Σy​∣1/21​exp(−21​(x−μy​)⊤Σy−1​(x−μy​)).

The sum inside the logarithm is the trouble. Without it, the log of each joint probability would split into simple pieces, and maximizing would be as easy as for LDA: if we knew each message’s kind, we would just average each kind’s messages. With it, there is no closed form, and in many cases the optimization is computationally hard. The Expectation-Maximization algorithm, EM, due to Dempster, Laird and Rubin, is an iterative way to search for a maximum. It may stop at a local maximum rather than the global one, but it often works well in practice.

EM’s idea is a chicken-and-egg loop. If we knew the kinds, finding θ\thetaθ would be easy. If we knew θ\thetaθ, we could compute, for each message, how probable each kind is. So alternate. Why EM never goes downhill

An iterative algorithm with no guarantee is a hope. EM comes with one: the log-likelihood never decreases. The proof is short once we see EM as climbing a different function, one coordinate at a time. The plan: build a function GGG of the beliefs and the parameters that is never above the log-likelihood, touches it whenever the beliefs are the E step’s, and goes up at every step. Then the log-likelihood, which GGG touches after every E step, must go up too.

Soft k-means

Let’s write EM out for a mixture of kkk Gaussians in which every covariance is the identity, Σy=I\Sigma_y = IΣy​=I, so the parameters are the shares ccc and the centres μ1,…,μk\mu_1, \dots, \mu_kμ1​,…,μk​.

E step. By Bayes’ rule (lesson 24a’s identity from “how likely is this message under each kind” to “how likely is each kind given this message”), the probability of kind yyy given message xix_ixi​ is the joint probability divided by the total over kinds. Compare this with the k-means algorithm of lesson 22. There, each message is assigned to its nearest centre, and each centre moves to the average of its assigned messages. Here, each message is assigned to every centre with a probability, and each centre moves to a weighted average over the entire sample. That is why EM for this model is called soft k-means.

SoftKMeans(x_1..x_m, k, T):
  choose initial c and μ_1..μ_k
  repeat T times (or until L stops improving):
    for i = 1..m:                      ▷ E step
      for y = 1..k:
        Q[i, y] ← c_y · exp(−½‖x_i − μ_y‖²)
      divide row i of Q by its sum
    for y = 1..k:                      ▷ M step
      n_y ← Σ_i Q[i, y]
      μ_y ← (Σ_i Q[i, y] x_i) / n_y
      c_y ← n_y / m
  return c, μ

Cost. Each E step computes mkmkmk squared distances in ddd dimensions, O(mkd)O(mkd)O(mkd) time; each M step is another weighted pass, O(mkd)O(mkd)O(mkd). So one iteration costs O(mkd)O(mkd)O(mkd) time, the same as one k-means iteration, and the memory is O(mk+kd)O(mk + kd)O(mk+kd) for QQQ and the centres (the data itself aside). In practice the exponentials are computed after subtracting each row’s largest exponent, so that exp⁡\expexp of a very negative number does not underflow to 0 for every kind at once.

Fix the bug

Ines has the safe loop, and the product team wants to ship it on the live archive. Your manager will accept it only with a certificate, held to this lesson’s theorems and not to this week’s luck. It has three parts:

  1. Never worse: every round’s E step gives each message its exact posterior over the two kinds, and every round’s M step leaves the expected log-likelihood FFF at least as high as it found it. By this lesson’s theorem the archive’s log-likelihood then never falls, on any archive.
  2. Settled: within a budget of 40 rounds, the last round raises the log-likelihood by less than 0.01 nats, on each of 40 test archives.
  3. A real fit: on each of the 40 test archives, the final log-likelihood is at most 1 nat below that of the true shares and centres. (An honest fit scores above the truth, and the test archives come from a generator whose truth is known.)

The 40 test archives are 200-score archives like the one above, drawn with seeds 0 to 39, and every run starts from the same guess: centres 0 and 0.5, shares one half each.

You choose three things. The E step: the exact posterior, Ines’s hard assignment, or the posterior computed as if both shares were one half. The M step: the exact maximizer; a half-way step, which moves every parameter half the distance from its old value to the maximizer; refitting only the centres and keeping the shares; or the exact maximizer plus a random jitter of up to 0.3 on each centre. And the number of rounds TTT, at most 40. Here are the three parts as code, for some of the settings, so that you can work out which to try before you open the lab:

What people get wrong

“EM finds the maximum likelihood estimate.” The theorem only says it never goes downhill. It can stop at a local maximum, and a bad start (two identical centres, for instance) can keep it there forever.

“Giving each message wholly to its most probable kind is just a cheaper version of the same loop.” It is a different loop, and the theorem does not cover it. The soft assignment is what makes GGG touch the log-likelihood after every E step; an all-or-nothing assignment leaves a rounding tax, up to log⁡2\log 2log2 nats per message for two kinds. On the archive of this lesson that loop fell from −422.2-422.2−422.2 to −433.7-433.7−433.7 while the soft loop climbed to −364.0-364.0−364.0.

“LDA weighs each score by how far apart its averages are.” The weights are Σ−1(μ1−μ0)\Sigma^{-1}(\mu_1 - \mu_0)Σ−1(μ1​−μ0​), not μ1−μ0\mu_1 - \mu_0μ1​−μ0​. In this lesson’s clouds junk has an average link score one higher than keep, and the link score still gets weight 0, because the capitals score already explains it.

Recap and the next bug

  1. A Gaussian cloud in ddd dimensions has a centre μ\muμ and a covariance matrix Σ\SigmaΣ; its density thins out with the stretched squared distance (x−μ)⊤Σ−1(x−μ)(x - \mu)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(x - \mu)(x−μ)⊤Σ−1(x−μ).
  2. LDA assumes equally likely labels, Gaussian classes and one shared covariance. The Bayes optimal predictor is then a halfspace, with w=Σ−1(μ1−μ0)w = \Sigma^{-1}(\mu_1 - \mu_0)w=Σ−1(μ1​−μ0​) and b=−12⟨w,μ0+μ1⟩b = -\frac12\langle w, \mu_0 + \mu_1\rangleb=−21​⟨w,μ0​+μ1​⟩.   3. A latent variable is part of the production story that is never observed. A mixture of Gaussians is a weighted sum of bells, one per hidden kind, and its log-likelihood has a sum inside the log, so it has no closed-form maximum.
  4. EM alternates an E step (the soft assignment, the posterior of each kind given each message) and an M step (refit the parameters to maximize FFF). It is alternate maximization of GGG, and GGG touches the log-likelihood after every E step, so the log-likelihood never decreases. It can stop at a local maximum, and identical centres never separate.
  5. With identity covariances, EM is soft k-means: weighted averages for the centres and soft counts for the shares, O(mkd)O(mkd)O(mkd) per iteration.
  6. Replace the soft assignment by a hard one and GGG no longer touches the log-likelihood: L=G+τL = G + \tauL=G+τ, the rounding tax. A round then promises only L−τ+γL - \tau + \gammaL−τ+γ, which is why Ines’s loop went downhill.
  7. A loop worth shipping needs an exact E step, an M step that never lowers FFF, a log-likelihood that has settled within budget, and a fit close to the truth. Never getting worse is not the same as being right: freezing the shares is safe, and wrong.
