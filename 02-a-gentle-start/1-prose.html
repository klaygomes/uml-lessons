Thirty features and a few hundred labels

Your filter works again. The one-feature learner followed the spammers from one feature to the next, and the inbox is quiet. Then your manager drops by with two pieces of news.

The first is that the real mail system does not have four features, or ten. It has thirty. Every message comes in as thirty yes/no answers. Does the subject say “FREE”? Is there a link? Is the sender unknown? Was it sent at night? Is there an attachment? And twenty-five more like those.

The second is about money. Every label is a person reading a message and clicking “junk” or “keep”, and those people have better things to do. You can afford a few hundred labels. Not thousands.

So you face two questions, and lesson 1 left both of them open. Which hypothesis classes can you afford with a few hundred examples? And when the learner returns a hypothesis that fits all your examples, how sure can you be that its true risk is small?

In lesson 1 we answered a toy version of the second question. In a world of sixteen messages, we computed exactly how often a wrong one-feature hypothesis survives mmm random examples. That calculation leaned on everything being uniform and independent, and the real mail room is neither. Today we answer it for any finite hypothesis class and for any way messages might arrive. By the end you will have the first real theorem of this course, proved line by line, and a formula that turns a label budget into a promise.

First, though, we need a shared language. We have been using some of it already.

The language of the course

Most notation in learning theory is either standard or defined on the spot, and a notation table is something you consult, not something you read. But a few conventions will be on every page from here on, so let’s meet them through the mail room.

Distributions and sampling. D\mathcal DD is a distribution over some set, usually X\mathcal XX or Z\mathcal ZZ. In the mail room, D\mathcal DD says how often each of the 2302^{30}230 possible messages arrives. Writing z∼Dz \sim \mathcal Dz∼D means zzz is drawn according to D\mathcal DD.

The loss function. One more idea won’t be needed until lesson 3: a score for how badly a hypothesis does on one example. One last convention. Some lists of numbers have no smallest member. The risks 1/2,1/4,1/8,…1/2, 1/4, 1/8, \dots1/2,1/4,1/8,… keep shrinking toward 0 without ever reaching it, so no member is the minimum, yet 0 is clearly the floor they press against. We write min⁡\minmin and max⁡\maxmax even where a mathematician would insist on inf⁡\infinf and sup⁡\supsup, because the minimum might not be reached. The distinction rarely matters in this course. Keep it in mind when a “minimum” looks suspicious.

The framework: who knows what

Now the real content: the statistical learning framework. Its whole point is to say precisely what the learner knows and what it doesn’t.

Here is a story far from the mail room. You have just arrived on a small Pacific island where papayas are a big part of the diet, and you have never tasted one. You decide to judge them by two features, color and softness, and you taste a few to learn which ones are good. We will come back to the papayas twice. Your situation in the mail room is the same, with messages for papayas and junk for “not tasty”. Here are the pieces.

The learner’s input.

  • The domain set X\mathcal XX: every message that could arrive. Its elements are also called instances, and X\mathcal XX the instance space.
  • The label set Y\mathcal YY. For now it has two elements, {0,1}\{0, 1\}{0,1}, with 1 for junk.
  • The training data S=((x1,y1),…,(xm,ym))S = ((x_1, y_1), \dots, (x_m, y_m))S=((x1​,y1​),…,(xm​,ym​)), a finite sequence of labelled messages. This is our training sequence, and it is the only thing the learner ever sees. Many texts call it the training set, or the sample, but it is always a sequence: the same example can appear twice, and some learners care about the order. Each round bracket (xi,yi)(x_i, y_i)(xi​,yi​) is one example, the iii-th message with its label, and mmm is how many there are; with eight labelled messages, m=8m = 8m=8 and the sequence runs from (x1,y1)(x_1, y_1)(x1​,y1​) to (x8,y8)(x_8, y_8)(x8​,y8​).

The learner’s output is a hypothesis h:X→Yh : \mathcal X \to \mathcal Yh:X→Y, a rule that takes any message and returns a label; the arrow reads “from messages to labels”, just as it did for the loss. Other names for it are predictor and classifier, and we’ll keep saying hypothesis. A(S)A(S)A(S) is the hypothesis that learner AAA returns on SSS.

How the data is made. This is the new part, so read it slowly. The framework assumes two hidden objects.

  1. A probability distribution D\mathcal DD over X\mathcal XX, which decides how often each message arrives. It can be anything at all.
  2. A labelling function f:X→Yf : \mathcal X \to \mathcal Yf:X→Y, the “correct” label of every message, so that yi=f(xi)y_i = f(x_i)yi​=f(xi​) for every example.

Each example is made in two steps: draw a message xix_ixi​ from D\mathcal DD, then label it with fff. The learner knows neither D\mathcal DD nor fff. Finding fff is the whole job.

How success is measured. The true risk of hhh is the probability that hhh gets a fresh message wrong. Take the tiny world you are about to meet, where the truth is “FREE and unknown sender” and all sixteen messages are equally likely. The hypothesis “unknown sender means junk” disagrees with the truth on four of them (the ones with an unknown sender and no FREE), so a fresh message trips it up 4/16=1/44/16 = 1/44/16=1/4 of the time. In symbols:

Empirical risk minimization

The learner wants a hypothesis with small true risk, but it cannot compute true risk, because that needs D\mathcal DD and fff. What it can compute is the empirical risk, the fraction of the training examples hhh gets wrong. If your users labelled eight messages and hhh disagrees with them on three, its empirical risk is 3/83/83/8, no knowledge of D\mathcal DD required. In symbols:

Something may go wrong: overfitting

ERM sounds so natural that it’s worth seeing it fail before we go any further.

Back to the papayas. Picture a gray square of area 2, with a smaller blue square of area 1 inside it. Papayas are spread uniformly over the gray square, and a papaya is tasty exactly when it lands in the blue one. So half of all papayas are tasty.

ERM with an inductive bias

We don’t give up on ERM. Instead, we look for conditions under which ERM is safe. Safe means that when the ERM hypothesis does well on the training sequence, it is also very likely to do well on D\mathcal DD.

The fix is the one you met in lesson 1. Before seeing any data, the learner chooses a hypothesis class H\mathcal HH, and ERM is only allowed to search inside it. You already ran one such search: inside H≤2\mathcal H_{\le 2}H≤2​, the smallest empirical risk on the eight examples was 0, reached by hUh_UhU​ and hFUh_{FU}hFU​, and the learner had to return one of those two. In symbols:

ERMH(S)∈argmin⁡h∈HLS(h).\mathrm{ERM}_{\mathcal H}(S) \in \operatorname*{argmin}_{h \in \mathcal H} L_S(h).ERMH​(S)∈h∈Hargmin​LS​(h).

Inside out: LS(h)L_S(h)LS​(h) is the empirical risk of one hypothesis. The argmin⁡\operatorname{argmin}argmin with h∈Hh \in \mathcal Hh∈H beneath it runs over every hhh in the class and returns not the smallest risk itself but the set of hypotheses that reach it: “the arguments that minimize”. The ∈\in∈ says ERM returns some member of that set, any one. The subscript H\mathcal HH on ERM\mathrm{ERM}ERM names the class it searches, and the (S)(S)(S) says its answer depends on the training sequence. In plain words: ERM over H\mathcal HH returns one of the hypotheses in H\mathcal HH with the fewest training mistakes. The last line of the ERM snippet printed this set, ['U', 'FU'].

Restricting the learner this way biases it toward certain hypotheses, and the restriction is called an inductive bias, the same term as in lesson 1. Since H\mathcal HH is chosen before any data arrives, it should come from prior knowledge about the problem. For the papayas, a good choice is the class of axis-aligned rectangles in the color-softness plane, and ERM over it does not overfit. (Proving that is problem 3 at the end of this lesson.) The memorizer shows the opposite. A class containing every hypothesis that says “tasty” on a finite set of points is too big for ERM to be safe.

That raises a fundamental question of learning theory: over which hypothesis classes does ERMH\mathrm{ERM}_{\mathcal H}ERMH​ avoid overfitting? And there is a tension built into it. A smaller class protects you better against overfitting, but it is a stronger bias, and a stronger bias is more likely to leave the truth out. That is lesson 1’s pigeon-and-rat tradeoff again, and lesson 5 makes it exact.

Finite hypothesis classes

The simplest restriction you can put on a class is a cap on its size. This section shows that if H\mathcal HH is finite, ERMH\mathrm{ERM}_{\mathcal H}ERMH​ does not overfit, as long as the training sequence is large enough, and “large enough” depends on ∣H∣|\mathcal H|∣H∣.

Finite sounds restrictive. It isn’t, really. Think of the class of every hypothesis that can be written as a C++ program of at most 10910^9109 bits. That class is finite, and it is enormous. Two assumptions: realizability and i.i.d.

The first assumption is about the class. For now we take the simplest case: the class contains a hypothesis that is never wrong, apart from a set of messages that has probability zero. In the tiny world, H≤2\mathcal H_{\le 2}H≤2​ contains the truth hFUh_{FU}hFU​, whose true risk is 0, so this holds. The best one-feature hypothesis still has true risk 1/41/41/4, so for that class it fails.

The second assumption is about the training sequence. Any guarantee has to depend on how SSS relates to D\mathcal DD. If your users only ever labelled messages from their friends, SSS would tell you nothing about junk. The standard assumption is the following.

Misleading training sequences

How can ERM fail? Let’s catch it in the act. Since hSh_ShS​ is consistent, a failure means that ERM returned a consistent hypothesis whose true risk is above ε\varepsilonε. For that to happen, such a hypothesis has to exist in the first place.

So we name two sets. The bad hypotheses are the ones with true risk above ε\varepsilonε. Take ε=0.2\varepsilon = 0.2ε=0.2 in the tiny world: every hypothesis except hFUh_{FU}hFU​ is bad, since all nine wrong ones have true risk 0.25 or more. In symbols:

HB={h∈H:L(D,f)(h)>ε}.\mathcal H_B = \{ h \in \mathcal H : L_{(\mathcal D, f)}(h) > \varepsilon \}.HB​={h∈H:L(D,f)​(h)>ε}.

And a training sequence is misleading if some bad hypothesis is consistent with it. The eight examples of the ERM table are one: hUh_UhU​ is bad, with true risk 0.25, yet it makes no mistake on them. On a misleading training sequence, a bad hypothesis looks exactly as good as the truth. The proof: one bad hypothesis at a time

Here is the theorem. Call it the finite-class guarantee: it is the first real guarantee in the course. Its idea fits in a sentence: if the class is finite and contains the truth, a large enough training sequence makes ERM accurate with high confidence, and “large enough” grows with the logarithm of the class size. In the tiny world, with 10 hypotheses, ε=0.2\varepsilon = 0.2ε=0.2 and δ=0.01\delta = 0.01δ=0.01, large enough turns out to be 35 examples.

The finite-class guarantee. Let H\mathcal HH be a finite hypothesis class. Let δ∈(0,1)\delta \in (0, 1)δ∈(0,1) and ε>0\varepsilon > 0ε>0, and let mmm be an integer with

m≥log⁡(∣H∣/δ)ε.m \ge \frac{\log(|\mathcal H| / \delta)}{\varepsilon}.m≥εlog(∣H∣/δ)​.

Read the condition on mmm from the inside out. ∣H∣|\mathcal H|∣H∣ is how many hypotheses the class has, and δ∈(0,1)\delta \in (0, 1)δ∈(0,1) says δ\deltaδ is strictly between 0 and 1 (round brackets leave the endpoints out). The ratio ∣H∣/δ|\mathcal H| / \delta∣H∣/δ grows when the class grows or when you demand more confidence. log⁡\loglog is the natural logarithm, which tames that ratio into a small number. Dividing by ε\varepsilonε makes a stricter accuracy target cost more. And m≥m \gem≥ says the training sequence must be at least that long. In plain words: the number of examples you need is the logarithm of class size over failure chance, divided by the error you will tolerate. But affordable is not the same as safe. The guarantee assumes realizability. So the decision has two halves. First, choose a class that contains the truth, or that you have good reason to believe does. Second, choose the smallest such class, because its log-size is what you pay for in labels.

ERM over a finite class, as code

For a finite class, ERM is a brute-force search: score every hypothesis on the training sequence and keep one with the fewest mistakes.

ERM(H, S):
  best ← none
  best_errors ← m + 1
  for h in H:
    errors ← 0
    for i = 1..m:
      if h(x_i) ≠ y_i: errors ← errors + 1
    if errors < best_errors:
      best ← h
      best_errors ← errors
  return best

Ties go to the first hypothesis found, which is one legitimate member of the argmin. Under realizability, the loop could stop at the first hhh with zero errors.

Time. The search makes m⋅∣H∣m \cdot |\mathcal H|m⋅∣H∣ hypothesis evaluations. If one evaluation costs TTT, the total is O(m ∣H∣ T)O(m \, |\mathcal H| \, T)O(m∣H∣T). For a pair hypothesis T=O(1)T = O(1)T=O(1): read two bits. So ERM over the 465 hypotheses of H≤2\mathcal H_{\le 2}H≤2​ with 215 examples is 99,975 evaluations, nothing at all. For conjunctions of literals, it would be about 1.5×10171.5 \times 10^{17}1.5×1017 evaluations: the class is affordable in theory, hopeless as brute force. Whether a cleverer search exists is a separate question, and lesson 8 is about exactly that tension.

Space. The loop keeps one best hypothesis and one counter, so beyond the input it uses O(1)O(1)O(1) words plus whatever it takes to represent one hypothesis. This assumes the class can be enumerated one hypothesis at a time, as the singles and pairs can with two nested loops. Storing H\mathcal HH as a list would cost O(∣H∣)O(|\mathcal H|)O(∣H∣) instead.

What people get wrong

“The guarantee says I need 215 labels, so with 200 the learner fails.” The condition is sufficient, not necessary. Below 215 the guarantee simply says nothing. In the lab’s uniform world, misleading sequences are already rare at 30 or 40 examples. The 215 buys a promise that holds for every distribution, including ones you would never guess.

“A bigger class needs proportionally more data.” The cost grows with log⁡∣H∣\log |\mathcal H|log∣H∣, not with ∣H∣|\mathcal H|∣H∣. Going from 30 hypotheses to 465, fifteen times as many, raises the requirement from 161 to 215. What breaks the budget is a class whose size is exponential in the number of features, such as 3303^{30}330 or 22302^{2^{30}}2230.

“The guarantee is about the hypothesis I got.” It is about the procedure. The probability is over the draw of SSS. Think of all the training sequences your users might have labelled: at most a δ\deltaδ fraction of them lead ERM to a hypothesis with true risk above ε\varepsilonε. Once you hold one particular hSh_ShS​, the guarantee can’t tell you whether your SSS was one of the unlucky ones.

“ε\varepsilonε and δ\deltaδ are interchangeable knobs.” They have very different prices. From the 215 at ε=0.05\varepsilon = 0.05ε=0.05 and δ=0.01\delta = 0.01δ=0.01, halving ε\varepsilonε doubles the requirement to 430. Making δ\deltaδ ten times smaller only adds log⁡10/ε≈46\log 10 / \varepsilon \approx 46log10/ε≈46 examples, giving 261. Confidence is cheap; accuracy is not.
