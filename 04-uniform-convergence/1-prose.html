The dashboard that always looks better than production

Since the last lesson, the team has grown up. Nobody pretends any more that one hypothesis will agree with every label: users disagree, people click in a hurry, and the promise you now make is the agnostic one, a true risk close to the best in the class. The mail room this year has the thirty features from lesson 2, and the class the team works with is the simplest one: thirty one-feature hypotheses, h1,…,h30h_1, \dots, h_{30}h1​,…,h30​, where hjh_jhj​ says junk exactly when feature jjj is present.

To keep everyone honest, someone built a dashboard. Every Monday, users label a fresh batch of 60 messages. The dashboard has one row per hypothesis, and each row shows that hypothesis’s empirical risk on this week’s 60 examples: the fraction it gets wrong. Then the team does the obvious thing. They ship the hypothesis at the top of the dashboard, the one with the smallest empirical risk. That is ERM, empirical risk minimization from lesson 2, run by hand, every week.

A month later, production reports back. Production measures the shipped hypothesis on the thousands of messages users labelled after it went live, which is so many that its number is, for all practical purposes, the true risk. And production is disappointed. Not once, but every single week. On average, the dashboard said the shipped hypothesis would be wrong on 18% of messages. Production says 28%.

You check the code. The counting is right: each row really is the fraction of this week’s examples that its hypothesis gets wrong. The examples are drawn fairly, nobody peeks, and no row is computed on the wrong data. Every number on the dashboard is correct, and yet the number you act on is wrong by ten points, always in the same direction.

How can a dashboard where every row is computed correctly be systematically misleading? And how many labels would you need before you could trust it? That is this lesson.

A world we can see into

The team cannot see the true risks; that is the whole problem. But we can build a world where we know them, and watch the dashboard from above.

In this world, every message is junk or keep with equal chance, and each of the thirty features is a noisy signal of the label. Feature jjj agrees with the label except on a fraction rjr_jrj​ of messages, and these disagreements happen independently from feature to feature. So the true risk of hjh_jhj​ is exactly rjr_jrj​. We spread the thirty rows evenly: the first row is wrong on 25% of messages, and each row after it is wrong half a percentage point more often than the one before, so row 2 is at 25.5% and row 3 at 26%. That fairness has a name and a one-line proof, and it is worth having both. Recall from lesson 3 that a loss function ℓ(h,z)\ell(h, z)ℓ(h,z) scores hypothesis hhh on one example zzz. An estimate whose expectation equals the thing it estimates is called unbiased. Every row of the dashboard, looked at alone, is unbiased.

Here is the same claim run 10,000 times instead of eight. Under the 0-1 loss, the number of mistakes in a week is a sum of 60 independent coin flips that each come up “mistake” with probability 0.30, which Python can draw directly. Nothing here is a coincidence of this particular week. Ranking by empirical risk sorts the rows by true risk plus luck, and the top of that ranking is exactly where rows with large negative luck collect. Each row is a fair estimate, but the row you pick for being smallest is not. This is called the winner’s curse: when you select the best-looking of many noisy estimates, the estimate of the winner is biased toward looking good. The name comes from auctions, where the bidder who wins a contested oil lease is usually the one who overestimated it most.

We can prove the direction of the bias in general. In what follows, H\mathcal HH is the hypothesis class, as in lesson 1, here the thirty rows, and hSh_ShS​ is the hypothesis ERM returns on the training sequence SSS, the shipped row. Its dashboard number is the smallest on the dashboard, 0.183 in the typical week, which we write LS(hS)=min⁡h∈HLS(h)L_S(h_S) = \min_{h \in \mathcal H} L_S(h)LS​(hS​)=minh∈H​LS​(h): read min⁡h∈H\min_{h \in \mathcal H}minh∈H​ as “the smallest value, over every hhh in H\mathcal HH, of”.

ES[LS(hS)]  ≤  min⁡h∈HLD(h)  ≤  ES[LD(hS)].\mathbb E_S\big[L_S(h_S)\big] \;\le\; \min_{h \in \mathcal H} L_{\mathcal D}(h) \;\le\; \mathbb E_S\big[L_{\mathcal D}(h_S)\big].ES​[LS​(hS​)]≤h∈Hmin​LD​(h)≤ES​[LD​(hS​)].

Read it from the inside out. In the middle, min⁡h∈HLD(h)\min_{h \in \mathcal H} L_{\mathcal D}(h)minh∈H​LD​(h) is the best true risk in the class, a fixed number that does not change from week to week: 0.25 here, the true risk of h1h_1h1​. On the left, LS(hS)L_S(h_S)LS​(hS​) is the number the dashboard shows for the shipped row, and ES\mathbb E_SES​ averages it over all possible weeks. Proof. Start with the left inequality. Pick any fixed hypothesis h′∈Hh' \in \mathcal Hh′∈H. The right inequality is even simpler. Whatever SSS is, hSh_ShS​ is a member of H\mathcal HH, so its true risk is at least the smallest true risk in H\mathcal HH. Take expectations again. ■\blacksquare■

Notice exactly where unbiasedness was used: for a fixed h′h'h′, chosen before the examples. The proposition tells you which way the dashboard errs. It does not tell you by how much, and it gives you no way to make the error small. For that we need a stronger idea. A dashboard you can trust row by row

Watch what the lab did as you raised the number of examples. The luck in every row shrank, and once every row’s luck was small, the winner’s luck had nowhere to hide. That is the whole idea of this lesson, and it deserves a definition. Pick a small tolerance, say 0.1, and suppose that this week every row’s luck, the winner’s included, lies between −0.1 and +0.1. Then no row, whichever one you ship, can look more than 0.1 better or worse than it is. We call the tolerance ε\varepsilonε (the Greek letter epsilon), the accuracy parameter of lesson 3.

Definition (ε-representative sample). A training sequence SSS is ε\varepsilonε-representative (with respect to the set ZZZ of all possible examples, the domain, a hypothesis class H\mathcal HH, a loss function ℓ\ellℓ and a distribution D\mathcal DD) if

∀h∈H,∣LS(h)−LD(h)∣≤ε.\forall h \in \mathcal H, \quad |L_S(h) - L_{\mathcal D}(h)| \le \varepsilon.∀h∈H,∣LS​(h)−LD​(h)∣≤ε.

The bars ∣⋅∣|\cdot|∣⋅∣ around a number mean its absolute value, its size with the sign dropped (around a set, as in lesson 1, the same bars mean how many elements it has; here they wrap a number). So ∣…∣≤ε|\dots| \le \varepsilon∣…∣≤ε says the luck is at most ε\varepsilonε in either direction. The symbol ∀\forall∀ reads “for every”, so ∀h∈H\forall h \in \mathcal H∀h∈H demands this of every row at once. In plain words: this week, no row of the dashboard is off from its true risk by more than ε\varepsilonε.

Why is this the right condition? Because it survives selection. The winner’s curse happens because the row you pick depends on the luck. A promise about every row cannot be dodged that way: whichever row ERM picks, the promise was already made about it. Concretely, if every row is within 0.1 this week, the shipped row’s true risk is at most its dashboard number plus 0.1. And the best row, say at 0.25, shows at most 0.35, so the shipped row shows at most 0.35 too and is truly at most 0.45: within twice the tolerance of the best.

The uniform convergence property

The lemma is about one fixed training sequence. But the training sequence is random. So the real question is: how many examples do we need so that, with high probability, the sample we get is representative? For instance: how many examples a week make the 30-row dashboard 0.05-representative in at least 95 weeks out of 100, whatever the world looks like? The 5 bad weeks in 100 we are willing to tolerate are written δ=0.05\delta = 0.05δ=0.05 (the Greek letter delta), the confidence parameter of lesson 3.

Definition (uniform convergence). A hypothesis class H\mathcal HH has the uniform convergence property (with respect to a domain ZZZ and a loss function ℓ\ellℓ) if there exists a function mHUC:(0,1)2→Nm^{\mathrm{UC}}_{\mathcal H}: (0, 1)^2 \to \mathbb NmHUC​:(0,1)2→N such that for every ε,δ∈(0,1)\varepsilon, \delta \in (0, 1)ε,δ∈(0,1) and for every probability distribution D\mathcal DD over ZZZ, if SSS is a training sequence of m≥mHUC(ε,δ)m \ge m^{\mathrm{UC}}_{\mathcal H}(\varepsilon, \delta)m≥mHUC​(ε,δ) examples drawn i.i.d. from D\mathcal DD, then with probability at least 1−δ1 - \delta1−δ, SSS is ε\varepsilonε-representative.

This is the densest sentence in the lesson, so let’s take it apart.

  • mHUCm^{\mathrm{UC}}_{\mathcal H}mHUC​ is a function. The superscript UC stands for uniform convergence, and the subscript says which class it belongs to.
  • (0,1)2→N(0, 1)^2 \to \mathbb N(0,1)2→N is its type: it takes a pair of numbers, each strictly between 0 and 1 (that is what (0,1)2(0, 1)^2(0,1)2 means), and returns a natural number, an element of N={1,2,3,… }\mathbb N = \{1, 2, 3, \dots\}N={1,2,3,…}, here a count of examples. Infinite classes, the engineer’s way

The dashboard as code

Here is the procedure the team should have run from the start. The new line is the first one: the number of examples is computed from the promise, before any data arrives.

ShipFromDashboard(H, ε, δ):
  m ← ⌈ log(2|H|/δ) / (2ε²) ⌉
  S ← m fresh labelled examples z_1, ..., z_m
  for each h in H:
    dashboard[h] ← (1/m) · Σ_{i=1..m} ℓ(h, z_i)
  h_S ← an h with the smallest dashboard[h]
  return h_S, with the promises
    L_D(h_S) ≤ dashboard[h_S] + ε
    L_D(h_S) ≤ min_{h ∈ H} L_D(h) + 2ε
    each holding with probability at least 1 − δ

What people get wrong

“Every row is an unbiased estimate, so the winner’s row is too.” Unbiasedness is a property of a hypothesis fixed before the data. The winner is chosen by the data, and the proposition of this lesson shows its empirical risk is, on average, at most the best true risk in the class, while its true risk is at least that. In our world: 0.18 shown, 0.28 delivered.

“More hypotheses on the dashboard can only help: more choice, better winner.” More choice also means more chances for a lucky row to win. In the lab, with true risks spread evenly from 0.25 to 0.40 and 60 examples a week, growing the dashboard from 30 rows to 465 pushes the winner’s empirical risk down from about 0.18 to about 0.13 while its true risk stays near 0.27. The cure costs examples, but only log⁡∣H∣\log |\mathcal H|log∣H∣ of them.

“The theorem says I need 1,419 examples.” It says 1,419 examples are enough, for every distribution. It is a sufficient sample size, not a necessary one. In our particular world, about 900 would have kept the non-representative weeks under 5%. You use the theorem because you cannot see D\mathcal DD, not because it is tight.

“Uniform convergence is just the law of large numbers.” The law of large numbers is about one average, and only in the limit. Uniform convergence is about all the averages in the class at once, at a finite mmm, with a number attached. That is why we needed Hoeffding’s exponential tail and the union bound, not just the limit.

Where this comes from

Classes for which uniform convergence holds are also called Glivenko-Cantelli classes, after Valery Glivenko and Francesco Cantelli, who proved the first uniform convergence result in the 1930s. The connection between uniform convergence and learnability was studied in depth by Vapnik. Lesson 6 will show that for binary classification, uniform convergence is not only sufficient for learnability but also necessary. For more general learning problems that is not the case.
