Terms:
- unbiased: an estimate whose expectation equals the quantity it estimates; \(\mathbb E_S[L_S(h)] = L_{\mathcal D}(h)\) for a fixed \(h\), by linearity of expectation.
- luck: \(L_S(h) - L_{\mathcal D}(h)\) for one row in one week.
- winner's curse: selecting the smallest of many noisy estimates makes the winner's estimate optimistic; \(\mathbb E[L_S(h_S)] \le \min_h L_{\mathcal D}(h) \le \mathbb E[L_{\mathcal D}(h_S)]\).
- \(\varepsilon\)-representative: \(\forall h \in \mathcal H,\ |L_S(h) - L_{\mathcal D}(h)| \le \varepsilon\) (w.r.t. \(Z\), \(\mathcal H\), \(\ell\), \(\mathcal D\)).
- uniform convergence property: there is \(m^{\mathrm{UC}}_{\mathcal H}: (0,1)^2 \to \mathbb N\) such that for all \(\varepsilon, \delta\) and all \(\mathcal D\), \(m \ge m^{\mathrm{UC}}_{\mathcal H}(\varepsilon, \delta)\) i.i.d. examples are \(\varepsilon\)-representative with probability at least \(1 - \delta\); "uniform" over \(\mathcal H\) and over \(\mathcal D\).
- measure concentration inequality: a finite-\(m\) bound on the probability that an average strays from its expectation.
- Markov: \(Z \ge 0\), \(\mathbb P[Z \ge a] \le \mathbb E Z / a\). Chebyshev: \(\mathbb P[|Z - \mathbb E Z| \ge a] \le \operatorname{Var} Z / a^2\).
- Hoeffding's lemma: \(X \in [a, b]\), \(\mathbb E X = 0\): \(\mathbb E e^{\lambda X} \le e^{\lambda^2 (b-a)^2/8}\).
- Hoeffding's inequality: i.i.d. \(Z_i \in [a, b]\), mean \(\mu\): \(\mathbb P[|\bar Z - \mu| > \varepsilon] \le 2e^{-2m\varepsilon^2/(b-a)^2}\).
- discretization trick: a class described by \(d\) 64-bit numbers has at most \(2^{64d}\) members, so \(m_{\mathcal H} \le (128d + 2\log(2/\delta))/\varepsilon^2\).

Misconceptions (all four in "What people get wrong"):
- "Each row is unbiased, so the winner's row is too." Why wrong: unbiasedness holds for a hypothesis fixed before the data; the winner is chosen by the data (proposition; 0.179 shown, 0.25 best, 0.2755 delivered).
- "More hypotheses can only help." Why wrong: more lucky candidates; at \(m = 60\), 30 to 465 rows moves the winner's empirical risk 0.181 to 0.127 while its true risk stays about 0.27 (act 1 numbers below).
- "The theorem says I need 1,419." Why wrong: sufficient, not necessary; about 900 suffice in this world.
- "Uniform convergence is the law of large numbers." Why wrong: one average in the limit versus all averages at a finite \(m\) with a number.

Derivation (prose order):
1. Unbiasedness of a fixed row by linearity (book p. 56).
2. Winner's curse proposition: \(L_S(h_S) \le L_S(h')\) pointwise, expectations, unbiasedness of fixed \(h'\), min over \(h'\); right side pointwise \(L_{\mathcal D}(h_S) \ge \min\). Not in the book.
3. Definition 4.1; Lemma 4.2 in the form "\(\varepsilon\)-representative implies \(L_{\mathcal D}(h_S) \le L_S(h_S) + \varepsilon\) and \(\le \min + 2\varepsilon\)", chain of three inequalities as in the book's proof.
4. Definition 4.3; agnostic PAC recalled (Def. 3.3/3.4); Corollary 4.4 with proof (event inclusion).
5. Markov (indicator proof), Chebyshev, variance of an i.i.d. average (cross terms vanish by independence), Hoeffding's lemma (convexity chord, expectation with mean 0, change of variables \(p = -a/(b-a)\), \(h = \lambda(b-a)\), \(L(0) = L'(0) = 0\), \(L'' = q(1-q) \le 1/4\), Taylor with Lagrange remainder), Hoeffding's inequality (Markov on \(e^{\lambda \bar X}\), independence of functions of independent variables, lemma with \(\lambda/m\), optimal \(\lambda = 4m\varepsilon/(b-a)^2\), symmetric tail, union bound of two one-sided events; \(>\) is contained in \(\ge\)).
6. Corollary 4.6 in six steps: complement, union (Eq. 4.1), Hoeffding per fixed \(h\) with \(\theta_i = \ell(h, z_i)\) (Eq. 4.2), sum to \(2|\mathcal H|e^{-2m\varepsilon^2}\), solve for \(m\), apply Corollary 4.4 with \(\varepsilon/2\). Why Hoeffding cannot be applied to \(h_S\) directly.
7. Remark 4.1, discretization trick.

Concept check, answers (never shown to the learner; critique against these):
1. Hoeffding needs \(\theta_1, \dots, \theta_m\) i.i.d. with mean \(\mu\) fixed in advance. With \(\theta_i = \ell(h_S, z_i)\), the hypothesis \(h_S\) is a function of all of \(z_1, \dots, z_m\), so the \(\theta_i\) are not independent draws of a fixed variable, and their mean is not \(L_{\mathcal D}(h_S)\) (conditioning on which hypothesis won changes the distribution of the examples). The proof applies Hoeffding to each hypothesis fixed before the sample and pays with the union bound. Lab evidence: k = 465, m = 200, \(\varepsilon = 0.05\): the shortcut promises at most \(2e^{-1} = 0.736\); in 97.1% of 4,000 weeks (`cc1.py`, seed 3) the shipped hypothesis's true risk exceeded its empirical risk by more than 0.05. (At k = 30, m = 60 the shortcut's bound \(2e^{-0.3} > 1\) is vacuous, so that setting does not refute it; look for the learner choosing a setting where the bound is below 1.) Also acceptable: the mean gap, 0.179 against 0.276, which a fixed row never shows.
2. (a) Two hypotheses A and B with \(L_{\mathcal D}(A) = 0.25\), \(L_{\mathcal D}(B) = 0.35\). A training sequence of 20 examples on which each makes 6 mistakes: \(L_S(A) = L_S(B) = 0.30\), both within 0.05, so it is 0.05-representative; ERM may break the tie toward B, whose true risk is \(0.35 = 0.25 + 2 \times 0.05\). The factor 2 is reached (exactly with a tie; without a tie, arbitrarily close). (b) One row: ERM always returns it, so there is no selection and no winner's curse; the row is unbiased. The lemma's second promise is trivial (\(h_S\) is the best in the class); the first is just the row's own concentration. The theorem gives \(m \ge \log(2/\delta)/(2\varepsilon^2)\), plain Hoeffding with no union bound.
3. \(\lceil \log(2 \cdot 4525/0.01)/(2 \cdot 0.05^2) \rceil = \lceil 2743.14 \rceil = 2744\). Within 0.05 of the best: \(m^{\mathrm{UC}}(0.025, 0.01) = \lceil 10972.55 \rceil = 10973\). Doubling to 9,050 rows: 2,882, so 138 more (\(\log 2/(2 \cdot 0.0025) = 138.6\)). Look for the learner computing the increment as \(\log 2/(2\varepsilon^2)\) rather than rerunning blindly.
