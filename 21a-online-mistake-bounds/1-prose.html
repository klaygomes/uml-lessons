Online Mistake Bounds

Lesson text (draft) · Teacher's sheet

The spammers are watching

Lesson 20 ended with the triangle filter, certificate and all, losing ground the very next morning. Look closer at what the spammers do now. They no longer send a big batch of junk and wait. They send one message, see whether it got through, and then decide what to send next. If “FREE” in the subject gets caught, the next message says “F.R.E.E”. If the filter lets a message with a link through, the next hundred all have links. Every decision the filter makes is information for them, and they use it straight away.

Your monitoring shows the damage. The filter is retrained every night on the day’s labelled mail, and every morning it starts losing again, because whatever it learned overnight is exactly what the spammers stop doing. The mail today depends on what the filter did yesterday, and on what it did five minutes ago. Every guarantee from lessons 2 to 20 began with one promise: the messages are drawn independently from one fixed distribution D\mathcal DD, and the learner gets a whole training sequence S∼DmS \sim \mathcal D^mS∼Dm before it has to say anything. An opponent who reacts to you is about as far from that promise as anything can be, so not one of those guarantees applies.

So what can a learner promise when the mail is chosen by someone who reads its decisions? It sounds hopeless. It is not, and this lesson and the next, lesson 21b, are about what you can still prove. This one handles the case where some rule behind the junk is perfect; lesson 21b drops even that.

One message at a time

First, the shape of the new problem. Messages arrive one at a time. For each one, the filter must decide on the spot, junk or keep. Only afterwards does it find out the right answer, because a user opens the message and either reports it as junk or doesn’t. Then the next message arrives.

Each pass through that loop is a round. On round ttt:

  1. a message xtx_txt​ arrives (the spammers chose it);
  2. the learner announces a prediction ptp_tpt​, its label for xtx_txt​, with 1 for junk and 0 for keep, as always;
  3. the true label yty_tyt​ is revealed;
  4. the learner may update itself before round t+1t + 1t+1.

A learner that works this way is an online learner, and the setting is online learning. Lesson 1 named it, with the stockbroker who must decide every day using only what happened so far; now we study it. There is no separate training phase and test phase. Every message is first a test (the learner must predict) and then a training example (the learner sees the label). The score is the number of mistakes, the rounds where pt≠ytp_t \neq y_tpt​=yt​. Each mistake is a junk message in someone’s inbox or a real message in the junk folder, so the filter is paying for its education as it goes.

Notice what is missing: no D\mathcal DD. We make no assumption about where the messages come from. They may be fixed in advance, random, or chosen by an opponent who watches the learner’s every move and changes tactics after each decision. We call that opponent the adversary. A guarantee that holds against the adversary holds against anything, which is exactly what a filter facing real spammers needs.

Can anything be guaranteed against such an opponent? Let’s try the simplest possible attack before anything else. The adversary sends the same message every round, waits for the prediction, and then declares the opposite label to be the true one. Whatever the learner does, it is wrong.

def learner(history, x):
    past = [y for (u, y) in history if u == x]
    return int(2 * sum(past) >= len(past)) if past else 0

history, mistakes, T = [], 0, 100
for t in range(T):
    x = (1, 0, 1, 1)
    p = learner(history, x)
    y = 1 - p
    mistakes += p != y
    history.append((x, y))
print(mistakes, "mistakes in", T, "rounds")

It prints 100 mistakes in 100 rounds. The learner here is a sensible one: it predicts whatever label message xxx got most often in the past. It does not matter. The adversary reads p and sets y = 1 - p, so every round is a mistake. Replace learner with anything you like that returns 0 or 1, and the count stays at 100.

So with no restriction at all, the adversary wins every round. To say anything, we have to restrict the adversary somehow, the same way lesson 1 had to restrict the learner. The question is which restriction is both realistic and enough.

Realizability buys a mistake bound

The attack above works because the adversary’s labels make no sense: the same message is junk on Monday and keep on Tuesday. Real junk has a rule behind it. So here is the first restriction, and you have met it before. In lesson 2 it was called realizability: the labels come from some hypothesis in a class H\mathcal HH that the learner knows in advance.

Online, it reads like this. Before the game starts, the learner is told H\mathcal HH. The adversary still chooses every message, and in whatever order and with whatever cunning it likes. It may even put off deciding which hypothesis is “the truth” until the end, as long as, at the end, some single h⋆∈Hh^\star \in \mathcal Hh⋆∈H agrees with every label it gave. So all labels satisfy yt=h⋆(xt)y_t = h^\star(x_t)yt​=h⋆(xt​). Under that rule, the attack with one repeated message dies: once the adversary has called xxx junk, it must call xxx junk forever, because one hypothesis cannot give one message two labels.

Let’s use the smallest class we know, the one-feature class of lesson 1. Messages are four bits FLUN (says “FREE”, has a link, unknown sender, sent at night), and the class has four hypotheses, H1={hF,hL,hU,hN}\mathcal H_1 = \{h_F, h_L, h_U, h_N\}H1​={hF​,hL​,hU​,hN​}, where hFh_FhF​ says junk exactly when the F bit is 1, and so on. The adversary has picked one of the four as the truth, or will pick one by the end. How many mistakes can it force?

That number, the worst the adversary can do, is how we will measure online learners. Here it is in words first: run the learner on a sequence of examples and count its mistakes; then take the worst sequence the adversary could choose, over every length and every h⋆h^\starh⋆ in the class. If that worst count is finite, the learner has a promise to make: “whatever you send me, I will make at most this many mistakes, ever.”

Now the notation. A sequence of TTT labelled messages is written S=((x1,y1),…,(xT,yT))S = ((x_1, y_1), \dots, (x_T, y_T))S=((x1​,y1​),…,(xT​,yT​)), where TTT is the number of rounds. We call SSS realizable by H\mathcal HH when some h⋆∈Hh^\star \in \mathcal Hh⋆∈H has yt=h⋆(xt)y_t = h^\star(x_t)yt​=h⋆(xt​) for every round ttt. For an online learner AAA,

MA(S)=∣{t∈[T]:pt≠yt}∣,MA(H)=sup⁡SMA(S).M_A(S) = \left| \{ t \in [T] : p_t \neq y_t \} \right|, \qquad M_A(\mathcal H) = \sup_{S} M_A(S).MA​(S)=∣{t∈[T]:pt​=yt​}∣,MA​(H)=Ssup​MA​(S).

Read it from the inside out.

  • ptp_tpt​ is the prediction AAA made on round ttt, and yty_tyt​ the label revealed afterwards. The test pt≠ytp_t \neq y_tpt​=yt​ asks one yes/no question: was round ttt a mistake?
  • [T][T][T] is the set of round numbers {1,2,…,T}\{1, 2, \dots, T\}{1,2,…,T}, as [m][m][m] was the set of example positions in lesson 1.
  • The braces {t∈[T]:… }\{ t \in [T] : \dots \}{t∈[T]:…} collect the rounds where the answer is yes. The colon reads “such that”: the rounds ttt such that AAA got round ttt wrong.
  • The bars ∣⋅∣|\cdot|∣⋅∣ mean “how many”. So MA(S)M_A(S)MA​(S) is the number of mistakes AAA makes on the sequence SSS. No division this time: online, we count mistakes rather than average them.
  • sup⁡S\sup_SsupS​ (“supremum over SSS”) means: the largest value over every realizable sequence SSS, of any length TTT, labelled by any h⋆∈Hh^\star \in \mathcal Hh⋆∈H. It is a maximum that is allowed to be infinite, or to be approached without being reached.

In plain words: MA(S)M_A(S)MA​(S) is how many mistakes learner AAA makes on one sequence, and MA(H)M_A(\mathcal H)MA​(H) is the most mistakes any adversary can force on it, as long as the labels follow some hypothesis in H\mathcal HH. For a tiny number: if the learner predicts “junk” on every message and the adversary sends 1000, 0100, 0010 (all keep) and then 0001 (junk), labelled by hNh_NhN​, then MA(S)=3M_A(S) = 3MA​(S)=3.

A bound MA(H)≤B<∞M_A(\mathcal H) \le B < \inftyMA​(H)≤B<∞ is a mistake bound (here BBB is just a number, not the norm bound of lessons 12b to 15). A class H\mathcal HH is online learnable if some online learner AAA has a mistake bound for it. For now we ignore running time completely, as lesson 3 did at first with PAC learning: the learners in this lesson may be slow, and the efficient ones arrive in lesson 21b.

Consistent: keep the survivors

Lesson 2 taught us a learner that works in the batch world: return any hypothesis that is consistent with every example (under realizability, that is what empirical risk minimization, ERM, returns). The online version is just as natural. Keep the set of hypotheses that have agreed with every label so far; when a message arrives, pick any one of them and predict with it. Call it Consistent.

The set of hypotheses still consistent with everything seen is the version space. On round 1 it is the whole class. After each round, every hypothesis that disagreed with the revealed label leaves.

Let’s play it against the adversary on H1\mathcal H_1H1​. Suppose Consistent picks the first surviving hypothesis in the order F, L, U, N. The adversary knows this (it may know the learner’s code; only the truth is hidden from the learner). Could the adversary have done better than three? Here is the argument that it couldn’t, in words first. Every mistake throws out at least one hypothesis: the one Consistent predicted with was wrong, so it leaves. And the truth h⋆h^\starh⋆ never leaves, because it is never wrong. So the version space starts with ∣H∣|\mathcal H|∣H∣ hypotheses, loses at least one per mistake, and always keeps at least one.

Claim (Consistent’s mistake bound). For a finite class H\mathcal HH, MConsistent(H)≤∣H∣−1M_{\text{Consistent}}(\mathcal H) \le |\mathcal H| - 1MConsistent​(H)≤∣H∣−1.

Proof. Run Consistent on any realizable sequence and let MMM be its number of mistakes. On a mistake round, the hypothesis hhh that Consistent used predicted pt=h(xt)≠ytp_t = h(x_t) \neq y_tpt​=h(xt​)=yt​, so h∉Vt+1h \notin V_{t+1}h∈/Vt+1​: the version space shrinks by at least one. On other rounds it does not grow, since Vt+1⊆VtV_{t+1} \subseteq V_tVt+1​⊆Vt​. So after all TTT rounds, ∣VT+1∣≤∣H∣−M|V_{T+1}| \le |\mathcal H| - M∣VT+1​∣≤∣H∣−M.

The truth h⋆h^\starh⋆ has h⋆(xt)=yth^\star(x_t) = y_th⋆(xt​)=yt​ on every round, so by induction it is in every VtV_tVt​, and ∣VT+1∣≥1|V_{T+1}| \ge 1∣VT+1​∣≥1. Putting the two together, 1≤∣H∣−M1 \le |\mathcal H| - M1≤∣H∣−M, which rearranges to M≤∣H∣−1M \le |\mathcal H| - 1M≤∣H∣−1. The sequence was arbitrary, so the supremum obeys the same bound. ■\blacksquare■

With four hypotheses, that is at most 3, which the adversary reached. For lesson 1’s ten one-feature hypotheses it is 9, and for a class of a million hypotheses it is 999,999. A bound that grows linearly with the size of the class is a poor promise. Can a learner do better by choosing more carefully which survivor to follow?

Halving: let the survivors vote

Here is a better idea. Instead of following one survivor, let all of them vote. If more survivors say junk than keep, predict junk; otherwise predict keep. Call it Halving, and when the vote is tied, let it predict junk.

Why the name? Think about what a mistake means for a voting learner. If Halving is wrong, the majority was wrong, and every hypothesis in the majority leaves the version space. At least half of the survivors are gone in one round. That is the whole idea, and it is worth seeing with numbers before any symbols.

Before reading the next table, try to play adversary yourself. Against Halving on H1\mathcal H_1H1​, what should the first message be? Sending 1000 again is a waste: three survivors say keep and one says junk, so Halving predicts keep, and calling it keep is no mistake, while calling it junk is a mistake that leaves only hFh_FhF​. The adversary wants a tie, so that whatever it says, it throws out only half.

Proof. Take any realizable sequence and suppose Halving errs on round ttt. You don’t have to trust either table to show the worst case. Python can play every possible adversary against each learner, trying every message and both labels in every round, and report the most mistakes any of them can force. That is MA(H1)M_A(\mathcal H_1)MA​(H1​) itself, the supremum, computed by brute force:

from functools import lru_cache
from itertools import product

X, H = list(product([0, 1], repeat=4)), "FLUN"
consistent = lambda V, x: x[H.index(min(V, key=H.index))]
halving = lambda V, x: int(2 * sum(x[H.index(h)] for h in V)
                           >= len(V))

@lru_cache(None)
def worst(V, learn):
    games = [(learn(V, x) != y) + worst(W, learn)
             for x in X for y in (0, 1)
             for W in [frozenset(h for h in V
                                 if x[H.index(h)] == y)]
             if W and W != V]
    return max(games, default=0)

print(worst(frozenset(H), consistent),
      worst(frozenset(H), halving))

It prints 3 2. The function worst is the adversary: from version space V, it tries each message x and each label y that keeps some hypothesis alive (if W), adds one if the learner got that round wrong, and recurses. Rounds where every survivor agrees teach nothing and cost nothing, so it skips them (W != V). The max is the sup⁡\supsup of the definition.

Notice something that separates the online world from the batch world. In lesson 2, any consistent hypothesis was a fine output. Online, choosing an arbitrary consistent hypothesis can cost exponentially more mistakes than choosing well.

The adversary’s plan is a tree

Halving’s bound depends on ∣H∣|\mathcal H|∣H∣, the number of hypotheses. Lesson 6a taught us to distrust counts: the class of thresholds is infinite, yet it is learnable from a few labels. Is there a better measure of how hard a class is online? To find it, look at the game from the adversary’s side.

The adversary wants to force a mistake on every one of the first few rounds. That part is easy: it waits for the prediction and announces the opposite label. The hard part is staying honest, so that at the end some hypothesis in H\mathcal HH agrees with every label it announced. Which message should it send on round 2? That depends on what happened on round 1, which depends on the learner. So the adversary’s plan is not a list of messages. It is a branching plan: “send v1v_1v1​ first; if the label turns out 0, send this next; if 1, send that.”

A branching plan is a binary tree. Each node holds a message. The adversary starts at the top node, the root, and sends its message. When the label is revealed, it moves to the left child if the label was 0 and to the right child if it was 1, and sends that node’s message next. The depth of the tree is the number of steps along a path from the root to the bottom. A tree in which every path has the same depth ddd and every node above the bottom has two children is complete, and it has 2d−12^d - 12d−1 nodes that hold messages: 1 for depth 1, 3 for depth 2.

Here is one for H1\mathcal H_1H1​, of depth 2. The root holds 1100, on which hFh_FhF​ and hLh_LhL​ say junk, hUh_UhU​ and hNh_NhN​ keep. Its left child, used after the label 0, holds 0010: the survivors are then hUh_UhU​ and hNh_NhN​, and 0010 splits them. Its right child, used after the label 1, holds 1000: the survivors are hFh_FhF​ and hLh_LhL​, and 1000 splits them. Every one of the four label paths (0 then 0, 0 then 1, 1 then 0, 1 then 1) is followed by exactly one hypothesis. So whatever the learner predicts, the adversary can announce the opposite label twice and still have a hypothesis that agrees. That is the depth-2 game from the Halving table, drawn as a plan.

So the messages are v1,v2,…,v2d−1v_1, v_2, \dots, v_{2^d - 1}v1​,v2​,…,v2d−1​. If iti_tit​ is the node used on round ttt, then i1=1i_1 = 1i1​=1 and it+1=2it+yti_{t+1} = 2 i_t + y_tit+1​=2it​+yt​: double to go down a level, and add the label to go right on a 1. That last check is the property we need, and it gets a name.

Definition (shattered tree). A complete binary tree of depth ddd with messages v1,…,v2d−1v_1, \dots, v_{2^d - 1}v1​,…,v2d−1​ is shattered by H\mathcal HH if for every sequence of labels (y1,…,yd)∈{0,1}d(y_1, \dots, y_d) \in \{0, 1\}^d(y1​,…,yd​)∈{0,1}d there is some h∈Hh \in \mathcal Hh∈H with h(vit)=yth(v_{i_t}) = y_th(vit​​)=yt​ for every t∈[d]t \in [d]t∈[d], where iti_tit​ is the node the labels lead to.

In plain words: whichever way the labels go down the tree, some hypothesis in the class agrees with all of them. The word is borrowed from lesson 6a, where a set of messages was shattered when the class could label it in every possible way; here it is a tree instead of a set, so later messages may depend on earlier labels.

Definition (Littlestone dimension). The Littlestone dimension of H\mathcal HH, written Ldim(H)\mathrm{Ldim}(\mathcal H)Ldim(H), is the largest depth ddd of a tree shattered by H\mathcal HH. If there are shattered trees of every depth, Ldim(H)=∞\mathrm{Ldim}(\mathcal H) = \inftyLdim(H)=∞.

The tree of depth 0 has no nodes, and the one label sequence of length 0 is matched by any hypothesis at all, so every nonempty class has Ldim≥0\mathrm{Ldim} \ge 0Ldim≥0. Our tree shows Ldim(H1)≥2\mathrm{Ldim}(\mathcal H_1) \ge 2Ldim(H1​)≥2.

No learner beats the tree

The first fact about the Littlestone dimension is the one the adversary’s story already told us.

Lemma (the tree lower bound). For every online learner AAA, MA(H)≥Ldim(H)M_A(\mathcal H) \ge \mathrm{Ldim}(\mathcal H)MA​(H)≥Ldim(H).

Proof. Let T=Ldim(H)T = \mathrm{Ldim}(\mathcal H)T=Ldim(H), assumed finite for now, and take a tree of depth TTT shattered by H\mathcal HH. The adversary walks it: on round ttt it sends xt=vitx_t = v_{i_t}xt​=vit​​, waits for ptp_tpt​, and announces yt=1−pty_t = 1 - p_tyt​=1−pt​. Every one of the TTT rounds is a mistake.

Is the sequence realizable? The labels (y1,…,yT)(y_1, \dots, y_T)(y1​,…,yT​) form one particular path, and the tree is shattered, so some h∈Hh \in \mathcal Hh∈H has h(vit)=yth(v_{i_t}) = y_th(vit​​)=yt​ for every ttt. That hhh is a legitimate truth. So this realizable sequence costs AAA exactly TTT mistakes, and the supremum MA(H)M_A(\mathcal H)MA​(H) is at least TTT. If Ldim(H)=∞\mathrm{Ldim}(\mathcal H) = \inftyLdim(H)=∞, run the same argument with a shattered tree of each depth TTT, so MA(H)≥TM_A(\mathcal H) \ge TMA​(H)≥T for every TTT, which means MA(H)=∞M_A(\mathcal H) = \inftyMA​(H)=∞. ■\blacksquare■

The learner can be as clever as it likes; it can even know the tree. It does not help, because the adversary moves after the learner has predicted.

Three classes, three dimensions

Let’s compute the dimension of a few classes, because the answers are surprising.

A finite class. A tree of depth ddd has 2d2^d2d label paths, and each path needs its own hypothesis (two different paths disagree on some node they both visit, so no hypothesis follows both). So a shattered tree of depth ddd needs ∣H∣≥2d|\mathcal H| \ge 2^d∣H∣≥2d, that is, Ldim(H)≤log⁡2∣H∣\mathrm{Ldim}(\mathcal H) \le \log_2 |\mathcal H|Ldim(H)≤log2​∣H∣. The tree lower bound and Halving’s bound give the same thing from the other side: Ldim(H)≤MHalving(H)≤log⁡2∣H∣\mathrm{Ldim}(\mathcal H) \le M_{\text{Halving}}(\mathcal H) \le \log_2 |\mathcal H|Ldim(H)≤MHalving​(H)≤log2​∣H∣.

One rogue sender. Suppose the junk this week comes from a single sender, and you don’t know which. Number the possible senders 1,…,d1, \dots, d1,…,d, let a message be just its sender, and let hjh_jhj​ say junk exactly when the sender is jjj. This is the class of singletons, with ddd hypotheses, so log⁡2d\log_2 dlog2​d can be large. Yet its Littlestone dimension is 1. A tree of depth 1 is just a root: send sender 1, and h1h_1h1​ says junk while h2h_2h2​ says keep, so depth 1 is shattered. For depth 2, the root holds some sender jjj; on the label 1 branch, only hjh_jhj​ is left, and one hypothesis cannot be split, so no depth-2 tree is shattered. The dimension can be far below log⁡2∣H∣\log_2 |\mathcal H|log2​∣H∣.

Thresholds on a score. Lesson 6a met thresholds on a spam score in [0,1][0, 1][0,1]: for each cut-off θ\thetaθ (“theta”), the hypothesis hθ(x)=1[x>θ]h_\theta(x) = \mathbb 1[x > \theta]hθ​(x)=1[x>θ] says junk when the score xxx is above θ\thetaθ. (The indicator 1[⋅]\mathbb 1[\cdot]1[⋅] is 1 when the statement inside is true and 0 when it is false, so h0.5(0.7)=1h_{0.5}(0.7) = 1h0.5​(0.7)=1, junk, and h0.5(0.2)=0h_{0.5}(0.2) = 0h0.5​(0.2)=0, keep.) Its VC dimension is 1, so in the batch world it is learnable from a handful of labels. Online, the adversary plays binary search. It sends score 1/21/21/2. Whatever the learner predicts, it announces the opposite. If it said junk, the threshold must be below 1/21/21/2, so it next sends 1/41/41/4; if keep, the threshold is at least 1/21/21/2, so it sends 3/43/43/4. And so on, halving the interval of thresholds that agree with everything so far. Every round is a mistake, and because the interval never becomes empty, some threshold agrees with all the labels, at every depth. So Ldim=∞\mathrm{Ldim} = \inftyLdim=∞, and no online learner has a mistake bound for thresholds.

from fractions import Fraction

def learner(x, lo, hi):
    return int(x > (2 * lo + hi) / 3)

lo, hi, stream, mistakes = Fraction(0), Fraction(1), [], 0

The learner here predicts with a threshold one third of the way into the current interval; swap in any rule you like. The exact fractions matter: with real numbers the binary search never runs out of room, and that is the density the proof uses.

Wow. A class that is easy for the batch filter is impossible for the online filter. The difference is that the batch filter got its examples at random, and an adversary is not random.

On a finite grid of scores the picture is in between. With 7 possible scores there are 8 thresholds and the dimension is 3; with 15 scores, 4. The next snippet computes Littlestone dimensions by brute force, using a recursion we prove in the next section; here it checks the three examples.

from functools import lru_cache
from itertools import product

@lru_cache(None)
def ldim(V):
    if not V:
        return -1
    n = len(next(iter(V)))
    splits = [[frozenset(h for h in V if h[x] == r)
               for r in (0, 1)] for x in range(n)]
    return max([1 + min(map(ldim, s)) for s in splits
                if all(s)], default=0)

singletons = {tuple(int(x == j) for x in range(8))
              for j in range(8)}
thresholds = lambda n: {tuple(int(x > t) for x in range(n))
                        for t in range(-1, n)}

      ldim(frozenset(thresholds(7))),

The dimension splits at the root

To build the best learner, we need one structural fact. Pick a message xxx and split a class VVV by what its hypotheses say on xxx:

V(r)={h∈V:h(x)=r},r∈{0,1}.V^{(r)} = \{ h \in V : h(x) = r \}, \qquad r \in \{0, 1\}.V(r)={h∈V:h(x)=r},r∈{0,1}.
  • V(1)V^{(1)}V(1) is the part of VVV that says junk on xxx, and V(0)V^{(0)}V(0) the part that says keep. The superscript (r)(r)(r) names the label, not a power.

In plain words: split the class into the hypotheses that call xxx junk and those that call it keep. For H1\mathcal H_1H1​ and x=1100x = 1100x=1100: V(1)={hF,hL}V^{(1)} = \{h_F, h_L\}V(1)={hF​,hL​} and V(0)={hU,hN}V^{(0)} = \{h_U, h_N\}V(0)={hU​,hN​}.

Now look at a shattered tree with root xxx. Every label path starts with y1y_1y1​. The paths that start with 0 go into the left subtree, and they must be matched by hypotheses that also say 0 on xxx, that is, by V(0)V^{(0)}V(0). So the left subtree is a tree of depth d−1d - 1d−1 shattered by V(0)V^{(0)}V(0), and the right subtree is one shattered by V(1)V^{(1)}V(1). The converse holds too: put any message xxx at a root, hang a tree shattered by V(0)V^{(0)}V(0) on the left and one shattered by V(1)V^{(1)}V(1) on the right, both of depth d−1d - 1d−1, and the result is a tree of depth ddd shattered by VVV. (If the two subtrees have different depths, cut the deeper one down: the top part of a shattered tree is still shattered.)

Lemma (the root split). For any class VVV with Ldim(V)≥1\mathrm{Ldim}(V) \ge 1Ldim(V)≥1,

Ldim(V)=max⁡x  (1+min⁡{Ldim(V(0)),Ldim(V(1))}),\mathrm{Ldim}(V) = \max_{x} \; \Big( 1 + \min\big\{ \mathrm{Ldim}(V^{(0)}), \mathrm{Ldim}(V^{(1)}) \big\} \Big),Ldim(V)=xmax​(1+min{Ldim(V(0)),Ldim(V(1))}),

where the maximum runs over messages xxx on which both parts are nonempty, and V(0),V(1)V^{(0)}, V^{(1)}V(0),V(1) are the parts for that xxx.

  • min⁡{⋅,⋅}\min\{\cdot, \cdot\}min{⋅,⋅} takes the smaller of the two dimensions: a tree of depth ddd needs both subtrees to have depth d−1d - 1d−1, so the weaker side decides.
  • The 1+1 +1+ counts the root.
  • max⁡x\max_xmaxx​ lets the adversary choose the best root.

In plain words: the deepest tree puts at its root the message that splits the class into two parts that are both as rich as possible, and is one deeper than the poorer part. For H1\mathcal H_1H1​ and root 1100, both parts have two hypotheses and dimension 1, so the formula gives 1+1=21 + 1 = 21+1=2. The ldim function above is this lemma, word for word: splits lists (V(0),V(1))(V^{(0)}, V^{(1)})(V(0),V(1)) for every message, if all(s) keeps those where both are nonempty, and 1 + min(map(ldim, s)) is the bracket. (It also returns 0 when no message splits the class and −1-1−1 for the empty class, a convention that makes the next section’s comparisons work.)

Proof. The paragraph before the lemma, in both directions: a shattered tree of depth d≥1d \ge 1d≥1 with root xxx makes the bracket for xxx at least ddd, and a bracket equal to ddd for some xxx builds a shattered tree of depth ddd under the root xxx. ■\blacksquare■

One more fact we will use: a subset can only lose dimension. If V′⊆VV' \subseteq VV′⊆V, every tree shattered by V′V'V′ is shattered by VVV, so Ldim(V′)≤Ldim(V)\mathrm{Ldim}(V') \le \mathrm{Ldim}(V)Ldim(V′)≤Ldim(V).

The Standard Optimal Algorithm

Halving splits the version space into the part that says junk and the part that says keep, and predicts with the bigger part. Bigger meant more hypotheses. What if “bigger” meant “higher Littlestone dimension” instead? That is the whole change, and the learner it gives is called the Standard Optimal Algorithm, SOA for short.

SOA(H):
  V_1 ← H
  for t = 1, 2, ...:
    receive x_t
    for r in {0, 1}: V_t^(r) ← {h in V_t : h(x_t) = r}
    predict p_t ← argmax over r of Ldim(V_t^(r))   (ties: 1)
    receive y_t
    V_{t+1} ← V_t^(y_t)

Why should this help? Halving fights the number of survivors. But the number is not what the adversary needs; it needs a deep shattered tree. SOA fights the dimension directly: it always predicts with the side that would leave the adversary the richer game, so that if it is wrong, the adversary is left with the poorer one.

Lemma (SOA’s mistake bound). MSOA(H)≤Ldim(H)M_{\text{SOA}}(\mathcal H) \le \mathrm{Ldim}(\mathcal H)MSOA​(H)≤Ldim(H).

Proof. It is enough to show that every mistake lowers the dimension of the version space by at least one: Ldim(Vt+1)≤Ldim(Vt)−1\mathrm{Ldim}(V_{t+1}) \le \mathrm{Ldim}(V_t) - 1Ldim(Vt+1​)≤Ldim(Vt​)−1 whenever pt≠ytp_t \neq y_tpt​=yt​. Since h⋆h^\starh⋆ is always in the version space, the dimension never drops below 0, so it can drop at most Ldim(H)\mathrm{Ldim}(\mathcal H)Ldim(H) times.

Suppose round ttt is a mistake, and suppose for contradiction that the dimension did not drop. Write d=Ldim(Vt)d = \mathrm{Ldim}(V_t)d=Ldim(Vt​). Since Vt+1=Vt(yt)⊆VtV_{t+1} = V_t^{(y_t)} \subseteq V_tVt+1​=Vt(yt​)​⊆Vt​, its dimension is at most ddd; not dropping means Ldim(Vt(yt))=d\mathrm{Ldim}(V_t^{(y_t)}) = dLdim(Vt(yt​)​)=d. SOA predicted ptp_tpt​ because its side had the larger (or tied) dimension, so Ldim(Vt(pt))≥Ldim(Vt(yt))=d\mathrm{Ldim}(V_t^{(p_t)}) \ge \mathrm{Ldim}(V_t^{(y_t)}) = dLdim(Vt(pt​)​)≥Ldim(Vt(yt​)​)=d, and it is also at most ddd as a subset. So both parts have dimension exactly ddd, and ptp_tpt​ and yty_tyt​ are the two different labels, so these are Vt(0)V_t^{(0)}Vt(0)​ and Vt(1)V_t^{(1)}Vt(1)​.

Now use the root split with root xtx_txt​: both parts are nonempty (they have dimension d≥0d \ge 0d≥0), so Ldim(Vt)≥1+min⁡{d,d}=d+1\mathrm{Ldim}(V_t) \ge 1 + \min\{d, d\} = d + 1Ldim(Vt​)≥1+min{d,d}=d+1. That contradicts Ldim(Vt)=d\mathrm{Ldim}(V_t) = dLdim(Vt​)=d. ■\blacksquare■

Put the two lemmas side by side. Every learner can be forced into Ldim(H)\mathrm{Ldim}(\mathcal H)Ldim(H) mistakes, and SOA never makes more.

Theorem (the Littlestone dimension characterizes online learnability). For every class H\mathcal HH, MSOA(H)=Ldim(H)M_{\text{SOA}}(\mathcal H) = \mathrm{Ldim}(\mathcal H)MSOA​(H)=Ldim(H), and no online learner AAA has MA(H)<Ldim(H)M_A(\mathcal H) < \mathrm{Ldim}(\mathcal H)MA​(H)<Ldim(H). In particular, H\mathcal HH is online learnable exactly when Ldim(H)<∞\mathrm{Ldim}(\mathcal H) < \inftyLdim(H)<∞.

Does SOA ever beat Halving in practice? Here is a class built to show it. Take eight messages, x0,u,v,s1,…,s5x_0, u, v, s_1, \dots, s_5x0​,u,v,s1​,…,s5​. Part A of the class is five hypotheses, “junk exactly when the message is sjs_jsj​”, one per jjj: singletons, dimension 1. Part B is four hypotheses that say junk on x0x_0x0​, keep on every sjs_jsj​, and every one of the four possible label pairs on uuu and vvv: dimension 2. When x0x_0x0​ arrives, part A (five hypotheses) says keep and part B (four) says junk. Halving counts heads and predicts keep; the adversary says junk, and is left with part B and two more forced mistakes. SOA sees that part B has the higher dimension and predicts junk; if the adversary says keep, it is left with the singletons and can force only one more mistake.

from functools import lru_cache

A = [(0, 0, 0) + tuple(int(i == j) for i in range(5))
     for j in range(5)]
B = [(1, u, v) + (0,) * 5 for u in (0, 1) for v in (0, 1)]
part = lambda V, x, r: frozenset(h for h in V if h[x] == r)
moves = lambda V: [(x, y, W) for x in range(8) for y in (0, 1)
                   for W in [part(V, x, y)] if W and W != V]

@lru_cache(None)
def ldim(V):
    return max([1 + min(ldim(W), ldim(V - W))
                for x, y, W in moves(V)], default=0)

@lru_cache(None)
def worst(V, learn):
    return max([(learn(V, x) != y) + worst(W, learn)
                for x, y, W in moves(V)], default=0)

big = lambda f, V, x: int(f(part(V, x, 1)) >= f(part(V, x, 0)))
halving = lambda V, x: big(len, V, x)
soa = lambda V, x: big(ldim, V, x)
H = frozenset(A + B)
print(len(H), ldim(H), worst(H, halving), worst(H, soa))

It prints 9 2 3 2: nine hypotheses, dimension 2, the best adversary forces 3 mistakes on Halving and only 2 on SOA. The only difference between the two learners is the function big compares the parts with: len for Halving, ldim for SOA.

A word on cost, since we promised to ignore it and should be honest about what we ignored. SOA needs the Littlestone dimension of two sets on every round, and computing it by the root split recursion visits a game tree that can be exponentially large. SOA is the optimal learner in mistakes, not in time.

Littlestone dimension against VC dimension

Thresholds showed that online learning can be much harder than batch learning. Is it ever easier? No, and the proof is a picture.

Theorem. For every class H\mathcal HH, VCdim(H)≤Ldim(H)\mathrm{VCdim}(\mathcal H) \le \mathrm{Ldim}(\mathcal H)VCdim(H)≤Ldim(H), and the gap can be arbitrarily large.

Proof. Recall from lesson 6a that a set {x1,…,xd}\{x_1, \dots, x_d\}{x1​,…,xd​} is shattered by H\mathcal HH when every one of the 2d2^d2d labellings of it is given by some hypothesis, and VCdim(H)\mathrm{VCdim}(\mathcal H)VCdim(H) is the largest such ddd. Take a shattered set of size ddd and build a complete tree of depth ddd whose nodes on level iii all hold xix_ixi​. Whatever the label path (y1,…,yd)(y_1, \dots, y_d)(y1​,…,yd​), the messages visited are x1,…,xdx_1, \dots, x_dx1​,…,xd​ in order, and the set is shattered, so some hhh gives h(xi)=yih(x_i) = y_ih(xi​)=yi​ for all iii. The tree is shattered, so Ldim(H)≥d\mathrm{Ldim}(\mathcal H) \ge dLdim(H)≥d. For the gap: thresholds on [0,1][0, 1][0,1] have VC dimension 1 and Littlestone dimension ∞\infty∞. ■\blacksquare■

For H1\mathcal H_1H1​: the set {1100,1010}\{1100, 1010\}{1100,1010} is shattered, since hF,hL,hU,hNh_F, h_L, h_U, h_NhF​,hL​,hU​,hN​ give it the labels (1, 1), (1, 0), (0, 1), (0, 0). The tree with 1100 at the root and 1010 at both children is then shattered.

The VC dimension asks for a tree whose messages may not depend on the labels; the Littlestone dimension lets them depend on the labels, which is exactly the extra power an adversary has.

The learners as code

Here are the three learners of this lesson side by side, with what each costs per round.

Consistent(H):
  V ← H
  each round: receive x; h ← any member of V; predict h(x)
              receive y; V ← {h in V : h(x) = y}

Halving(H):
  V ← H
  each round: receive x; predict 1 if at least half of V says 1,
              else 0; receive y; V ← {h in V : h(x) = y}

SOA(H):
  V ← H
  each round: receive x; split V into V^(0), V^(1) by h(x);
              predict the r with larger Ldim(V^(r)) (ties: 1)
              receive y; V ← V^(y)

Consistent and Halving keep the version space: O(∣H∣)O(|\mathcal H|)O(∣H∣) space, and O(∣H∣)O(|\mathcal H|)O(∣H∣) hypothesis evaluations per round, so O(T∣H∣)O(T|\mathcal H|)O(T∣H∣) in total. For lesson 1’s ten one-feature hypotheses that is nothing; for a class with millions of members it is already heavy, and for an infinite class, like the thresholds, you cannot even write the version space down as a list. SOA keeps the same version space, and on top of that needs the Littlestone dimension of two classes per round, a game-tree search that can grow exponentially with ∣H∣|\mathcal H|∣H∣ and the number of messages. All three are learners you would run on a small class to know the truth about it, not filters you would run on millions of messages a day. The cheap ones come in lesson 21b.

Fix the bug

Back to the spammers. You now have learners whose promises do not depend on where the mail comes from, and the lab below puts them against an adversary that plays as well as it can: in every round it computes the message and label that hurt your learner most, while keeping the labels honest, so that at the end some hypothesis in the week’s class agrees with all of them.

Each week has a class and a mistake budget: the most mistakes the filter may make, ever, however long the week runs. A filter that happens to do well against a lazy adversary earns nothing; the adversary here is not lazy, and the proved bound is what ships.

What people get wrong

“Online learning is batch learning done one example at a time.” The difference is not the order of the examples but who chooses them. Lesson 14’s SGD also sees one example per step, but its examples are fresh draws from D\mathcal DD. Online learning assumes nothing about where they come from, and that is why thresholds, easy in the batch world, become impossible online.

“Any consistent hypothesis is a fine choice.” It was in lesson 2. Online, Consistent can be forced into ∣H∣−1|\mathcal H| - 1∣H∣−1 mistakes while Halving makes at most log⁡2∣H∣\log_2|\mathcal H|log2​∣H∣. Which survivor you follow matters exponentially.

“The Littlestone dimension is just the VC dimension with a different name.” It is at least the VC dimension and can be infinitely larger. The VC dimension asks for one set of messages that every labelling fits; the Littlestone dimension lets each next message depend on the labels so far, which is exactly the power of an adversary.

Recap and the next bug

  1. In online learning, messages arrive one at a time, chosen by an adversary; the learner predicts, sees the label, and updates. With no restriction the adversary forces a mistake on every round.
  2. Under realizability, a learner is judged by its mistake bound MA(H)M_A(\mathcal H)MA​(H). Consistent makes at most ∣H∣−1|\mathcal H| - 1∣H∣−1 mistakes and Halving at most log⁡2∣H∣\log_2 |\mathcal H|log2​∣H∣, because each of its mistakes halves the version space.
  3. The adversary’s strategy is a binary tree of messages. The Littlestone dimension is the depth of the deepest tree the class shatters. Every learner can be forced into Ldim(H)\mathrm{Ldim}(\mathcal H)Ldim(H) mistakes, and SOA, which predicts with the side of higher dimension, never makes more. So a class is online learnable exactly when its Littlestone dimension is finite.
  4. The Littlestone dimension is always at least the VC dimension, and thresholds show the gap can be infinite: a class that is easy in the batch world can be impossible online.

The filter ships with a proved mistake bound, and for a few days the spammers get nowhere. Then, on Wednesday morning, a user reports a newsletter she subscribed to, a plain daytime message from a sender she knows, as junk. An hour later her colleague receives the very same newsletter and keeps it. One message, two labels. No hypothesis in any class can agree with both, so the version space is now empty, and every learner in this lesson was built on the promise that it never would be. What does Halving do when there is no one left to vote? And what can any filter promise once no hypothesis is perfect? That is lesson 21b.
