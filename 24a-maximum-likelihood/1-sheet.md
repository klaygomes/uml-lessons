# Generative Models and Maximum Likelihood

Terms:
- generative approach: assume \(\mathcal D\) has a parametric form and estimate its parameters (parametric density estimation); discriminative: learn a hypothesis directly.
- likelihood \(P_\theta[S]\); log-likelihood \(L(S;\theta) = \sum_i \log P_\theta[x_i]\); MLE \(\hat\theta \in \operatorname{argmax}_\theta L(S;\theta)\).
- log loss \(\ell(\theta, x) = -\log P_\theta[x]\); true risk \(= D_{\mathrm{RE}}(P\|P_\theta) + H(P)\).
- add-\(\alpha\) estimator \(\hat\theta_\alpha = (k+\alpha)/(m+2\alpha)\); \(\alpha = 1\) is Laplace’s rule.
- naive Bayes assumption \(\mathbb P[X = x \mid Y = y] = \prod_j \mathbb P[X_j = x_j \mid Y = y]\); \(2d + 1\) parameters.
- prior, posterior, predictive probability \(\mathbb P[X = x \mid S] = \sum_\theta \mathbb P[X = x\mid\theta]\mathbb P[\theta\mid S]\), MAP.

Misconception: “a probability of 0 is just a small probability”. Why wrong: in a product it vetoes all other evidence and in a log it is \(-\infty\); MLE produces it with probability \(\ge 1/2\) for \(m \le \log 2/(2\theta^\star)\).
Second: “smoothing is a hack”. Why wrong: add-\(\alpha\) is regularized MLE, add-one is the uniform-prior predictive, and it has a theorem.
Third: “more pseudo-counts are always safer”. Why wrong: bias \(\alpha/(m+2\alpha)\); \(\alpha = 20\) fails the accuracy certificate at 600 labels.
Fourth: “naive Bayes assumes the words are independent”. Why wrong: independent given the label.
Fifth: “MLE is not ERM”. Why wrong: it is ERM with the log loss.

Derivation:
- Bernoulli MLE: likelihood \(\theta^k(1-\theta)^{m-k}\), log, derivative \(k/\theta - (m-k)/(1-\theta)\), unique stationary point, negative second derivative, edge cases \(k = 0, m\) with \(0\log 0 = 0\). Unbiasedness by linearity; Hoeffding (lesson 4) solved for \(\varepsilon\): equation 24.2.
- Gaussian MLE: decomposition \(\sum(x_i - \mu)^2 = \sum(x_i - \hat\mu)^2 + m(\hat\mu - \mu)^2\) (cross term 0), then \(g'(\sigma) = (A - m\sigma^2)/\sigma^3\) sign change; degenerate case \(A = 0\) has no maximum. (The book only sets derivatives to zero.)
- MLE = ERM with log loss; true risk = \(D_{\mathrm{RE}} + H\); \(D_{\mathrm{RE}} \ge 0\) with equality iff equal, via \(\log t \le t - 1\) (ours; the book states it). Lesson 24b reuses this inequality in the EM lemma.
- Gaussian mean excess = \(\frac12(\hat\mu - \mu^\star)^2\) (Equation 24.6 line by line); expectation \(1/(2m)\) by the variance of an average; Chebyshev gives \(1/(2m\delta)\) with probability \(1 - \delta\).
- Rare-word failure: \((1-\theta)^m \ge e^{-2\theta m}\) for \(\theta \le 1/2\) via \(h(\theta) = \log(1-\theta) + 2\theta\); risk ∞.
- Pseudo-count theorem (book Exercise 2, worked in the prose with an add-\(\alpha\) generalization): (a) bias \(\alpha(1-2\theta^\star)/(m+2\alpha)\), fluctuation \(\frac{m}{m+2\alpha}(\bar x - \theta^\star)\), Hoeffding, triangle inequality; (b) \(\Delta(q) = D_{\mathrm{RE}}(\theta^\star\|q) \le (q-p)^2/(q(1-q))\) by convexity and the tangent inequality, \(q(1-q) \ge \alpha(m+\alpha)/(m+2\alpha)^2\), bias-variance split, \(\operatorname{Var}[k] = m\theta(1-\theta)\); (c) MLE ∞ with probability \((1-\theta^\star)^m\).
- Regularized objective of Exercise 2 (with weight \(\alpha/m\)) = pseudo-count likelihood; its minimizer by the Bernoulli proof with real-valued counts.
- Bayesian: marginal, predictive (total probability, conditional independence given θ), posterior (Bayes’ rule); \(I(a,b) = a!b!/(a+b+1)!\) by integration by parts; Laplace’s rule; MAP.
- Naive Bayes: \(2^d\) parameters, conditional independence, Bayes’ rule without the shared denominator (Equation 24.7), \(2d+1\) parameters; filter-level guarantee by the union bound over \(2d\) parameters with \(\delta/(2d)\).

End-of-chapter problem (§24.8, PDF p. 356). In the prose: Problem 1 = Exercise 1. Hints one rung at a time; never the solution.
- Problem 1 hint ladder:
  1. Expand \(\sum_i (x_i - \hat\mu)^2 = \sum_i (x_i - \mu)^2 - m(\hat\mu - \mu)^2\) (the decomposition from the Gaussian MLE proof, with \(\mu\) in place of a general centre).
  2. Take expectations: \(\mathbb E[(x_i - \mu)^2] = \sigma^2\).
  3. \(\mathbb E[(\hat\mu - \mu)^2] = \operatorname{Var}[\hat\mu] = \sigma^2/m\) (lesson 4’s variance of an average).
  4. So \(\mathbb E[\sum(x_i - \hat\mu)^2] = (m-1)\sigma^2\), and \(\mathbb E[\hat\sigma^2] = \frac{m-1}{m}\sigma^2\); the unbiased version divides by \(m - 1\). Note: the exercise says “the estimator of the variance”; \(\hat\sigma\) itself is also biased (Jensen), not required.
