The list that could grow forever

Your teammate has a pull request open, and she is excited about it. The filter, she argues, has been too timid. Every lesson so far has fixed a hypothesis class in advance and stayed inside it, and every real mistake she sees in the inbox would have been caught by one more test. So her change lets the filter learn a decision list: a chain of tests, read from the top, where each test looks at one feature of the message and, if that feature is present, hands out a label on the spot. If no test fires, a default label applies. Here is one:

if the sender is unknown:      junk
else if the subject says FREE: keep
else if it has a link:         junk
otherwise:                     keep

Her point is that nobody knows how long the right list is. Three tests? Five? Twelve? So her learner may use lists of any length, over any feature in the catalogue, and the catalogue keeps growing: the team adds a new feature most weeks. Whatever the junk looks like, some list will describe it.

The reviewer blocks the change, and for a reason you already know. Lists of any length can fit any labels at all on as many messages as you like. Recall from lesson 6a that a class shatters a set of messages when, for every way of labelling them, some hypothesis in the class produces exactly those labels, and that its VC dimension is the largest number of messages it can shatter. A class that can fit any labels on as many messages as you like has infinite VC dimension. Recall too that a class is PAC learnable (lesson 3) when one number of labels, fixed before you see any data, is enough to make the learner nearly as good as the best hypothesis in the class with high probability, whatever the mail stream looks like. The fundamental theorem of lesson 6a says flatly that a class with infinite VC dimension is not PAC learnable. No number of labels gives a guarantee that holds for every hypothesis in the class and every distribution. Rejected.

Your teammate is not wrong either, though, and that is the bug. Common sense says something the theorem does not seem to allow. A three-test list that fits a thousand labelled messages well deserves your trust. A forty-test list that fits thirty labelled messages perfectly deserves none. Short lists should be easy to trust, long ones should need more evidence, and nothing should be ruled out forever. The fundamental theorem gives one sample size for the whole class. What we want is a guarantee whose price depends on the hypothesis.

That is exactly what this lesson builds. By the end you will know how to split your confidence across list lengths, so that a short list is certified with few labels and a long one with many, and you will see exactly which classes this trick can learn and which it cannot.

Why tie the features a list may use to its size? Because without that, even one-test lists would be hopeless: a single test “feature jjj is present” can pick out any subset of kkk messages if the messages are allowed to carry many features, so one-test lists over an unbounded catalogue would already have infinite VC dimension. With the tie, every Hn\mathcal H_nHn​ is a finite class, and you can count it. Try n=1n = 1n=1 by hand. A list in H1\mathcal H_1H1​ has no tests, and then only a default, 0 or 1: two lists. How much can thirty, or three hundred, examples certify for each class? Lesson 4 answered that for a finite class, with two tools. Hoeffding’s inequality says that the average of mmm independent mistakes-or-not rarely strays far from its expected value, so for one fixed list the empirical risk is close to the true risk. The union bound says that the chance that at least one of several bad events happens is at most the sum of their chances, so the guarantee can be stretched to every list in the class at once, at the price of a factor ∣Hn∣|\mathcal H_n|∣Hn​∣. A confidence budget, shared out

Here is the way out. Look again at the gap formula. The δ\deltaδ in it is a confidence budget: the probability you allow for the guarantee to fail. Lesson 4 spent the whole budget on one class. Nothing forces you to do that. You can split it.

Say your budget is δ=0.05\delta = 0.05δ=0.05. Give size 1 a share of 0.03, size 2 a share of 0.0076, size 3 a share of 0.0034, and so on, shares that never add up to more than 0.05. In symbols, size nnn gets a share δn\delta_nδn​, with δ1+δ2+⋯≤δ\delta_1 + \delta_2 + \cdots \le \deltaδ1​+δ2​+⋯≤δ; the dots mean the sum goes on forever, one share per size. For each nnn separately, the finite-class bound with confidence δn\delta_nδn​ fails with probability at most δn\delta_nδn​. By the union bound, the probability that any of them fails is at most ∑nδn≤δ\sum_n \delta_n \le \delta∑n​δn​≤δ, where ∑n\sum_n∑n​ is the sum over every size nnn: the shares together, never more than the budget. So with probability at least 1−δ1 - \delta1−δ, all of them hold at once: every list of size nnn, for every nnn, is within its own gap of its true risk.

It is convenient to write each share as a fraction of the budget: size 3’s share of 0.0034 is about 6.8% of 0.05. So write δn=w(n)⋅δ\delta_n = w(n) \cdot \deltaδn​=w(n)⋅δ, where w(n)w(n)w(n) is the fraction of the budget that size nnn gets. The weight function w:N→[0,1]w: \mathbb N \to [0, 1]w:N→[0,1] takes a size nnn (the arrow reads “maps each element of N\mathbb NN to a number in”) and returns a number between 0 and 1, and the fractions must satisfy ∑nw(n)≤1\sum_n w(n) \le 1∑n​w(n)≤1. A weight says how much you believe in a size before seeing any data. If there were only finitely many sizes, say NNN of them, you could give each the same weight 1/N1/N1/N: no preference at all. With infinitely many sizes that is impossible (equal positive weights would sum to infinity), so you must lean towards some sizes. Two standard choices are

w(n)=6π2n2andw(n)=2−n.w(n) = \frac{6}{\pi^2 n^2} \qquad \text{and} \qquad w(n) = 2^{-n}.w(n)=π2n26​andw(n)=2−n.

Read the first from the inside out: n2n^2n2 is the size squared, so the weight falls off with the square of the size; π≈3.1416\pi \approx 3.1416π≈3.1416 is the circle constant, and 6/π2≈0.6086/\pi^2 \approx 0.6086/π2≈0.608 is just the scaling that makes the weights add up to exactly 1, because ∑n1/n2=1+1/4+1/9+⋯=π2/6\sum_n 1/n^2 = 1 + 1/4 + 1/9 + \cdots = \pi^2/6∑n​1/n2=1+1/4+1/9+⋯=π2/6. What people get wrong

“Nonuniform learnability means the class is learnable after all, just more slowly.” It is a weaker promise, not a slower version of the same one. A PAC guarantee tells you before you collect any data how many examples you need. A nonuniform guarantee tells you how many you need to compete with a particular hhh, and you do not know which hhh is best until you have learned. The fundamental theorem still stands: decision lists of any length are not PAC learnable.

“SRM picks the list with the smallest true risk.” It picks the list with the smallest certified bound. At 30 labels on our stream, SRM ships “everything is junk”, with true risk 0.375, while ERM on H3\mathcal H_3H3​ would have found lists with true risk 0.125 to 0.175. SRM is not being stupid: with 30 labels it cannot tell those lists from their 720 neighbours at cap 6, and it refuses to certify what it cannot check. A bound is a promise about the worst case, and it is only as tight as the evidence.

“Choose the weights to match the data.” Weights encode prior knowledge, and they must be fixed before the training sequence is seen. Weights tuned after looking at the labels void the union bound: the bad events BnB_nBn​ are then no longer the ones whose shares you budgeted, and the proof says nothing. Putting all the weight on size 3 is legal if you believed it in advance (and it ships a list with bound 0.1921 at 1,000 labels), but then a size-5 list can never ship, however much data arrives.
