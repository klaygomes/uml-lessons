The filter that would not sit still

Lesson 12 ended with an uneasy feeling. A user corrected one label, someone retrained the hinge filter out of habit, and the new weights looked nothing like the old ones. The bound was fine, because it never promised that the output would sit still. But the feeling turned into a bug report within the week.

The team now retrains every convex filter every night. The newest one scores messages from a marketing campaign, and it is the junk score of lesson 9: a weight vector w\mathbf ww, a score ⟨w,x⟩\langle \mathbf w, \mathbf x \rangle⟨w,x⟩, and the square loss, which lesson 12 showed is convex and smooth. Each night it is trained on the twenty most recent labelled campaign messages, so from one night to the next only a handful of labels change: three old ones drop out, three new ones come in.

Here is what the dashboard showed for one week. The weight on the word “free” went from 2.2 to −8.2, back to −2.2, then to −5.9. The weight on “offer” jumped from −0.3 to 11.8. And the production error, the filter’s average loss on fresh campaign messages, swung from 0.384 to 0.632 and back to 0.387 in three nights. Three labels out of twenty changed each night. Nobody would call that a big change in the data. Yet the learner’s output jumped around like a nervous bird, and its true quality jumped with it.

What is going on? The learner did exactly what it was told: it minimized the empirical risk, perfectly, every night. Nothing is broken in the optimization, and lesson 12 promised us the problem is convex. So why does a tiny change in the input make such a big change in the output, and why does that hurt the true risk? And the deeper question, the one lesson 12 left hanging: could a learner that refuses to jump around generalize for that very reason?

This lesson answers both. The fix is one extra term in the objective. The theory behind it is a new way to see that a learner does not overfit, one that never mentions uniform convergence, and lesson 13b turns it into a proof.

A week of retrains

Why would the data leave the split undecided? Think of the empirical risk as a landscape over the weights. Along some directions it rises steeply; along others it is nearly flat. For the half square loss, the curvature of LSL_SLS​ along a unit direction u\mathbf uu (the second derivative of t↦LS(w+tu)t \mapsto L_S(\mathbf w + t\mathbf u)t↦LS​(w+tu)) is

  • u\mathbf uu is a direction in weight space, with length 1; ⟨u,xi⟩\langle \mathbf u, \mathbf x_i \rangle⟨u,xi​⟩ is how much message iii’s score changes when you move the weights one unit along u\mathbf uu.
  • Squaring and averaging over the mmm training messages, 1m∑i=1m\frac1m \sum_{i=1}^mm1​∑i=1m​, gives the average squared change: the sum runs over i=1i = 1i=1 to mmm, one term per example.

In plain words: the curvature along a direction is the average squared effect that direction has on the training scores. A direction that barely changes any score, such as moving weight from “free” to “gift” when both words always appear together, has curvature near 0. For the five-word direction (1,1,1,1,1)/5(1, 1, 1, 1, 1)/\sqrt5(1,1,1,1,1)/5​, each message’s score moves by about 5×0.4/5≈0.895 \times 0.4/\sqrt5 \approx 0.895×0.4/5​≈0.89, whose square is about 0.8: close to the 0.814 the code finds below.

In the shuffle directions the empirical risk is a long, almost flat valley: moving from “all on free” to “all on bonus” barely changes the training fit. The bottom of a nearly flat valley is decided by whatever tiny tilt the jitter happens to give it, and three new examples change that tilt completely. So the minimizer slides from one end of the valley to the other.

Then why does production suffer? Because the valley is only nearly flat, and fresh messages have their own jitter. A weight vector with huge entries of opposite signs turns each fresh message’s jitter into a large, random swing of the score. That is the 0.0016 q0.0016\,q0.0016q term in the formula: on night 2, qqq is about 400, which alone adds about 0.3 to the true risk. The learner fitted the noise in the training jitter, and it paid in production. That is overfitting, seen from the inside.

Pay for the size: regularized loss minimization

How do you tell the learner to stop sliding along the valley? You could restrict the hypothesis class, as lesson 1 taught, and allow only weight vectors with small norm. Lesson 12 did exactly that with its bounded classes ∥w∥≤B\|\mathbf w\| \le B∥w∥≤B. Here is a softer version of the same idea: let the learner go anywhere, but charge it for the size of the weights.

Put a number on it. On night 2 the ERM weights had squared length ∑jwj2≈389\sum_j w_j^2 \approx 389∑j​wj2​≈389, while the best weights, 0.3 on every word, have squared length 5×0.09=0.455 \times 0.09 = 0.455×0.09=0.45. Suppose every unit of squared length cost the learner 0.1 on top of its training loss. Night 2’s weights would then pay about 38.9, a hundred times more than any training loss in this world, so the learner would never choose them; the best weights would pay only 0.045. The learner keeps minimizing a training score, but the score now includes a price for size.

Definition (regularized loss minimization). A regularization function is a map R:Rd→RR : \mathbb R^d \to \mathbb RR:Rd→R. The regularized loss minimization rule, RLM for short, returns a hypothesis in

argmin⁡w(LS(w)+R(w)).\operatorname*{argmin}_{\mathbf w} \left( L_S(\mathbf w) + R(\mathbf w) \right).wargmin​(LS​(w)+R(w)).

The regularization function measures, intuitively, how “complex” a hypothesis is, and RLM balances a low empirical risk against a “simpler” hypothesis. Many choices of RRR are possible, each reflecting some prior belief about the problem, much as the description language did for minimum description length in lesson 7. This lesson uses one of the simplest:

R(w)=λ∥w∥2,λ>0,∥w∥=∑i=1dwi2,R(\mathbf w) = \lambda \|\mathbf w\|^2, \qquad \lambda > 0, \qquad \|\mathbf w\| = \sqrt{\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^d w_i^2},R(w)=λ∥w∥2,λ>0,∥w∥=∑i=1d​wi2​​,

which gives the learning rule

Read it from the inside out.

  • ∥w∥2=∑j=1dwj2\|\mathbf w\|^2 = \sum_{j=1}^d w_j^2∥w∥2=∑j=1d​wj2​ is the squared length of the weight vector, and λ∥w∥2\lambda \|\mathbf w\|^2λ∥w∥2 is its price: λ\lambdaλ units of objective per unit of squared length.
  • LS(w)L_S(\mathbf w)LS​(w) is the empirical risk from lesson 1, now with a general loss: 1m∑i=1mℓ(w,zi)\frac1m \sum_{i=1}^m \ell(\mathbf w, z_i)m1​∑i=1m​ℓ(w,zi​), the average loss on the training sequence SSS.

There are two ways to read this rule, and both will pay off.

As structural risk minimization. Lesson 7 chose among nested classes by trading empirical risk against a complexity charge. Define H1⊂H2⊂H3⊂…\mathcal H_1 \subset \mathcal H_2 \subset \mathcal H_3 \subset \dotsH1​⊂H2​⊂H3​⊂… with Hi={w:∥w∥2≤i}\mathcal H_i = \{\mathbf w : \|\mathbf w\|^2 \le i\}Hi​={w:∥w∥2≤i}. If the number of examples each Hi\mathcal H_iHi​ needs grows with iii, then charging λ∥w∥2\lambda \|\mathbf w\|^2λ∥w∥2 is a smooth version of “pay more for a bigger class”, and RLM behaves like SRM over this sequence.

As a stabilizer. The penalty makes the minimizer sit still when the data move a little. That is the reading this lesson and lesson 13b develop, and it leads to a proof that the learner does not overfit. But first, let’s see the rule work on the campaign.

Ridge regression

Stable rules do not overfit

Let’s name what went wrong precisely. On night 2, ERM’s weights had an average loss of 0.202 on their own twenty training messages, and 0.632 on fresh messages: they looked three times better on the data they were fitted to than they really were. That difference is what overfitting means. A learner AAA overfits when the true risk of its output, LD(A(S))L_{\mathcal D}(A(S))LD​(A(S)), is much larger than its empirical risk, LS(A(S))L_S(A(S))LS​(A(S)). Throughout this lesson we look at the average over training sequences, the overfitting gap

where S∼DmS \sim \mathcal D^mS∼Dm reads “SSS is mmm examples drawn i.i.d. from D\mathcal DD” (lesson 2), and ES∼Dm\mathbb E_{S \sim \mathcal D^m}ES∼Dm​ averages over those draws. Our guarantees will be about expected risks, not “with probability at least 1−δ1 - \delta1−δ”. A problem at the end of lesson 13b shows how to turn one into the other.

Now, what does “a small change of the input” mean? Here is the change the nightly retrain makes, shrunk to its smallest: replace one example. Given a training sequence S=(z1,…,zm)S = (z_1, \dots, z_m)S=(z1​,…,zm​) and one more example z′z'z′, let

Read the pieces from the inside out.

  • S(i)S^{(i)}S(i): the superscript (i)(i)(i) names which position was replaced; everything else in the sequence is untouched. z′z'z′ is one more example drawn from the same D\mathcal DD.

Theorem (stable rules do not overfit). Let D\mathcal DD be a distribution, let S=(z1,…,zm)∼DmS = (z_1, \dots, z_m) \sim \mathcal D^mS=(z1​,…,zm​)∼Dm, let z′∼Dz' \sim \mathcal Dz′∼D be independent of SSS, and let iii be drawn uniformly from [m]={1,…,m}[m] = \{1, \dots, m\}[m]={1,…,m}, independently of both. Then for every learner AAA,

ES[LD(A(S))−LS(A(S))]=E(S,z′)∼Dm+1,  i∼U(m)[ℓ(A(S(i)),zi)−ℓ(A(S),zi)].\mathbb E_{S} \left[ L_{\mathcal D}(A(S)) - L_S(A(S)) \right] = \mathbb E_{(S, z') \sim \mathcal D^{m+1},\; i \sim U(m)} \left[ \ell(A(S^{(i)}), z_i) - \ell(A(S), z_i) \right].ES​[LD​(A(S))−LS​(A(S))]=E(S,z′)∼Dm+1,i∼U(m)​[ℓ(A(S(i)),zi​)−ℓ(A(S),zi​)].

We assume the learner is deterministic (a randomized learner can be handled by conditioning on its random bits) and that the expectations exist, which holds whenever the loss is bounded.

Read it from the inside out.

  • (S,z′)∼Dm+1(S, z') \sim \mathcal D^{m+1}(S,z′)∼Dm+1: the mmm training examples and the extra one are m+1m + 1m+1 independent draws from D\mathcal DD.
  • i∼U(m)i \sim U(m)i∼U(m): U(m)U(m)U(m) is the uniform distribution on [m][m][m], so each position is replaced with probability 1/m1/m1/m.
  • The expectation on the right averages the replace-one change over all three sources of randomness; the one on the left averages the true risk minus the empirical risk over SSS only.

In plain words: on average, a learner’s true risk exceeds its training fit by exactly as much as its loss on an example grows when that example is swapped out.

Proof. We compute the two expectations on the left separately, then subtract.

The true risk. Fix a training sequence SSS. Then A(S)A(S)A(S) is a fixed hypothesis, and because z′z'z′ is independent of SSS, conditioning on SSS leaves z′∼Dz' \sim \mathcal Dz′∼D. By the definition of the true risk, Ez′[ℓ(A(S),z′)]=LD(A(S))\mathbb E_{z'}[\ell(A(S), z')] = L_{\mathcal D}(A(S))Ez′​[ℓ(A(S),z′)]=LD​(A(S)). Averaging over SSS,

ES[LD(A(S))]=ES,z′[ℓ(A(S),z′)].\mathbb E_S[L_{\mathcal D}(A(S))] = \mathbb E_{S, z'}[\ell(A(S), z')].ES​[LD​(A(S))]=ES,z′​[ℓ(A(S),z′)].

The swap. The m+1m + 1m+1 examples (z1,…,zm,z′)(z_1, \dots, z_m, z')(z1​,…,zm​,z′) are i.i.d. draws from D\mathcal DD. The joint distribution of an i.i.d. sequence does not change when you exchange two of its positions, because it is a product of identical factors. Exchange position iii with the last position. The training sequence SSS becomes S(i)S^{(i)}S(i), and the extra example z′z'z′ becomes ziz_izi​. So the pair (S(i),zi)(S^{(i)}, z_i)(S(i),zi​) has exactly the same distribution as the pair (S,z′)(S, z')(S,z′), and therefore, for every fixed iii,

ES,z′[ℓ(A(S),z′)]=ES,z′[ℓ(A(S(i)),zi)].\mathbb E_{S, z'}[\ell(A(S), z')] = \mathbb E_{S, z'}[\ell(A(S^{(i)}), z_i)].ES,z′​[ℓ(A(S),z′)]=ES,z′​[ℓ(A(S(i)),zi​)].

Since this holds for every iii, it also holds on average over i∼U(m)i \sim U(m)i∼U(m): ES[LD(A(S))]=ES,z′,i[ℓ(A(S(i)),zi)]\mathbb E_S[L_{\mathcal D}(A(S))] = \mathbb E_{S, z', i}[\ell(A(S^{(i)}), z_i)]ES​[LD​(A(S))]=ES,z′,i​[ℓ(A(S(i)),zi​)].

The empirical risk. By definition LS(A(S))=1m∑i=1mℓ(A(S),zi)L_S(A(S)) = \frac1m \sum_{i=1}^m \ell(A(S), z_i)LS​(A(S))=m1​∑i=1m​ℓ(A(S),zi​), and an average over i∈[m]i \in [m]i∈[m] with weights 1/m1/m1/m is an expectation over i∼U(m)i \sim U(m)i∼U(m). Definition (on-average-replace-one stability). Let ε:N→R\varepsilon : \mathbb N \to \mathbb Rε:N→R be a monotonically decreasing function. A learner AAA is on-average-replace-one-stable with rate ε(m)\varepsilon(m)ε(m) if for every distribution D\mathcal DD,

E(S,z′)∼Dm+1,  i∼U(m)[ℓ(A(S(i)),zi)−ℓ(A(S),zi)]≤ε(m).\mathbb E_{(S, z') \sim \mathcal D^{m+1},\; i \sim U(m)} \left[ \ell(A(S^{(i)}), z_i) - \ell(A(S), z_i) \right] \le \varepsilon(m).E(S,z′)∼Dm+1,i∼U(m)​[ℓ(A(S(i)),zi​)−ℓ(A(S),zi​)]≤ε(m).

The rate plays the role of an accuracy, which is why it borrows the letter ε\varepsilonε. We will often just say stable. By the theorem, a learner stable with rate ε(m)\varepsilon(m)ε(m) has expected overfitting gap at most ε(m)\varepsilon(m)ε(m): stability and not overfitting are the same thing, on average.

In plain words: a learner is stable with rate ε(m)\varepsilon(m)ε(m) when, for every distribution, swapping out one of mmm examples costs it at most ε(m)\varepsilon(m)ε(m) of loss on that example, on average. “Monotonically decreasing” means the allowance never grows as mmm grows. For example, lesson 13b proves rates like 2ρ2/(λm)2\rho^2/(\lambda m)2ρ2/(λm); with ρ=0.4\rho = 0.4ρ=0.4, λ=0.05\lambda = 0.05λ=0.05 and m=20m = 20m=20 that is 2⋅0.16/1=0.322 \cdot 0.16/1 = 0.322⋅0.16/1=0.32, and doubling mmm halves it.

But not overfitting is not the same as learning. Take the learner that ignores its input and always returns w=0\mathbf w = \mathbf 0w=0. It is perfectly stable: A(S(i))=A(S)A(S^{(i)}) = A(S)A(S(i))=A(S), so the replace-one change is exactly 0, and so is the overfitting gap. In the campaign world it has true risk 0.5 every night, with no swings at all. A useful learner must do two things at once: fit the training sequence, meaning low empirical risk, and be stable. The regularization weight λ\lambdaλ is the dial between the two, and the fix at the end of this lesson is about setting that dial.

Fix the bug

Back to the campaign filter. Ridge with λ=0.1\lambda = 0.1λ=0.1 had a good week, but, as the ridge regression section admitted, “it worked this week” is not a guarantee. Before the filter goes into the nightly job for good, the manager wants two promises, and she wants them about the average night, not about any one night:

  1. The morning dashboard shows the training fit LS(A(S))L_S(A(S))LS​(A(S)). It should flatter the filter by at most 0.03 on average: an expected overfitting gap of at most 0.03.
  2. The expected production error should be at most 0.34.

Neither number can be read off the dashboard, because the production error needs the whole distribution. But the theorem hands you both. The gap is the expected replace-one change, and so the expected production error splits into two pieces you can measure on training data and swapped-in examples alone:

ES[LD(A(S))]=ES[LS(A(S))]+ES,z′,i[ℓ(A(S(i)),zi)−ℓ(A(S),zi)].\mathbb E_S[L_{\mathcal D}(A(S))] = \mathbb E_S[L_S(A(S))] + \mathbb E_{S, z', i}\left[ \ell(A(S^{(i)}), z_i) - \ell(A(S), z_i) \right].ES​[LD​(A(S))]=ES​[LS​(A(S))]+ES,z′,i​[ℓ(A(S(i)),zi​)−ℓ(A(S),zi​)].

  • On the left, LD(A(S))L_{\mathcal D}(A(S))LD​(A(S)) is the production error of the filter trained on SSS, and ES\mathbb E_SES​ averages it over training sequences: the expected production error.
  • The first term on the right, ES[LS(A(S))]\mathbb E_S[L_S(A(S))]ES​[LS​(A(S))], is the expected fit: the training loss of the learner’s own output, averaged over training sequences.
  • The second term is the expected replace-one change, averaged over SSS, the fresh example z′z'z′ and the position iii, exactly as in the theorem.
  • Why it holds: write LD(A(S))=LS(A(S))+(LD(A(S))−LS(A(S)))L_{\mathcal D}(A(S)) = L_S(A(S)) + (L_{\mathcal D}(A(S)) - L_S(A(S)))LD​(A(S))=LS​(A(S))+(LD​(A(S))−LS​(A(S))), take expectations, and let the theorem replace the expected gap by the expected replace-one change.

In plain words: the expected production error is the expected fit plus the expected replace-one change. For ERM in the campaign world, the fit averages about 0.240 and the replace-one change about 0.184; together 0.424, while the formula for the true risk, averaged over the same training sequences, gives 0.425. import random

def draw(rng):
    y = rng.choice((1, -1))
    f = .4 * y * rng.choice((1, 1, 1, 1, -1))
    return [f + rng.choice((.04, -.04)) for _ in range(5)], y

def train(S, lam):
    X, k = [x + [y] for x, y in S], 2 * lam * len(S)
    M = [[sum(r[i] * r[j] for r in X) + k * (i == j)
          for j in range(6)] for i in range(5)]
    for c in range(5):
        M[c] = [v / M[c][c] for v in M[c]]
        for r in set(range(5)) - {c}:
            M[r] = [v - M[r][c] * p for v, p in zip(M[r], M[c])]
    return [row[5] for row in M]

loss = lambda w, z: (sum(a * b for a, b in zip(w, z[0]))
                     - z[1]) ** 2 / 2

                 - .48 * sum(w) + 1) / 2

What people get wrong

“Regularization is about keeping weights small because small weights are good.” Small is not the goal. The penalty adds curvature 2λ2\lambda2λ in every direction, which matters in the flat directions the data cannot pin down and barely matters elsewhere. What you buy is stability, and stability is exactly a bound on overfitting.

“A stable learner is a good learner.” The constant learner that always returns 0\mathbf 00 is perfectly stable and has production error 0.5 in the campaign world. Stability bounds the gap between true and empirical risk; you still need a small empirical risk. A good learner fits and is stable.

“Stability is only a hint that a learner generalizes.” On average it is not a hint at all; it is an identity. In the one-word world the expected overfitting gap and the expected replace-one change both came out at 0.128, and in the campaign world the fit plus the replace-one change matched the production error to within the simulation’s noise. A learner overfits, on average, exactly as much as it is unstable.

  2. Regularized loss minimization minimizes LS(w)+R(w)L_S(\mathbf w) + R(\mathbf w)LS​(w)+R(w).
