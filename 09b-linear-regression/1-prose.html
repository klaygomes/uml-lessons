The score nobody can vouch for

Lesson 9a ended with the halfspace filter in production and the facilities team getting its lunch invitations again. It also ended with a request. The new inbox sorts messages by how suspicious they are, and for that it needs a junk score: the percentage of recipients who click “report junk” on a message. The inbox had a launch date, so a teammate shipped the score with a rule tuned by hand: “each free is worth 20 points”.

Monday brings the screenshots. A message with three “free”s shows a junk score of 60, and 70% of its recipients reported it. A message with one “free” shows 20, and only 10% did. Nothing is wildly off, but nothing is right either, and the product manager asks two questions you cannot answer yet. Is there a better line? And if you hand one over, can you prove that no line does better?

The teammate’s defence is honest: “I tried a hundred settings, and this one had the smallest average miss.” But a hundred settings out of infinitely many proves nothing about the hundred-and-first. You met this wall at the start of lesson 9a, where brute force over real-valued weights was hopeless. The way past it there was a certificate: a separator whose margin bounded the Perceptron’s updates, checked on the labelled messages before training started. This lesson finds the same kind of certificate for a score, a property you can check on your weights, using only the messages you have, that proves no other line has a smaller risk. Then, because the designers want it too, the same linear score will give a probability of junk.

A junk score: linear regression

Let’s say the product manager’s request in the course’s language. Each message comes with a number, its junk score, and the learner has to predict that number.

That is a regression problem: the label set is Y=R\mathcal Y = \mathbb RY=R, not {±1}\{\pm 1\}{±1}. Linear regression is a common tool for modelling how a real-valued outcome depends on some explanatory variables. Its hypothesis class is just the affine functions, with no sign on top:

Hreg=Ld={x↦⟨w,x⟩+b  :  w∈Rd, b∈R}.\mathcal H_{\text{reg}} = L_d = \{ \mathbf x \mapsto \langle \mathbf w, \mathbf x \rangle + b \; : \; \mathbf w \in \mathbb R^d, \, b \in \mathbb R \}.Hreg​=Ld​={x↦⟨w,x⟩+b:w∈Rd,b∈R}.

This is the same set LdL_dLd​ we read slowly in lesson 9a, now given the name Hreg\mathcal H_{\text{reg}}Hreg​, “the hypothesis class for regression”: every weighted sum plus a constant, used directly as the prediction.

The 0-1 loss is useless here. If a message’s true score is 30, then predicting 30.001 and predicting 90 are both “wrong”, but one is clearly better. So we need a loss that grows with the size of the miss. Take the teammate’s guess, “each free is worth 20 points”, h(x)=20xh(x) = 20xh(x)=20x. On the message with three “free”s it predicts 60 against a true 70, a miss of −10-10−10; squaring the miss gives a cost of 100, while a miss of 2 would cost only 4. That is the square loss you met in lesson 3,

ℓ(h,(x,y))=(h(x)−y)2.\ell(h, (\mathbf x, y)) = (h(\mathbf x) - y)^2.ℓ(h,(x,y))=(h(x)−y)2.

The letter ℓ\ellℓ names the loss; its inputs are the hypothesis hhh and one labelled example (x,y)(\mathbf x, y)(x,y). Inside out: h(x)h(\mathbf x)h(x) is the prediction, subtracting yyy gives the miss, and the exponent 2 squares it, so misses in either direction cost the same and big misses cost much more. In plain words: the cost of a prediction is the square of how far it missed. With it, recall from lesson 1 that LS(h)L_S(h)LS​(h) is the empirical risk of hhh on the training sequence SSS, the average loss over the examples; here it is the average squared miss,

LS(h)=1m∑i=1m(h(xi)−yi)2,L_S(h) = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^m (h(\mathbf x_i) - y_i)^2,LS​(h)=m1​i=1∑m​(h(xi​)−yi​)2,

often called the mean squared error. Inside out: one squared miss per example iii, the sum ∑i=1m\sum_{i=1}^m∑i=1m​ adds all mmm of them, and 1m\frac1mm1​ divides by how many there are. Least squares

Least squares is the algorithm that solves ERM for linear regression with the square loss. In homogeneous form (with the constant 1 inside x\mathbf xx), it looks for

argmin⁡wLS(hw)=argmin⁡w1m∑i=1m(⟨w,xi⟩−yi)2.\operatorname*{argmin}_{\mathbf w} L_S(h_{\mathbf w}) = \operatorname*{argmin}_{\mathbf w} \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^m (\langle \mathbf w, \mathbf x_i \rangle - y_i)^2.wargmin​LS​(hw​)=wargmin​m1​i=1∑m​(⟨w,xi​⟩−yi​)2.

The right side just writes LSL_SLS​ out, with the score ⟨w,xi⟩\langle \mathbf w, \mathbf x_i \rangle⟨w,xi​⟩ as the prediction. In plain words: find the weights whose line has the smallest average squared miss. With x=(1,x)\mathbf x = (1, x)x=(1,x), the guess 20x20x20x is w=(0,20)\mathbf w = (0, 20)w=(0,20) with risk 53; (5,18)(5, 18)(5,18) has risk 76; and (−2.429,20.971)(-2.429, 20.971)(−2.429,20.971), which we will derive below, beats both:

### When AAA cannot be inverted

Suppose two words always appear together the same number of times: say marketing templates always pair “free” with “gift”. Then every message has x1=x2x_1 = x_2x1​=x2​, and AAA is singular: it has no inverse. Is ERM stuck?

No. Take three such messages, whose “free” and “gift” counts are both 1, 2 and 3, with junk scores 10, 35 and 70 (no constant 1 this time, to keep the numbers small). One more request, this time from the inbox designers. They want to grey out messages that are probably junk and show the filter’s confidence on hover. Regression and logistic regression as code

LeastSquares(S):
  A ← d × d zero matrix;  b ← zero vector in R^d
  for i = 1..m:
    A ← A + x_i x_iᵀ
    b ← b + y_i · x_i
  if A is invertible: return w = solve(A, b)
  else:               return w = A⁺ b

PolynomialRegression(S, n):
  ψ(x) = (1, x, ..., x^n)
  S' ← ((ψ(x_1), y_1), ..., (ψ(x_m), y_m))
  a ← LeastSquares(S')
  return p(x) = ⟨a, ψ(x)⟩

LogisticRegression(S, steps, η):
  w ← 0 in R^d
  repeat steps times:
    g ← (1/m) Σ_i  −y_i · x_i · φ_sig(−y_i ⟨w, x_i⟩)
    w ← w − η · g
  return h(x) = φ_sig(⟨w, x⟩)

What people get wrong

“A flat point might only be a local dip.” For many losses, yes. For the square loss, no: the expansion in “Least squares” shows that at a zero gradient every step adds an average of squares, so every flat point is a global minimum. That is exactly why a zero gradient can serve as a certificate.

“The perfect fit is the best fit.” The degree-5 polynomial passes through all six messages, empirical risk 0. A class rich enough to pass through any six points can do that whatever the scores are, which is the memorizer’s trick from lesson 1: a perfect fit with too rich a class says nothing about the next message. Step 2 of the build-it challenge shows how far its prediction strays.

“A logistic probability of 0.71 means 71% of such messages are junk.” Only if the data really follow the logistic model. The number is what the fitted hypothesis says; whether it is calibrated is a separate question.
