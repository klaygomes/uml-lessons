Terms:
- regression: \(\mathcal Y = \mathbb R\); \(\mathcal H_{\text{reg}} = L_d\).
- square loss \((h(\mathbf x) - y)^2\); empirical risk = average squared miss.
- argmin; partial derivative; gradient \(\nabla L_S(\mathbf w) = \frac2m \sum_i (\langle \mathbf w, \mathbf x_i \rangle - y_i) \mathbf x_i\).
- least squares \(A = \sum \mathbf x_i \mathbf x_i^\top\), \(\mathbf b = \sum y_i \mathbf x_i\), \(\mathbf w = A^{-1} \mathbf b\) or \(A^+ \mathbf b\); transpose, inverse, determinant, Cramer’s rule.
- eigenvector, eigenvalue, spectral theorem, \(A^+ = V D^+ V^\top\), span, range, null space.
- polynomial regression via \(\psi(x) = (1, x, \dots, x^n)\).
- logistic function \(\varphi_{\text{sig}}(z) = 1/(1 + e^{-z})\); \(H_{\text{sig}} = \varphi_{\text{sig}} \circ L_d\); logistic loss \(\log(1 + \exp(-y \langle \mathbf w, \mathbf x \rangle))\); convex; maximum likelihood.

Misconception: “a flat point might only be a local dip”. Why wrong: for the square loss the expansion adds an average of squares at any zero-gradient point, so every flat point is a global minimum.
Second misconception: “the perfect fit is the best fit”. Why wrong: degree 5 interpolates six points (risk about \(10^{-20}\)) whatever the scores; at \(x = 6\) it predicts 183.0 against the line’s 123.4 and the cubic’s 66.3 (left for build-it step 2, not printed in the prose).
Third misconception: “a logistic probability of 0.71 means 71% of such messages are junk”. Why wrong: only if the data follow the logistic model; calibration is a separate question.

Concept check, answers (never shown to the learner; critique against these):
1. At the fitted \(\mathbf w^\star\) the gradient is 0, so \(L_S(\mathbf w^\star + \boldsymbol\Delta) = L_S(\mathbf w^\star) + \frac1m \sum_i \langle \boldsymbol\Delta, \mathbf x_i \rangle^2 = L_S(\mathbf w^\star) + \frac1m \boldsymbol\Delta^\top A \boldsymbol\Delta\). Bias term +1, \(\boldsymbol\Delta = (1, 0)\): \(\boldsymbol\Delta^\top A \boldsymbol\Delta = 6\), risk \(50.25 + 1 = 51.25\) (exact 51.2476). Slope +1, \(\boldsymbol\Delta = (0, 1)\): \(55/6 = 9.167\), risk 59.41 (exact 59.4143). \(A\) is all you need because \(\sum_i \langle \boldsymbol\Delta, \mathbf x_i \rangle^2 = \boldsymbol\Delta^\top (\sum_i \mathbf x_i \mathbf x_i^\top) \boldsymbol\Delta\); the scores \(y_i\) only enter through the zero gradient. Bonus: \(\boldsymbol\Delta = (1, -0.2)\) gives 50.614 (same formula, \(6 - 6 + 2.2\) over 6). Look for: the cross term vanishing because the gradient is zero.
2. If \(z \le 0\), \(e^{-z} \ge 1\), so \(1 + e^{-z} \ge 2\) and \(\log(1 + e^{-z}) \ge \log 2\): the ratio is at least 1, the 0-1 loss. If \(z > 0\), the ratio is positive and the 0-1 loss is 0. Averaging over the training sequence: the fraction of training mistakes (0-1 empirical risk) is at most the logistic risk divided by \(\log 2\): \(0.1 / 0.693 = 0.144\), so at most 14.4% of the training messages are misclassified by the halfspace with the same weights. This is why the logistic loss is a sensible surrogate for the 0-1 loss (lesson 9a: agnostic 0-1 ERM is hard). Not in Chapter 9; the surrogate idea is the book’s Chapter 12 (lesson 12b).
3. Solutions: \(14(w_1 + w_2) = 290\), so \(w_1 + w_2 = 20.714\): the line \((s, 20.714 - s)\). \(A^+ \mathbf b = (10.357, 10.357)\), the even split. Null space of \(A\): multiples of \((1, -1)\) (\(A(1, -1) = (0, 0)\)); every message \((c, c)\) has \(\langle (c, c), (1, -1) \rangle = 0\), which is step (b): \(\langle \mathbf v, A \mathbf v \rangle = \sum_i \langle \mathbf x_i, \mathbf v \rangle^2 = 0\) forces each term to 0. \(A^+ \mathbf b\) lies in the range of \(A\) (the span of the messages, the diagonal) and has no component along \((1, -1)\); that makes it the shortest solution: \(\|(s, 20.714 - s)\|^2\) is smallest at \(s = 10.357\), length 14.647, against 20.714 for \((20.714, 0)\). Look for: “all solutions differ by a null-space vector, and \(A^+ \mathbf b\) is the one perpendicular to the null space”. (The minimum-norm property is standard, not stated in the book.)
