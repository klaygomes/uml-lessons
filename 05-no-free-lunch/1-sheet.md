# No Free Lunch

Objective: after this, you can state and prove the No-Free-Lunch theorem step by step (the family of tasks on \(2m\) messages, the expectation as an average over sequences, max ≥ average ≥ min, keeping only unseen messages, the twin pairing, and the split that turns an expectation of at least 1/4 into a probability of at least 1/7 of risk at least 1/8); deduce that the class of all functions over an infinite domain is not PAC learnable; split ERM's true risk into approximation and estimation error, say what each depends on, bound the estimation error of a finite class from lesson 4's results; and choose a class along a nested family for a given number of examples (the bias-complexity tradeoff).

Key words: target (a labelling function the adversary picks to define a task; not "concept", "ground truth"), the truth (the world's actual rule in the eight-feature world, as in lesson 1), twin (the target that differs from another only at one unseen message), learning task (a distribution \(\mathcal D\) over \(\mathcal X \times \{0,1\}\); "task" for short), universal learner (not "general-purpose learner"), universal filter (Theo's learner: ERM over all hypotheses), No-Free-Lunch theorem (capitalized as in lesson 1), approximation error (not "bias" as a noun for the number; "inductive bias" stays the lesson 1 term), estimation error (not "variance"), bias-complexity tradeoff (not "bias-variance tradeoff"), overfitting, underfitting, nested family, rung (one class of the ladder), \(k\)-feature table, cell (one setting of the first \(k\) features), table learner (ERM over \(\mathcal H_k\)), label budget (story word for \(m\)), message, feature, junk / keep, example, training sequence (never "training set", "sample", "dataset"), hypothesis (not "model", "classifier", "predictor"; "Bayes optimal predictor" is the one fixed exception), learner, hypothesis class, empirical risk, true risk, consistent, ERM, best in the class, PAC learnable, realizable, Bayes risk, Markov's inequality.

Terms:
- learning task: a distribution \(\mathcal D\) over \(\mathcal X \times \{0,1\}\); the learner's goal is small \(L_{\mathcal D}(h)\).
- target: a function \(f_i: C \to \{0,1\}\) that labels the task \(\mathcal D_i\) (draw \(x\) uniform on \(C\), label it by \(f_i\)).
- universal learner: a learner \(A\) and a size \(m\) such that on every task, \(A(S)\) with \(S \sim \mathcal D^m\) probably has small true risk. None exists.
- approximation error: \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{app}} = \min_{h \in \mathcal H} L_{\mathcal D}(h)\); depends on the class and the task, not on \(m\); zero under realizability; at least the Bayes risk.
- estimation error: \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}} = L_{\mathcal D}(h_S) - \varepsilon_{\mathrm{app}}\) for ERM's \(h_S\); random, nonnegative, shrinks with \(m\), grows with \(\log|\mathcal H|\).
- bias-complexity tradeoff: a richer class lowers \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{app}}\) and can raise \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}}\) (overfitting); a poorer class does the reverse (underfitting).
- \(k\)-feature table, \(\mathcal H_k\): all hypotheses that depend only on the first \(k\) features; \(|\mathcal H_k| = 2^{2^k}\); \(\mathcal H_8\) is every hypothesis on \(\{0,1\}^8\).

Misconceptions:
- "No free lunch means learning is impossible." Why wrong: every task the theorem builds is learned by ERM over \(\{f\}\) with no examples; the theorem rules out one learner for all tasks, not learning.
- "So every learner is equally good." Why wrong: equal only on average over all targets weighted uniformly; real tasks are not uniform random targets, and prior knowledge is exactly that claim.
- "A bigger class is safer." Why wrong: it lowers \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{app}}\) but raises \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}}\); at 50 labels the only class containing the truth (\(\mathcal H_8\)) is the worst rung but one (0.424; only \(\mathcal H_0\) at 0.499 is worse).
- "Approximation error is bad luck with the data." Why wrong: it is fixed by class and task before any example; more examples leave it unchanged.
- Lesson 1's two failure modes map here: the rat's failure (class misses the truth) is approximation error; the pigeon's (class too large for the examples) is estimation error.

Derivation:
- No-Free-Lunch theorem, seven steps as in the book's proof, steps renamed: (1) \(C\), \(T = 2^{2m}\) targets, \(\mathcal D_i\), \(L_{\mathcal D_i}(h) = \frac{1}{2m}\sum_{x \in C}\mathbb 1[h(x) \ne f_i(x)]\); (2) eq. 5.3, with the independence step spelled out (each of the \(k = (2m)^m\) sequences has probability \((1/2m)^m\)); (3) eq. 5.4 (max ≥ avg, swap finite sums, avg ≥ min); (4) eq. 5.5 (\(p \ge m\), drop seen terms, \(1/2m \ge 1/2p\)); (5) eq. 5.6; (6) the pairing, identified with lesson 1's flip map \(\varphi\) applied to targets; reduction from \(A'\) on \(\mathcal X\) to \(A\) on \(C\) (the book's "Clearly", made explicit: restrict \(A'(S)\) to \(C\), extend \(f_{i^\star}\) by keep outside \(C\)); (7) eq. 5.2 to the probability statement by splitting \(\mathbb E[\theta]\) at 1/8 (the book's Exercise 1, via Lemma B.1), with Markov's inequality on \(1 - \theta\), \(a = 7/8\), as the alternative.
- Corollary: the class of all functions over an infinite domain is not PAC learnable; proof by contradiction with \(\varepsilon < 1/8\), \(\delta < 1/7\), noting \(f \in \mathcal H\) so the task is realizable for \(\mathcal H\).
- Error decomposition (eq. 5.7); \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{app}}\) monotone under nesting (min over a superset); \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}} \ge 0\).
- Proposition (estimation error of a finite class): with probability ≥ \(1 - \delta\), \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}} \le \sqrt{2\log(2|\mathcal H|/\delta)/m}\), from Hoeffding + union bound (Corollary 4.6 at \(\varepsilon/2\)) and Lemma 4.2's three-line chain. The book states only that \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}}\) grows logarithmically with \(|\mathcal H|\) and decreases with \(m\) (p. 65).
- ERM over \(\mathcal H_k\) is majority vote per cell (empirical risk separates over cells; every combination of cell labels is in the class).

Algorithms:
- TableERM(S, k): \(O(mk)\) time, \(O(k \min(m, 2^k))\) space, \(O(k)\) per prediction; at \(k = d\) it is the memorizer.
- Adversary(A, C, m): the proof as code; \(O(2^{2m}(2m)^m(t_A + m))\) time, \(O(m)\) extra space; \(m = 4\) is 1,048,576 learner runs.
- Build-it challenge: `table_learner`, `true_risk`, `approximation_error`, `adversary` in standard-library Python with the 50-message starter sequence (generated with `random.Random(5)`). Concept check, answers (never shown to the learner; critique against these):
1. The theorem needs \(m < |\mathcal X|/2 = 5\); \(m = 10\) does not satisfy it, so it says nothing here. Not applying is not success. Task: uniform on the 10 messages, all junk. The memorizer is right on seen messages and wrong on unseen ones; each message is missed by all 10 draws with probability \(0.9^{10}\), so the expected true risk is \(0.9^{10} \approx 0.3487\). Look for: the learner separating "the theorem's hypothesis fails" from "the conclusion fails", and computing via linearity of expectation over messages.
2. If the twins differ at a message in \(S_j\), their labelled sequences \(S_j^i\) and \(S_j^{i'}\) differ, so \(A\) may return different hypotheses; the identity \(A(S_j^i) = A(S_j^{i'})\) fails and the indicator sum can be 0 (a learner that copies seen labels is right in both). \(p \ge m\): \(S_j\) has \(m\) entries, so at most \(m\) distinct messages of the \(2m\). If every sequence has \(m\) distinct messages, \(p = m\), step 4 loses nothing and the bound is still 1/4 (exactly 1/4 averaged over targets for a learner consistent on seen messages); the slack in the true average (0.28 to 0.30) comes from repeats.
3. Expected \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{est}} = 0.1196 - 26/256 = 0.1196 - 0.1016 = 0.0180\). Bound ≤ 0.05 needs \(m \ge 2(2^k \ln 2 + \ln 20)/0.05^2\): \(k = 3\): 6,832.7, so 6,833; \(k = 8\): 144,353.1, so 144,354. Point out the gap: the bound holds for every distribution and is loose here (actual expected estimation error at 50 examples is 0.018).
