The certificate operations would not sign

Lesson 12a ended on a small victory and an awkward email. The job that would have needed 5×10295 \times 10^{29}5×1029 years finished in 87 steps: a walk down lesson 9’s logistic risk on the ten messages, stopped by a certificate proving that no weight vector in the ball of radius 3 does more than 0.01 better on those ten messages. Operations read the report and sent back two questions, and the certificate answers neither.

The first question: “Certified on which mail?” The proof is about the training sequence, the messages you already have. Operations wants a promise about next month’s mail, bought with the 3,000 labels the budget pays for. A colleague reading over your shoulder says the loss is convex, so of course it learns; just add labels.

The second question: “We count mistakes.” The certificate is about the logistic loss, a smooth score. A filter is judged by how many messages it gets wrong, the 0-1 loss, and lesson 12a’s opening showed that nobody can minimize that directly.

So the filter cannot ship. Operations’ requirement is concrete: a written bound, before training, saying that the expected fraction of mistakes on fresh mail is at most 0.36, using at most 3,000 labels. By the end of this lesson you will meet it with your own hands. On the way you will find out that the colleague is wrong: convexity alone does not learn, not even in one dimension, and the reason tells you exactly what to add.

Is convexity enough?

Lesson 12a showed that convex problems can be optimized. Can they be learned? That is a different question: it asks whether the hypothesis ERM returns on mmm examples has small true risk, with a number of examples that depends only on ε\varepsilonε and δ\deltaδ. As in lesson 3, ε\varepsilonε is the extra true risk you tolerate above the best in the class, and δ\deltaδ the chance you allow of an unlucky training sequence; the true risk LD(w)L_{\mathcal D}(\mathbf w)LD​(w) is the average loss on a fresh example drawn from the distribution D\mathcal DD. A problem is agnostic PAC learnable, in lesson 3’s words, when some learner meets that (ε,δ)(\varepsilon, \delta)(ε,δ) promise for every distribution, with no assumption that a perfect hypothesis exists.

You have good reasons to hope so. Lessons 6 and 9 showed that halfspaces in ddd dimensions are learnable (perhaps not efficiently). Lesson 4’s discretization trick suggested that any class described by ddd numbers is learnable with a number of examples that grows with ddd. So surely, for a fixed small ddd, every convex learning problem over Rd\mathbb R^dRd is learnable?

No. Not even for d=1d = 1d=1. This does not contradict lesson 6, which is about binary classification with the 0-1 loss, while here losses are real-valued and can be huge. Nor does it contradict the discretization trick, which assumed a bounded loss and that a finite number of bits per parameter is enough. Here is the counterexample, and it is worth going through slowly, because every step shows a different way that “convex” fails to imply “learnable”.

Surrogate loss functions

Now back to the bug. The natural loss for the filter is the 0-1 loss, and it is not convex in w\mathbf ww, as lesson 12a showed by a chord and by the terraces of its lab. Minimizing it on non-separable data is NP-hard. The popular way around the hardness is to upper bound the nonconvex loss by a convex one. A convex surrogate loss for a loss ℓ\ellℓ is a loss that

  1. is convex, and
  2. upper bounds the original loss: it is at least ℓ\ellℓ on every hypothesis and every example.

For halfspaces, the hinge loss is a convex surrogate for the 0-1 loss. Lesson 12a showed it is convex. The upper bound is a two-case check on the signed score z=y⟨w,x⟩z = y\langle \mathbf w, \mathbf x \ranglez=y⟨w,x⟩. If z≤0z \le 0z≤0, the 0-1 loss is 1 and the hinge loss is max⁡{0,1−z}≥1−z≥1\max\{0, 1 - z\} \ge 1 - z \ge 1max{0,1−z}≥1−z≥1. If z>0z > 0z>0, the 0-1 loss is 0 and the hinge loss is a maximum with 0, so it is at least 0. Either way, ℓ0−1(w,(x,y))≤ℓhinge(w,(x,y))\ell^{0-1}(\mathbf w, (\mathbf x, y)) \le \ell^{\text{hinge}}(\mathbf w, (\mathbf x, y))ℓ0−1(w,(x,y))≤ℓhinge(w,(x,y)).

What people get wrong

“The loss is convex, so the problem is learnable.” Convexity makes ERM an optimization problem without false bottoms. It says nothing about how many examples you need. One-dimensional linear regression with the square loss is convex and not learnable at all. You also need a bounded class and a Lipschitz or smooth loss.

“More labels always help.” Not when the class or the loss is unconstrained. In the two-worlds game the smallest excess you could force the adversary down to rose with mmm, from 0.956 at 10 labels to 0.999 at 10,000, because more labels let the adversary hide a rarer example.

“Bounding the weights fixes it.” Not by itself. The bounded counterexample kept ∣w∣≤1|w| \le 1∣w∣≤1 and still failed, because one rare, huge example made the loss arbitrarily steep. The constants ρ\rhoρ or β\betaβ must be fixed in advance, which in practice means bounding the messages, ∥x∥≤R\|\mathbf x\| \le R∥x∥≤R, as well.

“Minimizing the hinge loss minimizes the number of mistakes.” It minimizes an upper bound on them. The two minimizers can differ, and the optimization error is exactly the price. In the lab world it was 0.17, more than three times the best possible 0-1 risk.

“Any convex loss is a surrogate for the 0-1 loss.” It must also sit above it. The natural-log logistic loss is log⁡2≈0.69\log 2 \approx 0.69log2≈0.69 at a zero score, below the 0-1 loss’s 1; divide by log⁡2\log 2log2 to make it a surrogate.

“A bigger BBB is always better, because the class is richer.” Richer classes lower the floor and raise the estimation term ρB/m\rho B/\sqrt mρB/m​. Past the point where the floor stops falling (about B=2.7B = 2.7B=2.7 in the lab world), more BBB only costs labels.

Recap and the next bug

  1. In a convex learning problem, ERM is a convex optimization problem, but convexity is not enough to learn, even in one dimension: whatever a deterministic learner outputs, one of two worlds that usually show it the same training sequence charges an excess of about 0.99, and more labels make it worse. Convexity plus a bounded class is still not enough, because one rare, huge example can make the loss arbitrarily steep.
  2. Convex-Lipschitz-bounded and convex-smooth-bounded problems add a norm bound BBB on the class and a Lipschitz or smoothness constant fixed in advance. They are learnable, as lessons 13 and 14 prove; with Lipschitz losses, m≥ρ2B2/ε2m \ge \rho^2 B^2/\varepsilon^2m≥ρ2B2/ε2 examples suffice in expectation. Halfspaces with the hinge loss on messages of length at most RRR are convex-Lipschitz-bounded with parameters R,BR, BR,B.
  3. A convex surrogate is convex and upper bounds the original loss. The hinge loss is one for the 0-1 loss of halfspaces, and so is the base-2 logistic loss; the natural-log logistic loss is not, since it is below 1 at a zero score. The 0-1 risk of the learned halfspace is at most the approximation error plus the optimization error plus the estimation error.
  4. For a smooth surrogate, ERM is a walk with step 1/β1/\beta1/β that lowers the empirical risk at every step and stops with a certificate, at O(md)O(md)O(md) time per step instead of a search through every labelling.
  5. The bound to certify is the best surrogate risk over the ball plus ρB/m\rho B/\sqrt mρB/m​. A small ball raises the first term, a large ball the second: in the lab world with the hinge loss and 3,000 labels, BBB from 1.4 to 1.9 meets 0.36, and the best is B=1.5B = 1.5B=1.5, with bound 0.349.

The hinge filter ships with its certificate. A week later, a user writes in to correct a single label, one message out of 3,000, and someone retrains out of habit. The new weights look nothing like the old ones. Nothing is wrong with the bound, which never promised that the learner’s output would sit still; it promised something about the average over training sequences. But it makes you uneasy. The guarantees in this course have all come from uniform convergence, every hypothesis’s empirical risk staying close to its true risk at once. Is there a different reason a learner could generalize: a rule whose output barely moves when one example changes, and which learns every convex-Lipschitz-bounded problem because it is stable? That is the next lesson: regularization and stability.
