The job that never finishes

Lesson 7 ended with a filter that, on paper, does everything right. Your teammate’s decision lists (chains of tests, each on one feature, that hand out a label when their feature is present, with a default label at the end) may grow to any size, and structural risk minimization, SRM, keeps them honest: it shares the confidence budget out across the sizes, gives every list a gap (the most its empirical risk may be off from its true risk) that depends on its size, and returns the list with the smallest bound, its empirical risk plus its gap. The reviewer signed. The five-test list shipped with a certificate.

Then the operations team ran the same training job on the real mail stream, where hundreds of features are in use, and it never finished.

You already know why, because lesson 7 did the sum. SRM’s stopping rule runs ERM (empirical risk minimization, lesson 2’s rule: return a hypothesis with the fewest mistakes on the training examples) over the lists of size at most 1, then size at most 2, and so on, and it may stop only once the gap for the next size reaches the best bound found so far. With 1,000 labels the best bound was 0.1938, and the gap does not reach it before size 22. As in lesson 7, Hn\mathcal H_nHn​ is the class of decision lists of size at most nnn, so H22\mathcal H_{22}H22​ is every list with at most 22 tests, each on a different one of features 1 to 22, and it holds about 1.55×10281.55 \times 10^{28}1.55×1028 of them. On a toy stream with six features the rule could stop at size 6; on the real stream it cannot know that no later feature matters.

How long is “never”? Suppose the job does the simplest thing, checking every list of each size against every one of the 1,000 examples, on a machine that does a billion such checks a second. To price that, we need lesson 7’s count of lists, so here it is again, read slowly:

Notice which number is the problem. The statistics are fine: lesson 7’s guarantee says a thousand labels are enough. Each extra size adds only a logarithm’s worth to the gap, while the number of lists to check grows by a factor of twenty or more. Every lesson so far asked how many examples learning needs. Nobody asked how long it takes to use them.

That is this lesson. We will ask when a learning problem is computationally easy, when it is hard, and, the twist that rescues the filter, when changing the hypothesis class makes a hard problem easy.

Two prices for every class

Every hypothesis class has two prices. The first is the one you have been paying since lesson 2: the sample complexity, how many examples the learner needs before its guarantee holds. That is a price in information. The second is the computational complexity: how much computation the learner does to turn those examples into a hypothesis, and to use the hypothesis on a new message. That is a price in time.

How do you measure time without a stopwatch? The seconds an algorithm takes depend on the machine: the clock rate of its processor, its memory, the language. So computer science measures time asymptotically. When we say merge sort sorts nnn items in time O(nlog⁡n)O(n \log n)O(nlogn), we mean this: on any machine that implements some accepted abstract model of computation, there are constants ccc and n0n_0n0​, which may depend on the machine, such that for every n>n0n > n_0n>n0​, sorting any nnn items takes at most c nlog⁡nc \, n \log ncnlogn seconds. The machine changes the constant and nothing else.

That sentence is dense, so let’s take it apart.

  • O(⋅)O(\cdot)O(⋅), read “big O of”, names a growth rate and hides the constant in front.
  • nnn is the number of items, and log⁡n\log nlogn is its logarithm (base 2 here; another base only changes ccc), so nlog⁡nn \log nnlogn grows a little faster than nnn.
  • ccc is the constant a particular machine brings: a slow machine has a large ccc, a fast one a small ccc.
  • n0n_0n0​ is a threshold: the promise only has to hold for inputs larger than n0n_0n0​, because small inputs are cheap anyway.
  • “For every n>n0n > n_0n>n0​” and “any nnn items” say the bound holds for every large size and for the worst arrangement of that size.

In plain words: past some size, the time never exceeds a fixed multiple of nlog⁡nn \log nnlogn. For 8 items, 8log⁡28=248 \log_2 8 = 248log2​8=24, and merge sort makes at most 24 comparisons. Here is merge sort counting its comparisons against nlog⁡2nn \log_2 nnlog2​n, with c=1c = 1c=1.

All of that assumes we know what nnn, the input size, is. For sorting it is obvious: the length of the list, or the number of bits it takes to write down. For learning it is not obvious at all, and this is the first real subtlety of the lesson.

Why not the number of examples mmm, the length of the training sequence? Because that would be pointless. Give a learner far more examples than its sample complexity, and it can simply ignore the extra ones. A larger training sequence never makes learning harder, so the time we allow a learner should not grow just because we handed it more data. Measuring runtime against mmm would reward a learner for wasting examples.

Instead, we measure runtime against the natural parameters of the problem: the accuracy ε\varepsilonε (as since lesson 3, the extra true risk we tolerate above the best in the class, such as 0.05), the confidence δ\deltaδ (the chance we allow the guarantee to fail, such as 0.01), the number of features, and measures of how complex the hypothesis class is. To get an asymptotic question at all, we build a sequence of problems. Take axis aligned rectangles, which you will meet properly in a moment. Fix ε\varepsilonε and δ\deltaδ and let the dimension run through n=2,3,4,…n = 2, 3, 4, \dotsn=2,3,4,…: that is one sequence. Or fix the dimension and δ\deltaδ, and let the accuracy run through ε=1/2,1/3,…\varepsilon = 1/2, 1/3, \dotsε=1/2,1/3,…: that is another. Once you fix the sequence, you can ask how the runtime grows along it.

A learner that cheats

Before the formal definition, one more trap. Here is a learner that runs in constant time on every training sequence, whatever the class.

It does no work at all. It returns, as its hypothesis, a little program that stores the training sequence, and whenever someone asks it for the label of a message xxx, runs ERM on the stored examples and applies the result to xxx. The learner itself always returns the same thing and never touches the data. Constant time!

Of course nothing has been gained. The work moved from training to prediction: every message that arrives at the filter now triggers a full ERM search. This is the lazy learner, and the rule that defeats it is simple. We require that the hypothesis a learner returns can predict the label of a new example in time no greater than the learner’s own training time. A learner may not hide its work inside its output.

The formal definition

Now we can say exactly what “learning is fast” means. We count operations of an abstract machine, such as a Turing machine (or a Turing machine over the reals, when the inputs are real numbers), and we assume that every machine implementing it does each operation in at most some constant number of seconds.

Before the definition, its ingredients, all from earlier lessons. A learning task is a triple (Z,H,ℓ)(\mathcal Z, \mathcal H, \ell)(Z,H,ℓ): Z\mathcal ZZ is the set of possible examples (in the mail room, a message with its label), H\mathcal HH is the benchmark hypothesis class, and ℓ(h,z)\ell(h, z)ℓ(h,z) is the loss of hypothesis hhh on example zzz (1 for a mistake and 0 otherwise, for the filter). So an efficient learner can only ever use polynomially many examples. Efficient learning implies a polynomial sample complexity, never the other way round.

ERM by brute force

Let’s state the most natural learner precisely, because every algorithm in this lesson is a cleverer version of it. Given a class H\mathcal HH, a domain Z\mathcal ZZ and a loss ℓ\ellℓ, the rule ERMH\mathrm{ERM}_{\mathcal H}ERMH​ is: on a training sequence SSS of mmm examples, return some h∈Hh \in \mathcal Hh∈H with the smallest empirical risk LS(h)L_S(h)LS​(h), lesson 1’s average loss of hhh over the training examples. For the filter the loss is 1 for a mistake and 0 otherwise, so LS(h)L_S(h)LS​(h) is the fraction of training examples hhh gets wrong: on the eight FLUN messages of the conjunctions section below (F for “FREE”, L for a link, U for an unknown sender, N for sent at night), the rule “junk exactly when F” wrongly says junk to the keep messages 1001 and 1011, so LS(h)=2/8=0.25L_S(h) = 2/8 = 0.25LS​(h)=2/8=0.25.

For binary classification, lesson 6 showed that if learning is possible at all, ERM does it. So the question “how fast can we learn?” starts as “how fast can we run ERM?”.

Restricting yourself to a finite class sounds mild. The class of every hypothesis a C++ program of at most 10,000 bits can compute is finite. So is any class described by finitely many parameters, if you store each parameter with finitely many bits: rectangles with corners written to three decimal places, say. And the sample complexity of a finite class barely notices its size, because it pays only log⁡∣H∣\log |\mathcal H|log∣H∣. For the C++ class, there are 210,0002^{10{,}000}210,000 strings of 10,000 bits, so ∣H∣≤210,000|\mathcal H| \le 2^{10{,}000}∣H∣≤210,000, and its natural logarithm is log⁡∣H∣≤10,000log⁡2≈6,931\log |\mathcal H| \le 10{,}000 \log 2 \approx 6{,}931log∣H∣≤10,000log2≈6,931, because the logarithm of a power brings the exponent down in front (log⁡2≈0.693\log 2 \approx 0.693log2≈0.693). That is a perfectly reasonable number of examples.

Rectangles with noisy labels

Now drop realizability. Users mislabel things, and the band is not perfectly rectangular. ERM must now find the rectangle with the fewest mistakes, not one with none. How hard can that be?

Hard, it turns out, in a precise sense. To say it, we need three standard names from complexity theory. A decision problem asks a yes/no question about an input, such as “does this graph have a proper colouring with three colours?”. P is the class of decision problems some algorithm answers in time polynomial in the input size. NP is the class whose yes answers come with a certificate that can be checked in polynomial time (a colouring, for the graph question). A problem is NP-hard when every problem in NP reduces to it in polynomial time (its inputs can be translated quickly into inputs of this problem with the same answer), so a polynomial algorithm for it would put all of NP in P. Whether P=NPP = NPP=NP is the most famous open question in computer science, and almost everyone believes the answer is no.

Fact (agnostic ERM is NP-hard for rectangles). For many common classes, including axis aligned rectangles, solving the ERM problem in the agnostic setting is NP-hard. In most cases it is NP-hard even to find some h∈Hh \in \mathcal Hh∈H whose empirical risk is at most a constant c>1c > 1c>1 times that of the best. So unless P=NPP = NPP=NP, no algorithm whose runtime is polynomial in both mmm and nnn is guaranteed to find an ERM hypothesis.

We won’t prove this one; it is a research result, and the proof is a delicate reduction. But here is the surprise: for any fixed dimension nnn, there is an agnostic learner whose runtime is polynomial in 1/ε1/\varepsilon1/ε and 1/δ1/\delta1/δ. Its dependence on nnn is exponential, which is exactly what the hardness result allows.

The idea comes from the tightest box. A box built from a set of points is pinned down by at most 2n2n2n of them: one point achieving each minimum and one achieving each maximum. So try every subset of at most 2n2n2n training messages, build the tightest box around each, and keep the box with the fewest mistakes.

Claim (subset search is an exact ERM). Let SSS be any training sequence in Rn\mathbb R^nRn. Among the tightest boxes of all subsets of at most 2n2n2n training messages, together with the all-keep hypothesis, there is one whose empirical risk is the minimum over all rectangles.

Proof. Take any rectangle hhh. We will find a candidate on the list that labels every training message exactly as hhh does, so it has the same empirical risk. Apply this to an ERM rectangle and you are done.

Let TTT be the set of training messages that hhh labels 1 (whatever their labels in SSS). If TTT is empty, hhh labels every training message 0, just like the all-keep hypothesis.

Otherwise let BBB be the tightest box around TTT. Every message in TTT lies in BBB, by the same argument as before, so BBB labels it 1, like hhh. Every message of TTT lies inside the rectangle hhh, so the minimum and maximum of each coordinate over TTT lie inside hhh’s interval for that coordinate, which means B⊆hB \subseteq hB⊆h: the box BBB lies inside the rectangle hhh. A training message not in TTT lies outside hhh, hence outside BBB, so BBB labels it 0, like hhh.

Finally, BBB is also the tightest box of a small subset. For each coordinate, pick one message of TTT that achieves the minimum and one that achieves the maximum. That is at most 2n2n2n messages, call them T′T'T′, and the tightest box around T′T'T′ has the same 2n2n2n bounds as BBB, so it is BBB. Since T′T'T′ is a subset of the training messages with ∣T′∣≤2n|T'| \le 2n∣T′∣≤2n, BBB is on the list. ■\blacksquare■

Three kinds of junk

The conjunction filter ships, and for a while it holds. Then the security team walks in with a chart. There are now three kinds of junk: prize draws (“FREE” and a link), night blasts (unknown sender and sent at night), and fake invoices (a link from an unknown sender, no “FREE” in sight). Each kind is a conjunction. Junk is any of them.

That is a disjunction of conjunctions. A 3-term DNF formula (disjunctive normal form) over {0,1}n\{0,1\}^n{0,1}n is a hypothesis

h(x)=A1(x)∨A2(x)∨A3(x),h(x) = A_1(x) \vee A_2(x) \vee A_3(x),h(x)=A1​(x)∨A2​(x)∨A3​(x),

where each AiA_iAi​ is a Boolean conjunction. It says 1 if at least one of the three conjunctions says 1, and 0 if all three say 0. Let H3DNFn\mathcal H_{3DNF}^nH3DNFn​ be the class of all of them. In the mail room, the security team’s rule is (F∧L)∨(U∧N)∨(L∧U∧¬F)(F \wedge L) \vee (U \wedge N) \vee (L \wedge U \wedge \neg F)(F∧L)∨(U∧N)∨(L∧U∧¬F).

Read it piece by piece. A1(x)A_1(x)A1​(x), A2(x)A_2(x)A2​(x) and A3(x)A_3(x)A3​(x) are the three conjunctions applied to message xxx, each a 0 or a 1, called the terms. The symbol ∨\vee∨ is “or”: the result is 1 when at least one side is. In plain words: a 3-term DNF says junk when at least one of its three conjunctions says junk. On the security team’s rule, the message 0011 (unknown sender, at night) fires only the second term, so junk; 0110 (a link, unknown sender, no “FREE”) fires only the third, so junk; 1001 (“FREE”, at night) fires none, so keep.

Computationally, it is a different animal. To say how, we need two more definitions.

A learner is proper if the hypothesis it returns always belongs to the class H\mathcal HH we are learning. ERMH\mathrm{ERM}_{\mathcal H}ERMH​ is proper by definition: it returns a member of H\mathcal HH.

RP (randomized polynomial time) is the class of decision problems for which some algorithm that may flip coins runs in time polynomial in the input size on every input, always answers no when the right answer is no, and answers yes with probability at least 1/21/21/2 when the right answer is yes. Clearly P⊆RPP \subseteq RPP⊆RP, and it is known that RP⊆NPRP \subseteq NPRP⊆NP, where ⊆\subseteq⊆ reads “is contained in”: every problem in P is in RP, and every problem in RP is in NP. Nobody knows whether either inclusion is strict, but it is widely believed that NP is strictly larger than RP: NP-hard problems are believed to have no randomized polynomial algorithm.

Fact (proper learning of 3-term DNF is hard). Unless RP=NPRP = NPRP=NP, there is no polynomial time algorithm that properly learns the sequence of 3-term DNF problems in which the nnn-th problem has dimension nnn. In particular, ERMH3DNFn\mathrm{ERM}_{\mathcal H_{3DNF}^n}ERMH3DNFn​​ is proper, so it cannot be implemented in polynomial time either.

The proof reduces graph 3-colouring, a classic NP-hard problem, to properly PAC learning 3-term DNF: an efficient proper learner would colour graphs. We won’t prove it here. The second problem at the end of this lesson builds the same kind of reduction for a related class, so you will see the machinery with your own hands.

Notice how small the change was. Conjunctions: easy. Three conjunctions joined by OR: hard. A slight generalization of the class has made ERM intractable, even in the realizable case where conjunctions were a blink.

Change the representation

So the security team’s rule cannot be learned properly in polynomial time, unless something everyone believes false is true. Does that mean the filter cannot learn it?

Read the fact again. It forbids a fast learner that returns a 3-term DNF formula. Nobody said the filter must return one. What we actually want is a hypothesis with small true risk. If a hypothesis outside the class predicts just as well, who cares how it is written?

A learner that may return a hypothesis outside H\mathcal HH, from some larger class, is called representation independent (lesson 3 mentioned the idea in passing). The plan: find a bigger class that contains every 3-term DNF and whose ERM is easy.

The bigger class comes from a piece of Boolean algebra. OR distributes over AND, just as multiplication distributes over addition, so

A1∨A2∨A3=⋀u∈A1, v∈A2, w∈A3(u∨v∨w),A_1 \vee A_2 \vee A_3 = \bigwedge_{u \in A_1,\ v \in A_2,\ w \in A_3} (u \vee v \vee w),A1​∨A2​∨A3​=u∈A1​, v∈A2​, w∈A3​⋀​(u∨v∨w),

where u∈A1u \in A_1u∈A1​ means “uuu is one of the literals of A1A_1A1​”. The right side is an AND of clauses, each an OR of three literals: a 3-CNF formula (conjunctive normal form).

Read the right side from the inside out. u∨v∨wu \vee v \vee wu∨v∨w is one clause: true when at least one of its three literals holds. The big ⋀\bigwedge⋀ is an AND of many clauses, and the text under it says which: one clause for every way of picking a literal uuu from A1A_1A1​, a literal vvv from A2A_2A2​ and a literal www from A3A_3A3​. In plain words: three terms ORed together equal the AND of every clause that takes one literal from each term. The security team’s terms have 2, 2 and 3 literals, so the right side has 2×2×3=122 \times 2 \times 3 = 122×2×3=12 clauses. Why did it work? We swapped a class where ERM is hard for a larger class with more structure. The larger class is easier to search, because its ERM is crossing out literals, and it costs more examples, because it is larger. That is the trade, and you will feel it in the lab.

When no representation helps

The 3-CNF trick shows that hardness of ERM is not hardness of learning. So how could anyone ever prove that a learning problem is hard, for every learner, whatever it returns?

One route goes through cryptography. In a sense, cryptography is the opposite of learning. A learner tries to uncover the rule behind the examples it sees. A cryptographer tries to make sure that nobody can uncover a secret, even with access to partial information about it. So a result saying some system is secure should translate into a result saying some task is unlearnable.

There is a catch. Nobody can currently prove that any cryptographic protocol is unbreakable. Even P≠NPP \ne NPP=NP is not enough to prove it, although for most common cryptographic scenarios it is necessary. So cryptography starts from cryptographic assumptions, and the more these are relied on, the more we trust them, or at least, the more we believe algorithms that refute them are hard to find. Hardness of learning built this way is conditional on the same assumptions.

Definition (one-way function). A one-way function is a function f:{0,1}n→{0,1}nf : \{0,1\}^n \to \{0,1\}^nf:{0,1}n→{0,1}n (formally a sequence of them, one for each nnn) that is easy to compute and hard to invert: fff can be computed in time poly(n)\mathrm{poly}(n)poly(n), but for every randomized polynomial time algorithm AAA and every polynomial p(⋅)p(\cdot)p(⋅),

What people get wrong

“ERM over this class is NP-hard, so the class cannot be learned efficiently.” Hardness of ERM rules out efficient proper learning (under RP≠NPRP \ne NPRP=NP), nothing more. 3-term DNF is the counterexample: hard for any proper learner, easy through 3-CNF.

“Realizable or agnostic, it’s the same problem.” Statistically, yes: a class is learnable in both settings exactly when its VC dimension is finite. Computationally, the difference is immense. For rectangles and conjunctions, realizable ERM takes O(nm)O(nm)O(nm) time, and agnostic ERM is NP-hard.

“A smaller class is always cheaper.” Cheaper in labels, yes. But 3-term DNF is smaller than 3-CNF and, for proper learners, far more expensive in time. The two prices can point in opposite directions.

“The runtime should be measured against the number of examples.” A learner can always ignore surplus examples, so measuring against mmm would reward waste. Runtime is measured against nnn, 1/ε1/\varepsilon1/ε and 1/δ1/\delta1/δ, along a sequence of problems.

“My learner trains in constant time.” Check its hypothesis. If predicting one label runs a search, the work has only moved. The definition bounds prediction by the training budget.

Recap and the next bug

  1. Every class has two prices: the sample complexity (how many examples) and the runtime (how much computation). Lessons 2 to 7 priced the first; this lesson prices the second.
  2. Runtime is measured in operations, asymptotically, along a sequence of problems, as a function of nnn, 1/ε1/\varepsilon1/ε and 1/δ1/\delta1/δ. A learner is efficient when that function is polynomial, and its hypothesis must predict within the same budget.
  3. Exhaustive ERM over a finite class costs O(∣H∣ m)O(|\mathcal H| \, m)O(∣H∣m): fine for small classes, exponential when log⁡∣Hn∣\log |\mathcal H_n|log∣Hn​∣ grows like nnn, although the sample complexity stays polynomial.
  4. In the realizable case, ERM is fast for rectangles (the tightest box) and conjunctions (crossing out literals), both O(nm)O(nm)O(nm). In the agnostic case it is NP-hard for both; for rectangles in a fixed dimension, subset search runs in mO(n)m^{O(n)}mO(n).
  5. Proper learning of 3-term DNF is hard unless RP=NPRP = NPRP=NP, but learning it through 3-CNF is efficient: O(n3m)O(n^3 m)O(n3m) time for O(n3)O(n^3)O(n3) times more examples. Changing the representation trades labels for time.
  6. To show a problem is hard for every learner, cryptographic assumptions help: if trapdoor one-way functions exist, their inverses form a class that is small but not efficiently learnable.
