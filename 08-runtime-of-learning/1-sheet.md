# The Runtime of Learning

Objective: after this, you can state what it means for a learner to solve a learning task in time \(O(f)\) and to be efficient along a sequence of problems (including the prediction-time clause that defeats the lazy learner), explain why runtime is not measured against \(m\), compute the cost of exhaustive ERM over a finite class, run and prove correct the realizable ERM algorithms for axis aligned rectangles (tightest box, \(O(nm)\)) and Boolean conjunctions (crossing out literals, \(O(nm)\)), prove that subset search is an exact agnostic ERM for rectangles in time \(m^{O(n)}\), state the hardness of agnostic ERM (unless \(P = NP\)) and of proper 3-term DNF learning (unless \(RP = NP\)), prove the 3-term DNF to 3-CNF identity and use the clause features \(\psi\) to learn 3-term DNF efficiently, weigh the sample complexity against the runtime of a representation, and explain how trapdoor one-way functions give a class that is small but not efficiently learnable.

Terms:
- sample complexity vs computational complexity: the information price and the computation price of learning a class.
- feasible / efficiently computable: doable in time \(O(p(n))\), \(p\) a polynomial (p. 100).
- sequence of problems: \((\mathcal Z_n, \mathcal H_n, \ell_n)_{n \ge 1}\), along which runtime is analysed (pp. 101 to 103).
- lazy learner: returns a hypothesis that stores \(S\) and runs ERM at prediction time; ruled out by requiring prediction within the training budget (p. 102).
- solves the task in time \(O(f)\), runtime \(O(g)\), efficient: Definition 8.1 (pp. 102 to 103).
- tightest box: \(a_i = \min\), \(b_i = \max\) of coordinate \(i\) over the positive examples (p. 105).
- literal, Boolean conjunction, \(\mathcal H_C^n\), \(|\mathcal H_C^n| \le 3^n + 1\) (p. 106).
- 3-term DNF: \(A_1 \vee A_2 \vee A_3\) with conjunctions \(A_i\) (p. 107).
- proper learner: returns a member of \(\mathcal H\) (p. 107); representation independent: may return a hypothesis outside \(\mathcal H\) (p. 107).
- 3-CNF and \(\psi : \{0,1\}^n \to \{0,1\}^{(2n)^3}\) (p. 108).
- RP: Definition 8.2 (p. 112); P, NP, NP-hard: standard background (see Assumptions).
- one-way function, trapdoor one-way function, secret key (p. 109).

Misconception: "ERM over \(\mathcal H\) is NP-hard, so \(\mathcal H\) is not efficiently learnable". Why wrong: it only rules out proper learners (under \(RP \ne NP\)); 3-term DNF is efficiently learnable through 3-CNF.
Second misconception: "realizable and agnostic are the same problem". Why wrong: statistically yes (finite VC dimension in both), computationally no: \(O(nm)\) against NP-hard for rectangles and conjunctions.
Third misconception: "a smaller class is always cheaper". Why wrong: 3-term DNF is smaller than 3-CNF and cheaper in labels (2,070 against 4,746,098 at \(n = 30\)) but intractable for proper learners.

Derivation:
- Asymptotic runtime, feasibility, why not \(m\) (pp. 100 to 102); sequences of problems for rectangles (p. 102); the lazy learner (p. 102); Definition 8.1 (pp. 102 to 103); finite classes with \(|\mathcal H_n| = n\) vs \(2^n\) (p. 103).
- ERM rule and exhaustive search cost \(k|\mathcal H|m\), C++ programs of 10,000 bits (p. 104). The book writes the sample complexity as \(c(10{,}000 + \log(c/\delta))/\varepsilon^c\) (log base 2 for the size term); the prose gives \(10{,}000 \log 2 \approx 6{,}931\) with natural logs.
- Tightest box: the book says "easy to verify"; the prose gives the full proof (positives inside by construction; box inside any consistent rectangle; negatives outside). The no-positive case is ours.
- Agnostic rectangles: NP-hardness and constant-factor hardness cited (Ben-David, Eiron & Long 2003), not proved. Subset search: the book states "determined by at most \(2n\) examples" and runtime \(m^{O(n)}\); the full proof (tightest box of the points \(h\) labels 1, \(B \subseteq h\), \(2n\) witnesses) and the injection bound \(\sum_{k \le K}\binom mk \le (m+1)^K\) are ours.
- Conjunctions: \(|\mathcal H_C^n| \le 3^n + 1\), sample complexity \(n \log(3/\delta)/\varepsilon\) (p. 106; the prose proves it for \(n \ge 2\), \(\delta \le 1/2\)); the elimination algorithm and its proof (pp. 106 to 107), with the induction for "most restrictive" spelled out; agnostic hardness stated (p. 107).
- 3-term DNF: \(|\mathcal H| \le 3^{3n}\) in the book, correct as a count of functions (a term that is all-negative can be replaced by a copy of another term); the prose writes the looser but valid \((3^n+1)^3\) (counting triples of formulas) and says "about \(3n \log(3/\delta)/\varepsilon\)". Act 2's 2,070 uses \(3n \log 3\), the book's count; with \((3^n+1)^3\) the ceiling is still 2,070. Hardness unless \(RP = NP\) (Pitt & Valiant 1988, Kearns et al. 1994), reduction from graph 3-colouring, stated not proved (p. 107).
- 3-CNF: distributivity identity (p. 108, proof ours, both directions plus the empty-term corner), \(\psi\), sample complexity: book says "at most \(n^3 \log(1/\delta)/\varepsilon\)", which is wrong as an "at most" (at \(n = 30\), \(\delta = 0.01\) the finite-class bound's numerator is 237,305 against the book's 124,340), right only as an order; the prose gives the explicit \(8n^3 \log(3/\delta)/\varepsilon\) and "of order \(n^3 \log(1/\delta)/\varepsilon\)". The recap says \(O(n^3)\) examples in place of \(O(n)\) (the ratio at \(n = 30\) is 2,293, about \((8/3)n^2\)).
- Hardness via cryptography (pp. 108 to 110): one-way and trapdoor definitions as in the book; the book's argument is a short paragraph; the prose fills in \(\varepsilon = \delta = 1/4\), the \(9/16\) success probability and the contradiction with \(p(n) = 2\). Small Boolean circuits remark (Kearns & Vazirani 1994, ch. 6).

Concept check, answers (never shown to the learner; critique against these):
1. No junk examples: no literal is ever crossed out, so it returns \(h_0\), the conjunction of all \(2n\) literals, which says keep to every message (it contains \(x_i \wedge \neg x_i\)). It is consistent: every example is keep. (Its true risk is the probability of junk, which can be large: consistent is not correct.) With 1100 labelled both junk and keep: the junk copy crosses out literals as usual, giving at most F L ¬U ¬N, which says junk to 1100; the keep copy is never looked at. The learner does not notice; its output errs on the keep copy, so \(L_S \ge 1/m\). Since no hypothesis can be right on both copies, nothing does better on those two; but in general without realizability the output can be far from an ERM (one mislabelled junk example deletes truth literals). Look for: "keep examples are never read".
2. With \(A_2\) empty: the second direction assumes the DNF is 0, which is impossible because the empty conjunction is always 1; the step "pick a false literal \(v^\star \in A_2\)" has nothing to pick, but the case never arises. The right side has an empty index set (no \(v \in A_2\)), so the AND of no clauses is 1, matching the left side, which is 1. With \(A_1 = x_1 \wedge \neg x_1\): \(A_1\) is always 0, the identity still holds; for every \(x\) one of \(x_1, \neg x_1\) is false, so \(u^\star\) exists; the right side contains clauses \((x_1 \vee v \vee w)\) and \((\neg x_1 \vee v \vee w)\), whose AND equals \(v \vee w\) for each pair, so the right side equals \(A_2 \vee A_3\). Look for: separating the vacuous case from the false-literal choice.
3. \(n = 4\): 3-CNF \(512 \log 3 \approx 562.5\); memorizer \(16 \log 2 \approx 11.1\): the memorizer needs far fewer. Crossover: \(8n^3 \log 3\) vs \(2^n \log 2\): \(n = 15\): 29,663 vs 22,713 (memorizer smaller); \(n = 16\): 35,999 vs 45,426 (3-CNF smaller). Smallest \(n\) is 16. At \(n = 8\): 3-CNF \(4096 \log 3 \approx 4{,}500\) vs \(256 \log 2 \approx 177\): in the eight-feature world the 3-CNF class is huge relative to the 256 messages, so 25 examples are far too few; the 0.402 is expected and is not a failure of the method; the guarantee is the wrong tool at that size (any rule is cheaper there). Look for: the class size argument, not "the learner is bad".

End-of-chapter problems (§8.7, PDF pp. 110 to 113). In the prose they appear as the lesson's own Problems 1 to 3: Problem 1 = Exercise 1, Problem 2 = Exercise 3 part 2, Problem 3 = Exercise 4. Hints one rung at a time; never the solution.
- Exercise 1 (agnostic ERM for intervals in \(O(m^2)\)), standard.
  1. Sort the examples by \(x\) (cost \(O(m \log m)\)). An interval's labelling of \(S\) is decided by which contiguous block of sorted points it covers. How many blocks are there?
  2. For a block \(i..j\), the errors are (keep inside the block) + (junk outside it). Can you write that with prefix sums of junk and keep counts?
  3. With prefix sums, each block costs \(O(1)\); there are \(O(m^2)\) blocks. Do not forget the empty interval and ties in \(x\) (equal coordinates must be in or out together). The "dynamic programming" is the prefix-sum table.
- Exercise 3 part 2 (intersections of \(k\) halfspaces, NP-hard via graph \(k\)-colouring), standard but long.
  1. Zero error implies colourable: given \(h = \bigcap_j h_j\) with zero error, each \(e_i\) is negative, so some \(h_j(e_i) = -1\); colour \(v_i\) by the smallest such \(j\). Suppose an edge \((v_i, v_l)\) gets colour \(j\). What does \(h_j\) say about \(e_i\), \(e_l\) and their midpoint?
  2. The set where \(h_j\) is negative is a halfspace, hence convex; the midpoint of two negatives of \(h_j\) is negative for \(h_j\), so for \(h\), but it is labelled positive. Contradiction.
  3. Colourable implies zero error: use the book's hint, \(b = 0.6\), \(w_{t,i} = -1\) if \(f(v_i) = t\) and 0 otherwise (with the convention sign\((\langle w, x\rangle + b)\)). Check a vertex \(e_i\) of colour \(t\): \(-1 + 0.6 < 0\). Check an edge midpoint for every \(t\): at most one endpoint has colour \(t\), so the sum is at least \(-0.5 + 0.6 > 0\). Then: \(m = |V| + |E|\), the construction is polynomial, and \(k\)-colouring is NP-hard for \(k \ge 3\) (Karp 1972).
- Exercise 4 (efficient proper PAC learning puts ERM in RP), starred in spirit (no star in the book).
  1. Given \(S\), put the uniform distribution on \(S\) and run the proper learner \(A\) with \(\varepsilon = 1/|S|\) and a \(\delta\) giving success probability at least 0.3 (the book's hint; any constant works by the RP footnote). What does true risk below \(1/|S|\) under the uniform distribution on \(S\) mean for empirical risk?
  2. Answer yes iff the returned \(h \in \mathcal H\) has \(L_S(h) = 0\) (checkable in polynomial time). If the true answer is no, can you ever say yes? If yes, how often do you say yes?
  3. Runtime is polynomial in \(n\), \(1/\varepsilon = |S|\) and \(1/\delta\), so polynomial in the input. Amplify the constant to \(1/2\) by repetition. Conclusion: if ERM\(_\mathcal H\) (the zero-error decision version) is NP-hard, NP \(\subseteq\) RP, so NP = RP.
