SGD Variants and Faster Rates

Lesson text (draft) · Teacher's sheet

The filter the auditor signed

Lesson 14a ended with a filter that is no longer a day behind. It learns from the morning’s fresh labels, one message per step, and its guarantee is about the true risk itself. Then the auditor from lesson 13b came back with the certificate she had signed, and it was for a different filter.

Her filter is lesson 13’s regularized filter: the weight vector that minimizes the penalized objective on the audited training sequence, the two million labelled messages the team keeps on file, with a penalty weight of λ=0.01\lambda = 0.01λ=0.01. She can check that filter against the audited messages. The streaming filter is a different learner, and its guarantee is about a stream nobody kept. So she has made an offer. She does not need the exact minimizer. She will sign any filter whose regularized objective is, on average, within 0.05 of the exact minimum.

The trouble is the solver. The exact solver for that objective takes six hours on two million messages, which is how this whole story started. Lesson 14a warned against the obvious shortcut, looping SGD over a fixed archive, because it breaks the fresh-message guarantee for the true risk. But here we do not want the true risk. We want the minimum of one fixed function of the audited messages. Can SGD find it, one message per step, and can you certify how close it lands, before the six hours are up?

It can, and it does better than you might expect. The penalty does more than steady the filter, as lesson 13 showed: it curves the objective upward, and that curvature buys SGD a much faster rate than the 1/T1/\sqrt T1/T​ of lesson 14a. Getting there takes three relatives of SGD: a projection that keeps the iterates inside the class, a step size that shrinks as the walk goes on, and a rate for strongly convex functions. Along the way we also meet smooth losses, where the rate improves when the best filter is good.

Variants

The SGD of lesson 14a has a few useful relatives. Each comes with the same style of guarantee, and one of them buys a much faster rate.

### Adding a projection step

The analysis compared SGD with the best w⋆\mathbf w^\starw⋆ of norm at most BBB, the class H={w:∥w∥≤B}\mathcal H = \{\mathbf w : \|\mathbf w\| \le B\}H={w:∥w∥≤B}. But nothing stops the iterates from wandering outside that ball, and then wˉ\bar{\mathbf w}wˉ need not be in H\mathcal HH either. If the class is part of your inductive bias, you want the output inside it. The fix is a two-part update:

  1. w(t+12)=w(t)−ηvt\mathbf w^{(t + \frac12)} = \mathbf w^{(t)} - \eta \mathbf v_tw(t+21​)=w(t)−ηvt​,

The half in the superscript marks a halfway point, the ordinary step before any correction. The second part is the projection onto H\mathcal HH: argmin⁡w∈H\operatorname{argmin}_{\mathbf w \in \mathcal H}argminw∈H​ picks the point of H\mathcal HH closest to the halfway point. In plain words: take the usual step, then pull back to the nearest point of the class. ### Variable step size

Instead of a fixed η\etaη, you can shrink the step as you go, ηt=B/(ρt)\eta_t = B/(\rho\sqrt t)ηt​=B/(ρt​). The intuition: early on you are far from the minimum and big steps help; later, when you are close, careful steps avoid overshooting it. This schedule achieves a bound similar to the SGD guarantee, and it has a practical advantage: it does not need to know TTT in advance. You will prove it in Problem 2.

### Other averaging

The output need not be the plain average of all iterates. Alternatives include returning w(t)\mathbf w^{(t)}w(t) for a random t∈[T]t \in [T]t∈[T] (one of the steps 1,…,T1, \dots, T1,…,T), averaging only the last αT\alpha TαT iterates for some α∈(0,1)\alpha \in (0, 1)α∈(0,1), or a weighted average of the last few. These schemes can converge faster in some situations, notably for the strongly convex functions we turn to now.

### Strongly convex functions

Recall from lesson 13 that a strongly convex function sags below each of its chords by at least a fixed multiple of the chord’s squared length. Lesson 13 wrote the constant as σ\sigmaσ; here we call it λ\lambdaλ, because below it will be the regularization weight. So fff is λ\lambdaλ-strongly convex when, for all w,u\mathbf w, \mathbf uw,u and α∈(0,1)\alpha \in (0, 1)α∈(0,1),

The regularized solver as code

Here is the solver the auditor’s filter will run. It uses the closed form, so it never stores more than one running sum of subgradients: each step adds the drawn message’s hinge subgradient to the sum, and the new iterate is minus that sum over λt\lambda tλt.

SGD-RLM(S, λ, T):
  w ← 0 in R^d;  sum ← 0 in R^d;  total ← 0 in R^d
  for t = 1..T:
    total ← total + w
    (x, y) ← a message drawn uniformly at random from S
    if y ⟨w, x⟩ < 1:
      sum ← sum − y · x
    w ← −sum / (λ t)
  return w̄ = total / T

What people get wrong

“Projecting the iterates breaks the guarantee, because the walk stops following the subgradients.” Projecting onto a closed convex set never moves a point further from anything inside the set, including the target w⋆\mathbf w^\starw⋆. So the per-step identity becomes an inequality in the right direction, and every bound survives.

“Any shrinking step size gets the log⁡(T)/T\log(T)/Tlog(T)/T rate.” The fast rate needs a strongly convex function and the schedule ηt=1/(λt)\eta_t = 1/(\lambda t)ηt​=1/(λt) matched to its curvature. On a merely convex function, the shrinking schedule B/(ρt)B/(\rho\sqrt t)B/(ρt​) still gives only a rate of order 1/T1/\sqrt T1/T​ (the second problem below). And if you claim more curvature than the function has, using a λ\lambdaλ larger than the true one, the telescoping in the proof leaves positive terms behind and the bound is lost.

“The fast rate means SGD learns from fewer fresh messages.” The log⁡(T)/T\log(T)/Tlog(T)/T rate is about reaching the minimum of a strongly convex objective, such as the regularized objective on a fixed training sequence. How good that minimum is as a filter is lesson 13’s question, with its own price for the penalty. For the true risk of a convex-Lipschitz-bounded problem, the count is still B2ρ2/ε2B^2\rho^2/\varepsilon^2B2ρ2/ε2 fresh messages.

“The measured gap is far below the certificate, so fewer steps would do.” The measurement is one world and 100 runs. The certificate holds for every training sequence the auditor could hand you. A filter ships on what you can prove, not on how one test went.
