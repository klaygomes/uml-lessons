The committee nobody can certify

The filter has changed a great deal since the first lesson, and this week it changed again. Every message now arrives with a junk score, a number between 0 and 1 computed upstream by the rest of the filter: the higher the score, the more the message looks like junk. The last step of the filter is the simplest one in the whole system. It picks a number ttt, the threshold, and says junk exactly when the score is at least ttt. The team writes thresholds with nine decimal places, from 0.000000000 to 0.999999999, so there are a billion possible thresholds to choose from.

[diagram:dial: built on the lesson page]

Two things about this week are new, the two your team asked for at the end of the last lesson, and both of them come from good instincts.

The first is trust. Long before this week’s labels arrived, the team wrote down which thresholds it believed in. Last year’s threshold was 0.60, tuned on mail from the senders everyone already knew to be bad, and in all the years of records the right threshold has never drifted more than 0.05 in a year. So the team put 99% of its trust on the band from 0.55 to 0.65, spread evenly, and the last 1% evenly over everything else. They also like short thresholds, the round ones you can write on a whiteboard, such as 0.60 rather than 0.613527118. Trust of this kind is exactly the inductive bias of lesson 1, written down as numbers before any data arrives.

The second is randomness. Remember the randomized filter of lesson 21b, which answered by a coin flip so that a spammer could not predict it? The team has adopted the same habit. After looking at this week’s 650 labelled messages, they noticed that the threshold making the fewest mistakes on them sits near 0.62. Instead of shipping one threshold, they now ship a whole window of them: for every incoming message, the filter draws a threshold uniformly at random from the 40 million thresholds between 0.60 and 0.64, and uses that one. A spammer who probes the filter learns nothing sharp about where the line is. Each individual answer still comes from one readable rule, so any decision can be explained. The team calls the window the committee.

Then the reviewer from lesson 7a arrives with the same deal as before. The filter ships only with a certificate: a number that, except with probability δ=0.05\delta = 0.05δ=0.05, bounds the true risk from above, the chance that the filter mislabels the next message. The certificate must be at most 0.13, the trust must have been written down before the labels were drawn (it was), and the label budget is the 650 messages already labelled.

So you try every certificate the course has built. On this week’s labels the committee errs on 2.94% of the examples, and the single best threshold on 1.69%.

Look at what went wrong. Lesson 4’s certificate can average over a mix, but it cannot use trust. Lesson 7a’s can use trust, but only one hypothesis at a time, and the trust is spread so thinly over the band that no single threshold carries much of it. Yet the team’s trust is anything but thin: 99% of it sits in a band that holds the committee’s whole window. What the team needs is a certificate for a mix chosen after seeing the data, one that charges for the trust the mix actually uses, rather than for the trust of any one member.

How does trust written down before the data turn into a guarantee for a mix chosen after it? That is the question of this lesson, the last of the course, and its answer is called the PAC-Bayes bound.

A small rulebook

A billion thresholds is too many to reason about by hand, so let’s keep only the five round ones: 0.50, 0.55, 0.60, 0.65 and 0.70. For each threshold ttt, the hypothesis hth_tht​ says junk exactly when the score is at least ttt. In symbols, writing sss for a message’s score and 1[⋅]\mathbb 1[\cdot]1[⋅] for the indicator (1 when the statement inside is true, 0 when it is false),

ht(s)=1[s≥t].h_t(s) = \mathbb 1[s \ge t].ht​(s)=1[s≥t].

In plain words: hth_tht​ says junk (1) when the score reaches the threshold, and keep (0) otherwise. A message scored 0.63 is junk for h0.60h_{0.60}h0.60​ and keep for h0.65h_{0.65}h0.65​.

We need a world in which we know the truth, so that we can check every claim. Here is ours. Scores spread evenly over [0,1][0, 1][0,1], the interval of numbers from 0 to 1: every score is equally likely. Users follow a true cut at 0.62, so a message is junk exactly when its score is at least 0.62, except that 2% of the labels are flipped, because users misclick or disagree. The learner knows none of this.

What is the true risk of hth_tht​? Recall from lesson 1 that the true risk LD(h)L_{\mathcal D}(h)LD​(h) is the chance that hhh mislabels a fresh message drawn from the distribution D\mathcal DD of messages. A message whose score lies between ttt and 0.62 gets the opposite of the true cut’s answer from hth_tht​, so hth_tht​ is wrong on it unless the label was flipped: chance 0.98. Everywhere else hth_tht​ agrees with the true cut, so it is wrong only on a flipped label: chance 0.02. The stretch between ttt and 0.62 has length ∣t−0.62∣|t - 0.62|∣t−0.62∣, the distance between the two numbers, so

LD(ht)=0.02 (1−∣t−0.62∣)+0.98 ∣t−0.62∣=0.02+0.96 ∣t−0.62∣.L_{\mathcal D}(h_t) = 0.02\,(1 - |t - 0.62|) + 0.98\,|t - 0.62| = 0.02 + 0.96\,|t - 0.62|.LD​(ht​)=0.02(1−∣t−0.62∣)+0.98∣t−0.62∣=0.02+0.96∣t−0.62∣.

In plain words: each threshold pays the 2% of flipped labels everywhere, plus 96 points of error for every unit of distance to the true cut. A filter that rolls a die

Suppose the team builds a small committee here too: 60% of the time it uses h0.60h_{0.60}h0.60​, and 40% of the time h0.65h_{0.65}h0.65​. Each message gets its own fresh draw. How often is this filter wrong?

Take one message. With probability 0.6 it is judged by h0.60h_{0.60}h0.60​, which is wrong on a random message with probability 0.0392; with probability 0.4 it is judged by h0.65h_{0.65}h0.65​, wrong with probability 0.0488. Adding the two cases, the chance of a mistake is 0.6×0.0392+0.4×0.0488=0.04300.6 \times 0.0392 + 0.4 \times 0.0488 = 0.04300.6×0.0392+0.4×0.0488=0.0430. The randomized filter’s error is the weighted average of its members’ errors, with the committee’s weights.

Now the names. A posterior is a probability distribution QQQ over the hypothesis class H\mathcal HH: a number Q(h)≥0Q(h) \ge 0Q(h)≥0 for each hypothesis, adding up to 1. Here Q(h0.60)=0.6Q(h_{0.60}) = 0.6Q(h0.60​)=0.6, Q(h0.65)=0.4Q(h_{0.65}) = 0.4Q(h0.65​)=0.4, and QQQ is 0 on the other three. The word comes from Bayesian statistics, but nothing Bayesian is required: a posterior is any distribution over H\mathcal HH you like, chosen however you like, after looking at the labels or not. The Gibbs predictor of QQQ is the randomized filter that, for each new message, draws a hypothesis hhh at random with probabilities QQQ and answers h(x)h(x)h(x).

In plain words: the loss of the randomized filter on one example is the chance that the hypothesis it draws gets that example wrong. LD(Q)=Eh∼Q[LD(h)],LS(Q)=Eh∼Q[LS(h)].L_{\mathcal D}(Q) = \mathbb E_{h \sim Q}\big[L_{\mathcal D}(h)\big], \qquad L_S(Q) = \mathbb E_{h \sim Q}\big[L_S(h)\big].LD​(Q)=Eh∼Q​[LD​(h)],LS​(Q)=Eh∼Q​[LS​(h)].

In plain words: the risks of a Gibbs predictor are the posterior-weighted averages of its members’ risks. On this week’s labels, LS(Q)=0.6×0.0415+0.4×0.0431=0.0422L_S(Q) = 0.6 \times 0.0415 + 0.4 \times 0.0431 = 0.0422LS​(Q)=0.6×0.0415+0.4×0.0431=0.0422, and the true risk is the 0.0430 we found above. Python can check both: the first snippet runs the randomized filter on 200,000 fresh messages and compares its mistake rate with the formula.

import random

    y = int(s >= 0.62) ^ (rng.random() < 0.02)
    t = rng.choices(rules, Q)[0]

One consequence is worth saying out loud now, because it surprises people later. A Gibbs predictor is never better than its best member: an average of numbers is at least their minimum. The committee’s true risk, 0.0430, is above that of h0.60h_{0.60}h0.60​, 0.0392. Mixing does not lower the error. What it can lower, as we are about to see, is the price of the certificate.

Trust before, belief after

The team’s trust is also a distribution over the class, and it gets its own name. A prior is a probability distribution PPP over H\mathcal HH, a number P(h)≥0P(h) \ge 0P(h)≥0 for each hypothesis with ∑hP(h)=1\sum_{h} P(h) = 1∑h​P(h)=1, that is fixed before the training sequence is drawn. Its value P(h)P(h)P(h) is the prior score of hhh. In the small rulebook, P(h0.60)=0.4P(h_{0.60}) = 0.4P(h0.60​)=0.4 and P(h0.50)=0.1P(h_{0.50}) = 0.1P(h0.50​)=0.1.

So the two distributions differ only in when they are chosen. The prior is written down before any labels exist, like lesson 7a’s weights; the posterior is chosen afterwards, and may depend on the labels in any way at all. Lesson 7a’s weight functions are almost priors already: they had to sum to at most 1, and a prior sums to exactly 1, so you can turn weights into a prior by dividing each one by their total, which only increases each weight.

[diagram:prior-posterior: built on the lesson page]

Why must the prior come first? You have seen the answer twice. In lesson 1, a hypothesis class chosen after looking at the labels can always be made to contain a hypothesis that fits them, coincidences and all. In lesson 7a, weights tuned to the labels void the union bound. The prior is the same commitment in its most flexible form: instead of a class or a list of weights, a whole distribution of belief, and it is only worth something if it was made before the evidence.

The posterior has no such restriction, and that is the point of this lesson. The theorem we are about to prove holds for every posterior at once, including one picked by staring at the labels, as the committee was. The price is that the certificate charges for how far the posterior moved away from the prior. So we need a way to measure that distance.

How far did the committee move?

The committee puts 0.6 on h0.60h_{0.60}h0.60​, where the prior put 0.4: it believes in that threshold 1.5 times more than the team did before the labels. On h0.65h_{0.65}h0.65​ it puts 0.4 against a prior 0.2, twice as much. On the other three it puts nothing, which costs nothing: moving belief away from a hypothesis is free. Moving belief towards one is what costs.

How should those ratios be combined? Ratios multiply, so we take logarithms, which turn products into sums: log⁡1.5=0.405\log 1.5 = 0.405log1.5=0.405 and log⁡2=0.693\log 2 = 0.693log2=0.693. Then we average them, weighting each by how often the committee actually uses that hypothesis: 0.6×0.405+0.4×0.693=0.5210.6 \times 0.405 + 0.4 \times 0.693 = 0.5210.6×0.405+0.4×0.693=0.521. That number is the relative entropy.

Definition (relative entropy). For distributions QQQ and PPP over a countable class H\mathcal HH (finite, or listable as h1,h2,…h_1, h_2, \dotsh1​,h2​,…), the relative entropy of QQQ to PPP is

D(Q∥P)=Eh∼Q[log⁡Q(h)P(h)]=∑h : Q(h)>0Q(h)log⁡Q(h)P(h),D(Q \| P) = \mathbb E_{h \sim Q}\Big[\log \frac{Q(h)}{P(h)}\Big] = \sum_{h \,:\, Q(h) > 0} Q(h) \log \frac{Q(h)}{P(h)},D(Q∥P)=Eh∼Q​[logP(h)Q(h)​]=h:Q(h)>0∑​Q(h)logP(h)Q(h)​,

and D(Q∥P)=+∞D(Q \| P) = +\inftyD(Q∥P)=+∞ if some hhh has Q(h)>0Q(h) > 0Q(h)>0 but P(h)=0P(h) = 0P(h)=0.

Read it from the inside out.

  • Q(h)/P(h)Q(h)/P(h)Q(h)/P(h) is the factor by which the posterior’s belief in hhh exceeds the prior’s: 1.5 for h0.60h_{0.60}h0.60​ in the committee.
  • log⁡\loglog is the natural logarithm, so a factor above 1 gives a positive number, a factor below 1 a negative one, and a factor of exactly 1 gives 0.
  • Eh∼Q[⋅]\mathbb E_{h \sim Q}[\cdot]Eh∼Q​[⋅] averages over a hypothesis drawn from the posterior. The sum on the right writes that average out: the colon in ∑h : Q(h)>0\sum_{h \,:\, Q(h) > 0}∑h:Q(h)>0​ reads “such that”, so we add over the hypotheses hhh such that Q(h)>0Q(h) > 0Q(h)>0.   • The double bar ∥\|∥ just separates the two arguments, and their order matters: the average is taken under the first one, QQQ.
  • The infinite case: if the posterior uses a hypothesis that the prior ruled out completely, the ratio is a division by zero and no finite price can pay for it.

In plain words: the relative entropy is the posterior’s average log-factor of belief beyond the prior, averaged over how the posterior actually draws. It was introduced in lesson 24a, written DRED_{\mathrm{RE}}DRE​ there, as the extra surprise of believing one distribution when the truth is another; it is also called the Kullback–Leibler divergence. Since D(Q∥P)D(Q\|P)D(Q∥P) is an expectation, you can also estimate it the way you would estimate any average: draw many hypotheses from QQQ, compute log⁡(Q(h)/P(h))\log(Q(h)/P(h))log(Q(h)/P(h)) for each, and average. The snippet below computes it both ways.

Three facts make the relative entropy usable as a price.

It is never negative, and it is 0 exactly when Q=PQ = PQ=P. Lesson 24a proved this for finite sets, and the proof carries over word for word to countable ones. Its engine is the inequality log⁡u≤u−1\log u \le u - 1logu≤u−1 for every u>0u > 0u>0, with equality only at u=1u = 1u=1: apply it to u=P(h)/Q(h)u = P(h)/Q(h)u=P(h)/Q(h) inside the sum. So the price is zero when you do not update your belief at all.

A posterior that keeps the prior’s proportions on a set pays the log of one over the prior’s mass on that set. Here is the precise version.

The PAC-Bayes theorem

Here is the guarantee. It reads like lesson 4’s and lesson 7a’s, with the cost of the posterior in place of the cost of the class.

Theorem (PAC-Bayes). Let D\mathcal DD be any distribution over examples, H\mathcal HH a countable hypothesis class, ℓ:H×Z→[0,1]\ell: \mathcal H \times Z \to [0, 1]ℓ:H×Z→[0,1] a loss, PPP a prior over H\mathcal HH, δ∈(0,1)\delta \in (0, 1)δ∈(0,1) and m≥2m \ge 2m≥2. Then with probability at least 1−δ1 - \delta1−δ over an i.i.d. training sequence S∼DmS \sim \mathcal D^mS∼Dm, every posterior QQQ over H\mathcal HH, including any that depends on SSS, satisfies

LD(Q)≤LS(Q)+D(Q∥P)+log⁡(m/δ)2(m−1).L_{\mathcal D}(Q) \le L_S(Q) + \sqrt{\frac{D(Q \| P) + \log(m/\delta)}{2(m - 1)}}.LD​(Q)≤LS​(Q)+2(m−1)D(Q∥P)+log(m/δ)​​.

Read it from the inside out.

  • ℓ:H×Z→[0,1]\ell: \mathcal H \times Z \to [0, 1]ℓ:H×Z→[0,1] says the loss takes a hypothesis and an example (from the set ZZZ of all examples) and returns a number between 0 and 1; the 0-1 loss qualifies.
  • S∼DmS \sim \mathcal D^mS∼Dm reads “SSS is drawn from Dm\mathcal D^mDm”: mmm examples, each drawn from D\mathcal DD, independently of each other (i.i.d., lesson 1). The probability 1−δ1 - \delta1−δ is over that draw.
  • δ∈(0,1)\delta \in (0, 1)δ∈(0,1) says δ\deltaδ is strictly between 0 and 1: the chance of failure you accept, 0.05 for the reviewer.
  • D(Q∥P)D(Q\|P)D(Q∥P) is the price of the posterior; log⁡(m/δ)\log(m/\delta)log(m/δ) is a price for the confidence, larger than the log⁡(2/δ)\log(2/\delta)log(2/δ) that lessons 4 and 7a paid for one fixed hypothesis (9.47 against 3.69 here), because it grows with mmm.
  • Dividing by 2(m−1)2(m - 1)2(m−1) and taking the square root turns the total price into a margin that shrinks like one over the square root of mmm.
  • “Every posterior QQQ” is the key phrase. The bad event, the unlucky training sequences, is fixed before any posterior is chosen, and outside it the inequality holds for all of them simultaneously. So you may look at SSS, pick any QQQ, and the certificate still holds.

In plain words: except on a fraction δ\deltaδ of unlucky training sequences, every randomized filter’s true risk is at most its empirical risk plus a margin that grows with how far its posterior moved from the prior. Four tools for the proof

The proof uses four facts about averages. Two of them you met in lesson 4; the other two are new.

Markov’s inequality (lesson 4). For a random variable Z≥0Z \ge 0Z≥0 and any a>0a > 0a>0,

P[Z≥a]≤E[Z]a.\mathbb P[Z \ge a] \le \frac{\mathbb E[Z]}{a}.P[Z≥a]≤aE[Z]​.

P[⋅]\mathbb P[\cdot]P[⋅] is the probability of the event in brackets and E[Z]\mathbb E[Z]E[Z] the average of ZZZ. In plain words: a nonnegative quantity is rarely many times its own average. If ZZZ averages 2, it reaches 40 at most a twentieth of the time. (The proof: Z≥a⋅1[Z≥a]Z \ge a \cdot \mathbb 1[Z \ge a]Z≥a⋅1[Z≥a] for every outcome; take averages.)

Jensen’s inequality, two forms. Lesson 14a met Jensen’s inequality for convex functions of finite averages. We need two cases of it, for any random variable that averages over a countable set, and both have one-line proofs.

E[log⁡Y]≤log⁡E[Y](Y>0),(E[X])2≤E[X2].\mathbb E[\log Y] \le \log \mathbb E[Y] \quad (Y > 0), \qquad \big(\mathbb E[X]\big)^2 \le \mathbb E[X^2].E[logY]≤logE[Y](Y>0),(E[X])2≤E[X2].

On the left, YYY is a positive random variable: the average of its logarithms is at most the logarithm of its average. On the right, XXX is any random variable with a finite average square: the square of its average is at most the average of its squares. In plain words: taking the log after averaging gives more than averaging the logs, and squaring after averaging gives less than averaging the squares. With YYY equal to 3 with probability 0.6 and 0.5 with probability 0.4, the average of the logs is 0.6log⁡3+0.4log⁡0.5=0.38190.6\log 3 + 0.4 \log 0.5 = 0.38190.6log3+0.4log0.5=0.3819, and the log of the average is log⁡2=0.6931\log 2 = 0.6931log2=0.6931.

The proof

Here is the plan. We cannot apply Hoeffding to the posterior directly, because the posterior may depend on SSS, and Hoeffding needs a hypothesis fixed in advance. The prior is fixed in advance. So we prove a single fact about the prior, that one particular average over the prior is rarely large, and then transfer it to every posterior at once with an inequality that holds for every training sequence, no probability involved. The transfer is where the relative entropy appears.

The PAC-Bayes rule

The theorem holds for every posterior simultaneously, so it suggests a learner: choose the posterior with the smallest certificate.

The PAC-Bayes rule. Given a prior PPP fixed in advance and a training sequence SSS, return

QS∈argmin⁡Q[LS(Q)+D(Q∥P)+log⁡(m/δ)2(m−1)],Q_S \in \operatorname*{argmin}_{Q} \bigg[ L_S(Q) + \sqrt{\frac{D(Q\|P) + \log(m/\delta)}{2(m-1)}} \bigg],QS​∈Qargmin​[LS​(Q)+2(m−1)D(Q∥P)+log(m/δ)​​],

And the certificate of the returned posterior is valid, even though the posterior was chosen by looking at SSS, because the theorem covered every posterior in advance.

Here is the learner, scanning the dial λ\lambdaλ over a grid and keeping the posterior with the lowest certificate, followed by the Gibbs predictor.

PACBayesRule(S, H, P, δ, Λ):
  m ← |S|
  for each h in H:
    L_S[h] ← (1/m) · Σ_i ℓ(h, z_i)
  best ← none
  for each λ in Λ:
    w[h] ← P(h) · exp(−λ · L_S[h])   for each h in H
    Q[h] ← w[h] / Σ_h' w[h']          for each h in H
    D ← Σ_{h : Q[h] > 0} Q[h] · log(Q[h] / P(h))
    c ← Σ_h Q[h] · L_S[h] + sqrt((D + log(m/δ)) / (2(m−1)))
    if best is none or c < best.c: best ← (λ, Q, c)
  return best

GibbsPredict(Q, x):
  draw h from H with probability Q[h]
  return h(x)

What people get wrong

“The posterior has to be Bayes’ posterior.” It doesn’t. The theorem holds for every distribution over the class, chosen in any way, and Bayes’ rule appears nowhere in the proof. The names come from the Bayesian habit of thinking “belief before, belief after”; the guarantee is pure PAC.

“A mix of rules makes fewer mistakes than its best member.” The Gibbs predictor’s true risk is a weighted average of its members’ true risks, so it is never below the best member’s. The committee’s 0.0296 is above 0.02, the risk of the perfect threshold. What a mix can lower is the certificate, because spreading belief over many hypotheses that the prior trusted together costs far less than pinning it on one.

“If the data show the prior was wrong, fix the prior.” Then it is no longer a prior. Step 3 of the proof pulled each P(h)P(h)P(h) out of an average over training sequences because it was a constant. A prior rewritten after seeing SSS is a function of SSS, and the step breaks. The honest move is to keep the prior and let the posterior move, paying the relative entropy for it.

“PAC-Bayes always beats the older bounds.” On a single hypothesis it is slightly looser: log⁡(m/δ)\log(m/\delta)log(m/δ) instead of log⁡(2/δ)\log(2/\delta)log(2/δ), and m−1m - 1m−1 instead of mmm. It wins when belief is spread over many hypotheses with little individual trust, as on the dial.

“A hypothesis the prior gave zero trust is merely expensive.” It is impossible to use. If the posterior gives it any weight, the relative entropy is infinite and the certificate says nothing. A prior should give every hypothesis you might ever want to use some trust, which is why the team left a 1% layer under the whole dial.
