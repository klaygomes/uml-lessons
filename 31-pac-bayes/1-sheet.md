# PAC-Bayes

Objective: after this, you can define a prior and a posterior over a hypothesis class, compute the true and empirical risk of a Gibbs (randomized) predictor, compute the relative entropy \(D(Q\|P)\) by hand, by formula and by simulation, prove the PAC-Bayes theorem step by step (one-sided Hoeffding, the exponential-moment lemma, the swap that needs the prior fixed in advance, Markov, the change-of-measure inequality, Jensen), recover the finite-class and description-length certificates as point-mass special cases, and run the PAC-Bayes rule over Gibbs posteriors with its cost.

Book: Chapter 31, Section 31.1 (PAC-Bayes bounds, pp. 415 to 417: prior, posterior, randomized prediction rule, \(\ell(Q, z)\), \(L_{\mathcal D}(Q)\), \(L_S(Q)\), Theorem 31.1 and its proof with Equations 31.1 to 31.6, Remark 31.1 on regularization, Equation 31.7), Section 31.2 (bibliographic remarks, p. 417: McAllester 1998 and later; not used in the prose), Section 31.3 (exercises, pp. 417 to 418). Tools: Markov's inequality (Appendix B.1, p. 422), Hoeffding's inequality (Appendix B.4, p. 425; one-sided form from its proof), Kraft inequality and the MDL bound (Chapter 7, §7.3), finite-class uniform convergence (Corollary 4.6), regularized risk minimization (Chapter 13). Relative entropy was introduced in lesson 24a (Chapter 24, as \(D_{\mathrm{RE}}\)); Jensen's inequality in lesson 14a.

Key words: prior (not "weights" for the new object; lesson 7a's weights are named as the precursor), posterior (not "belief distribution"; "the committee" is the team's nickname for their window posterior only), Gibbs predictor (not "randomized classifier", "ensemble", "vote", "majority"; "randomized filter" only when recalling lesson 21b), relative entropy (not "KL distance"; "Kullback–Leibler divergence" named once), certificate (the right side of a bound, as in lesson 7a), threshold (\(h_t\)), junk score (not "probability"), price (informal word for \(D(Q\|P)\) in the certificate), gap \(\Delta(h)\) and positive part \(\Delta^+(h)\), good event \(G\), change of measure, Gibbs posterior \(Q_\lambda\), temperature dial \(\lambda\), PAC-Bayes rule. Logarithms are natural and written \(\log\) (series convention; the book writes \(\ln\)).

Terms:
- junk score: a number in [0, 1] attached to each message by the upstream scorer; in the lesson's world it is uniform on [0, 1].
- threshold hypothesis \(h_t(s) = \mathbb 1[s \ge t]\); the class: a billion thresholds with nine decimals, or the small rulebook \(\{0.50, 0.55, 0.60, 0.65, 0.70\}\).
- prior \(P\): a distribution over \(\mathcal H\) fixed before \(S\) is drawn; \(P(h)\) is the prior score.
- posterior \(Q\): any distribution over \(\mathcal H\), possibly depending on \(S\).
- Gibbs predictor: for each message draw \(h \sim Q\) and answer \(h(x)\); \(\ell(Q, z) = \mathbb E_{h\sim Q}\ell(h, z)\), \(L_{\mathcal D}(Q) = \mathbb E_{h\sim Q}L_{\mathcal D}(h)\), \(L_S(Q) = \mathbb E_{h \sim Q}L_S(h)\).
- relative entropy \(D(Q\|P) = \sum_{Q(h)>0} Q(h)\log(Q(h)/P(h))\), \(+\infty\) if \(Q\) uses a hypothesis with \(P(h) = 0\).
- restricted prior \(Q_A = P(\cdot)/P(A)\) on \(A\): \(D(Q_A\|P) = \log(1/P(A))\); point mass: \(\log(1/P(h))\).
- PAC-Bayes certificate \(L_S(Q) + \sqrt{(D(Q\|P) + \log(m/\delta))/(2(m-1))}\).
- Gibbs posterior \(Q_\lambda(h) = P(h)e^{-\lambda L_S(h)}/Z_\lambda\).

Misconception 1: "a mix makes fewer mistakes than its best member". Why wrong: \(L_{\mathcal D}(Q)\) is a weighted average of member risks, so at least the minimum; the mix lowers the certificate, not the error (committee 0.0296 vs perfect threshold 0.02; small committee 0.0430 vs \(h_{0.60}\)'s 0.0392).
Misconception 2: "the posterior must be the Bayesian posterior". Why wrong: the theorem holds for every \(Q\); Bayes' rule is never used.
Misconception 3: "fix the prior after seeing the data". Why wrong: step 3 pulls \(P(h)\) out of \(\mathbb E_S\) because it is a constant.
Misconception 4: "PAC-Bayes always beats older bounds". Why wrong: for a point mass it pays \(\log(m/\delta)\) vs \(\log(2/\delta)\) and \(m-1\) vs \(m\) (0.1525 vs 0.137 for a billion hypotheses at 650 labels).
Misconception 5: "a zero-prior hypothesis is merely expensive". Why wrong: any posterior weight on it makes \(D = \infty\).

Derivation (prose order): change-of-measure plan; Step 1 one-sided Hoeffding for \(\Delta^+\); Step 2 moment lemma (layer cake, substitution \(t = e^{2(m-1)\varepsilon^2}\), exponents combine to \(-2\varepsilon^2\), \(\int 4\varepsilon e^{-2\varepsilon^2} = 1\)); Step 3 \(\Phi(S) = \mathbb E_P e^{2(m-1)\Delta^{+2}}\), swap with the prior constant, \(\mathbb E_S\Phi \le m\); Step 4 Markov at \(m/\delta\), good event \(G\); Step 5 change-of-measure lemma (Jensen on \(\log\), cancel \(Q\), add back the rest of the support; tight at the tilted posterior); Step 6 on \(G\) for all \(Q\); Step 7 Jensen for the square and \(\Delta \le \Delta^+\). The book applies Markov to \(e^{f(S)}\) with \(f(S) = \sup_Q[\dots]\) and bounds it by the prior expression (its Eq. 31.1 to 31.5); the lesson applies Markov to \(\Phi(S)\) directly and uses the change of measure deterministically, which is the same argument in a different order and avoids measurability questions about the supremum.

Algorithms:
- Gibbs predictor: draw \(h \sim Q\) by cumulative sums, \(O(|\mathcal H|)\) per draw, or \(O(\log|\mathcal H|)\) after \(O(|\mathcal H|)\) preprocessing; on the dial with a window posterior, \(O(1)\).
- PAC-Bayes rule (Remark 31.1 / Eq. 31.7) restricted to Gibbs posteriors, scanned over a grid \(\Lambda\) of \(G\) values: \(O(m|\mathcal H| + G|\mathcal H|)\) time, \(O(|\mathcal H|)\) extra space. Dial with window posteriors: sort scores \(O(m \log m)\), each window \(O(m)\).

Concept check, answers (never shown to the learner; critique against these):
1. \(P(h_{0.30}) = 0\) and \(Q(h_{0.30}) = 0.1 > 0\), so \(D(Q\|P) = +\infty\) and the certificate is infinite: it says nothing. Rewriting the prior after seeing \(S\) breaks Step 3: \(\mathbb E_S[\Phi(S)] = \sum_h P(h)\mathbb E_S[\dots]\) needs each \(P(h)\) to be a constant; a prior that depends on \(S\) cannot be pulled out of the expectation, so \(\mathbb E_S\Phi \le m\) and the good event \(G\) are no longer justified (the same failure as tuning lesson 7a's weights to the labels). The honest fixes: a prior written in advance with some trust everywhere (the team's 1% layer), or a new week of labels after updating the prior.
2. Hoeffding (Step 1) needs a hypothesis fixed before \(S\); a posterior's average \(\mathbb E_Q\) involves a \(Q\) that may depend on \(S\), so \(\mathbb E_S[\mathbb E_{Q_S}e^{g}]\) cannot be bounded by swapping (the weights are random). \(\Phi(S)\) uses the prior, whose weights are constants. The transfer to all \(Q\) happens in Step 6 (via the deterministic Step 5): \(G\) is fixed in Step 4 without reference to any \(Q\), and the change of measure holds for every \(Q\) and every \(S\). Positive part: the moment lemma needs \(X \ge 0\) and only an upper tail; \(\Delta(h)^2\) would also be large when the gap is very negative, which the one-sided Hoeffding bound does not control (using two-sided Hoeffding doubles the tail and gives \(2m - 1\) instead of \(m\), problem 1). Since \(\Delta \le \Delta^+\), the upper bound on the true risk survives. (The book uses \(\Delta^2\) with a one-sided tail; see Assumptions.)
3. Flat prior: \(D = 0.5\log(0.5/0.2) \times 2 = \log 2.5 = 0.9163\); \(L_S(Q) = 0.5 \times 0.0415 + 0.5 \times 0.0431 = 0.0423\); margin \(\sqrt{(0.9163 + 9.4727)/1298} = 0.0895\); certificate 0.1318. Team prior: \(D = 0.5\log(0.5/0.4) + 0.5\log(0.5/0.2) = 0.5697\); certificate 0.1303. The team prior is lower because it had already given 0.6 of its trust to the two thresholds the posterior uses (flat: 0.4), so less belief has to move; same empirical risk, smaller price.

Assumptions:
- Every claim about the chapter was checked against the PDF, pp. 415 to 418, by the writer (2026-11-20). Book-check agents not run (CPU rule).
- Book slip, repaired: the proof applies the one-sided Hoeffding bound \(\mathbb P[\Delta(h) \ge \varepsilon] \le e^{-2m\varepsilon^2}\) to conclude \(\mathbb E_S[e^{2(m-1)\Delta(h)^2}] \le m\) (via Exercise 1). For \(\Delta^2\), negative gaps also count, and a two-sided tail doubles the bound (to \(2m - 1\)). The lesson runs the proof with the positive part \(\Delta^+ = \max\{\Delta, 0\}\), which keeps the book's exact statement with \(\log(m/\delta)\), since \(\Delta \le \Delta^+\) and \(\mathbb E_Q\Delta^+ \le \sqrt{\mathbb E_Q\Delta^{+2}}\). Exercise 1 is stated for any \(X\) with a one-sided tail; it needs \(X \ge 0\) (or \(X^+\)), which the lesson's lemma assumes.
- Book's Equation 31.2: the last step is written as an equality, \(\mathbb E_Q[e^{g}P/Q] = \mathbb E_P[e^g]\); it is \(\le\) in general (equality when \(Q\) and \(P\) have the same support). The lesson writes \(\le\) and adds the case \(D = \infty\) and the requirement that \(Q(h) > 0\) implies \(P(h) > 0\).
- The book's theorem allows continuous \(\mathcal H\) with densities; the lesson states it for countable \(\mathcal H\) (sums only), which covers the billion thresholds. \(m \ge 2\) is required (the margin divides by \(m - 1\)); the book leaves it implicit.
- The proof's order is rearranged (Markov on \(\Phi(S)\), then the deterministic change of measure), equivalent to the book's \(f(S) = \sup_Q\) route.
