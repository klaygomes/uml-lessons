A threshold with no guarantee

The data team has shipped something you have wanted for a while. Their new scoring service reads a message, every word of it, and returns one number between 0 and 1: the message’s spam score. A high score means the message looks like junk. Your learner no longer has to choose among features. It has a single decision to make, and it is about where to draw a line. The new filter says:

junk if the spam score is above ttt.

Your users label messages as before, and the learner picks ttt. In testing it behaves beautifully. With a few dozen labels, the line lands within a whisker of where your users would have drawn it themselves.

Then comes the launch review. Since lesson 2, every launch has needed a guarantee: how many labels, and how sure can you be? Lesson 2 answered it for a finite list of rules. Lesson 1’s four one-feature rules, for instance, needed 44 labels to promise a true risk (the chance of mislabelling the next message) of at most 10%, on all but 5% of unlucky training sequences. So you open that finite-class guarantee,

m≥log⁡(∣H∣/δ)ε,m \ge \frac{\log(|\mathcal H|/\delta)}{\varepsilon},m≥εlog(∣H∣/δ)​,

and you stop. First, a reminder of what it says, read from the inside out.

  • H\mathcal HH is the hypothesis class, the list of rules the learner may choose from, and the bars ∣H∣|\mathcal H|∣H∣ mean “how many rules it has”.
  • δ\deltaδ (delta) is the failure chance you tolerate, 0.05 above, so ∣H∣/δ|\mathcal H|/\delta∣H∣/δ grows when the class gets bigger or when you ask to be surer.
  • log⁡\loglog is the natural logarithm, which turns a huge number into a modest one: log⁡(4/0.05)=log⁡80≈4.38\log(4/0.05) = \log 80 \approx 4.38log(4/0.05)=log80≈4.38.

Ignore the second number for now; it is where this lesson is heading. The first says 948 labels buy a true risk of at most 5% with probability at least 95%.

The reviewer is not impressed, for two reasons. The first: if the machine moved to 128-bit numbers, your guarantee would double, while the rule, the users and the messages stayed exactly the same. A number that changes with the hardware is measuring the hardware, not the problem. The second reason is sharper. Lesson 5 proved that the class of all functions on an infinite domain cannot be learned at all, by any learner, from any number of examples. That class is infinite too. So infinitely many hypotheses can make learning impossible, and you have no tool that tells your infinite class apart from that one. Launch blocked, until you can say why your class is different.

Yet you have watched thresholds learn. So something about a class matters other than the number of hypotheses in it. What is it? This lesson finds it, gives it a name, and proves that it decides, completely, which classes can be learned.

Thresholds learn anyway

First let’s show that the reviewer is wrong about thresholds in particular, by proving a guarantee directly.

What a class can do to a few messages

Take a small set of scores, say C={0.3,0.7}C = \{0.3, 0.7\}C={0.3,0.7}, and ask: in how many different ways can thresholds label these two messages? Each hypothesis gives a pair of labels, one per score. Different thresholds may give the same pair, and we only care about the distinct pairs. A cut-off of 0.5 gives (keep, junk), or (0,1)(0, 1)(0,1); a cut-off of 0.1 gives (1,1)(1, 1)(1,1); a cut-off of 0.9 gives (0,0)(0, 0)(0,0); and 0.55 or 0.4 give (0,1)(0, 1)(0,1) again, nothing new. The collection of distinct pairs is what we want to name.

Definition (restriction). Let H\mathcal HH be a class of functions from X\mathcal XX to {0,1}\{0, 1\}{0,1}, and let C={c1,…,cm}⊂XC = \{c_1, \dots, c_m\} \subset \mathcal XC={c1​,…,cm​}⊂X, a set of mmm points of the domain (⊂\subset⊂ reads “is a subset of”). HC={(h(c1),…,h(cm)):h∈H}⊆{0,1}m.\mathcal H_C = \{ (h(c_1), \dots, h(c_m)) : h \in \mathcal H \} \subseteq \{0, 1\}^m.HC​={(h(c1​),…,h(cm​)):h∈H}⊆{0,1}m.

Why is it enough to try a handful of thresholds when there are infinitely many? Because the labelling hth_tht​ gives to CCC depends only on which points of CCC lie above ttt. Two thresholds in the same gap between consecutive points give the same labelling, so one threshold per gap (below everything, between each neighbouring pair, at or above the top) produces every labelling there is. An infinite class, seen through two points, is just three labellings.

And look at which one is missing: (1,0)(1, 0)(1,0), junk at 0.3 and keep at 0.7. No threshold can do that, because a threshold that calls 0.3 junk calls everything above 0.3 junk too.

Definition (shattering). A class H\mathcal HH shatters a finite set C⊂XC \subset \mathcal XC⊂X when its restriction to CCC contains every labelling: ∣HC∣=2∣C∣|\mathcal H_C| = 2^{|C|}∣HC​∣=2∣C∣.

In plain words: the class shatters CCC when it can label the points of CCC in every possible way. Why should shattering matter? Think back to the No-Free-Lunch theorem of lesson 5. Its adversary took a set CCC of 2m2m2m messages and looked at all 22m2^{2m}22m ways to label them. For each labelling it built a distribution, uniform on CCC and labelled that way. A learner that has seen at most mmm of the 2m2m2m messages knows nothing about the labels of the others, and the adversary chose the labelling that made the learner pay. The conclusion: some distribution makes the learner’s true risk at least 1/81/81/8 with probability at least 1/71/71/7.

In the PAC model, though, the adversary is restricted: it may only use labellings that some h∈Hh \in \mathcal Hh∈H achieves with risk 0. On CCC, that means labellings in HC\mathcal H_CHC​. Now suppose H\mathcal HH shatters CCC. Then every labelling of CCC is in HC\mathcal H_CHC​, the restriction costs the adversary nothing, and the whole argument goes through inside the PAC model. In the statement, X×{0,1}\mathcal X \times \{0, 1\}X×{0,1} is the set of pairs (score, label), so D\mathcal DD chooses both the message and its label, and A(S)A(S)A(S) is the rule learner AAA returns after training on SSS.

There is an old saying behind this: an explanation that can explain anything explains nothing. If the class can label half the set any way at all and the other half any way at all, then the labels you have seen say nothing about the labels you have not.

The VC dimension

So the question to ask of a class is not “how many hypotheses?” but “how large a set can it shatter?”

Definition (VC dimension). The VC dimension of a class H\mathcal HH, written VCdim(H)\mathrm{VCdim}(\mathcal H)VCdim(H), is the largest size of a set C⊂XC \subset \mathcal XC⊂X that H\mathcal HH shatters. If H\mathcal HH shatters sets of every finite size, its VC dimension is infinite, written ∞\infty∞.

In plain words: the VC dimension is the most points the class can label in every possible way. Thresholds shatter one point and no pair, so their VC dimension is 1.

The name honours Vladimir Vapnik and Alexey Chervonenkis, who discovered this characterization in 1970. An immediate consequence of the corollary:

Theorem (infinite VC dimension means not PAC learnable). If VCdim(H)=∞\mathrm{VCdim}(\mathcal H) = \inftyVCdim(H)=∞, then H\mathcal HH is not PAC learnable.

Proof. Suppose some learner AAA PAC learns H\mathcal HH, and pick ε<1/8\varepsilon < 1/8ε<1/8 and δ<1/7\delta < 1/7δ<1/7. Let m=mH(ε,δ)m = m_{\mathcal H}(\varepsilon, \delta)m=mH​(ε,δ). Since the VC dimension is infinite, H\mathcal HH shatters some set of size 2m2m2m. By the corollary there is a realizable distribution on which, with probability at least 1/7>δ1/7 > \delta1/7>δ, AAA returns a hypothesis with true risk at least 1/8>ε1/8 > \varepsilon1/8>ε. That contradicts the PAC guarantee. ■\blacksquare■

The converse also holds: a finite VC dimension guarantees learnability. This lesson states it and puts it to work, and the next lesson proves it. But first let’s measure some classes. To prove VCdim(H)=d\mathrm{VCdim}(\mathcal H) = dVCdim(H)=d, you need two things, and they are not symmetric:

  1. Some set of size ddd is shattered by H\mathcal HH. One example is enough.
  2. Every set of size d+1d + 1d+1 fails to be shattered. One example is not enough; you need an argument that works for any set.

For thresholds, step 1 is any single point, and step 2 is the labelling (1,0)(1, 0)(1,0), missing for every pair c1<c2c_1 < c_2c1​<c2​. An infinite class with VC dimension 1: that is what you will tell the reviewer.

The fundamental theorem

We have shown one direction: infinite VC dimension rules out PAC learning. The converse is also true, and together they form the central result of this part of the course. It ties together every idea from lessons 2 to 5: learnability, ERM, uniform convergence and now the VC dimension.

First, uniform convergence from lesson 4, idea before symbols. In lesson 1, the rule “unknown sender means junk” got 25% of its labelled examples wrong but 50% of all mail: a gap of 0.25 between how good it looked and how good it was. A training sequence is ε\varepsilonε-representative when no rule in the class has a gap above ε\varepsilonε: every h∈Hh \in \mathcal Hh∈H has ∣LS(h)−LD(h)∣≤ε|L_S(h) - L_{\mathcal D}(h)| \le \varepsilon∣LS​(h)−LD​(h)∣≤ε. A class has the uniform convergence property when there is a sample size mHUC(ε,δ)m^{\mathrm{UC}}_{\mathcal H}(\varepsilon, \delta)mHUC​(ε,δ) such that, for every distribution D\mathcal DD, at least that many i.i.d. examples are ε\varepsilonε-representative with probability at least 1−δ1 - \delta1−δ. Two more reminders from lesson 3. The 0-1 loss charges 1 for a wrong label and 0 for a right one, so average loss is the share of mistakes. A class is agnostic PAC learnable when, with no assumption that any rule is perfect, some learner gets within ε\varepsilonε of the best rule in the class, with probability at least 1−δ1 - \delta1−δ.

The fundamental theorem of statistical learning. Let H\mathcal HH be a class of functions from a domain X\mathcal XX to {0,1}\{0, 1\}{0,1}, with the 0-1 loss. The following are equivalent:

  1. H\mathcal HH has the uniform convergence property.
  2. Any ERM rule is a successful agnostic PAC learner for H\mathcal HH.
  3. H\mathcal HH is agnostic PAC learnable.
  4. H\mathcal HH is PAC learnable.
  5. Any ERM rule is a successful PAC learner for H\mathcal HH.
  6. H\mathcal HH has a finite VC dimension.

Pause on how much this says. Six properties that look quite different, one statistical (uniform convergence), three about learners, one combinatorial, are the same property. In particular, if a binary classification problem can be learned at all, by any clever learner, then plain ERM learns it, and uniform convergence is the reason.

ERM on the score line, as code

The fundamental theorem says ERM is the learner to run. On the score line it is fast, and it handles the agnostic case, where labels may be noisy and no hypothesis is consistent. Here is ERM for thresholds and for intervals. Each takes a training sequence SSS of mmm examples (xi,yi)(x_i, y_i)(xi​,yi​).

ERM-Threshold(S):
  group S by score, sorted ascending: (x, junk count, keep count)
  t ← -∞;  errors ← number of keep examples;  best ← errors
  for each group (x, j, k) in order:
    errors ← errors + j - k
    if errors < best: best ← errors;  t ← x
  return h_t, best

  group S by score, sorted ascending: (x, junk count, keep count)
  best_gain ← 0;  best ← empty interval
  run_gain ← 0;  run_start ← none
  for each group (x, j, k) in order:
    w ← j - k
    if run_start is none or run_gain ≤ 0:
      run_gain ← w;  run_start ← x
    else:
      run_gain ← run_gain + w
    if run_gain > best_gain:
      best_gain ← run_gain;  best ← [run_start, x]
  return best, (number of junk examples) - best_gain

ERM-Threshold starts with the threshold below every score, where every keep example is a mistake, and slides it up one distinct score at a time. Passing a group of scores turns its junk examples into mistakes and its keep examples into correct answers, so the mistake count changes by j−kj - kj−k. Grouping equal scores matters: a threshold cannot separate two examples with the same score.

ERM-Interval uses a different trick. An interval [a,b][a, b][a,b] makes a mistake on every junk example outside it and every keep example inside it. So its mistake count is (all junk) minus (junk inside minus keep inside). Give each group the weight w=j−kw = j - kw=j−k; minimizing mistakes means finding the run of consecutive groups with the largest total weight, or the empty interval if every run is negative. That is the classic maximum-subarray scan: keep extending the current run while its total is positive, and restart when it is not.

Both cost O(mlog⁡m)O(m \log m)O(mlogm) time for the sort and O(m)O(m)O(m) for the scan, with O(m)O(m)O(m) space. In the realizable case there is an even simpler ERM for intervals: return the tightest interval, from the smallest junk score to the largest, in O(m)O(m)O(m) time without sorting. Compare that with lesson 2’s brute-force ERM over a finite class, O(m∣H∣)O(m |\mathcal H|)O(m∣H∣): here ∣H∣|\mathcal H|∣H∣ is infinite, and the algorithm works anyway, for the same reason the theory does. Only the O(m)O(m)O(m) and O(m2)O(m^2)O(m2) labellings of the training scores matter, and the scan visits the right ones in order.

What people get wrong

“An infinite class can’t be learned.” Thresholds are infinite and learnable from 74 labels at ε=δ=0.05\varepsilon = \delta = 0.05ε=δ=0.05. What decides learnability is the VC dimension, a property of how the class labels points, not how many hypotheses it lists.

“To show the VC dimension is at least ddd, I need every set of ddd points to be shattered.” One shattered set of size ddd is enough. Thresholds on [0,1][0, 1][0,1] with t∈[0,1]t \in [0, 1]t∈[0,1] cannot shatter {0}\{0\}{0}, and their VC dimension is still 1. The asymmetry runs the other way for the upper bound: to show it is less than d+1d + 1d+1, every set of d+1d + 1d+1 points must fail, and one failing set proves nothing.

“The VC dimension is the number of parameters.” Often, not always. One parameter, θ\thetaθ, gives the class ⌈0.5sin⁡(θx)⌉\lceil 0.5 \sin(\theta x) \rceil⌈0.5sin(θx)⌉ an infinite VC dimension.

Your filter ships with a certificate. Look at what it leans on, though.
