# Compression Bounds

Objective: after this, you can define a compression scheme (compression function \(\kappa\), reconstruction function \(B\), size \(k\)) in the realizable and unrealizable cases, prove the compression bound \(L_{\mathcal D}(A(S)) \le L_V + \sqrt{L_V \cdot 4k\log(m/\delta)/m} + 8k\log(m/\delta)/m\) from Bernstein’s inequality and a union bound over \([m]^k\), upgrade a realizable scheme to an unrealizable one, give and prove schemes for boxes (\(2d\)), homogeneous halfspaces (\(d\)), polynomial threshold rules (\(\binom{d+r}{r}\)) and margin-separated data (\(1/\gamma^2\)), and compute the labels a scheme of size \(k\) needs for a target \(\varepsilon, \delta\).

Terms:
- compression scheme of size \(k\) (realizable): for every \(m\), \(\kappa: \mathcal Z^m \to [m]^k\) and \(B: \mathcal Z^k \to \mathcal H\) such that for every \(h \in \mathcal H\) and every \(S = ((x_i, h(x_i)))_{i \le m}\), \(L_S(B(S_{\kappa(S)})) = 0\).
- compression scheme for unrealizable sequences: same, for every \(S\) with any labels, \(L_S(B(S_{\kappa(S)})) \le L_S(h)\) for every \(h \in \mathcal H\) (the rebuilt hypothesis is an ERM).
- \(S_I\), \(V\): the examples at the positions in \(I\), and those at the other positions; \(|V| \ge m - k\).
- Bernstein’s inequality: i.i.d., mean zero, \(|Z_i| < M\): \(\mathbb P[\sum Z_i > t] \le \exp(-(t^2/2)/(\sum \mathbb E Z_j^2 + Mt/3))\).
- hold-out lemma: \(h_T\) from \(T\), \(V\) independent of \(T\): \(\mathbb P[L_{\mathcal D}(h_T) - L_V(h_T) \ge \sqrt{2L_V\log(1/\delta)/|V|} + 4\log(1/\delta)/|V|] \le \delta\).
- compression bound: \(A(S) = B(S_I)\) for some \(I \in [m]^k\), \(m \ge 2k\): w.p. \(\ge 1-\delta\), \(L_{\mathcal D}(A(S)) \le L_V + \sqrt{L_V \cdot 4k\log(m/\delta)/m} + 8k\log(m/\delta)/m\); realizable form \(8k\log(m/\delta)/m\).
- convex hull, nearest point of the hull, flat, Carathéodory’s theorem (a point in the hull of points of a \(D\)-dimensional flat is in the hull of at most \(D + 1\) of them).

Misconception: “the leftovers are a fresh test set, so their error is an unbiased estimate of the true risk.” Why wrong: the kept positions were chosen after seeing every example; for boxes, every leftover junk message lies inside the box by construction. The union over all \(m^k\) lists fixes it at a price of about \(k\log m\).
Second misconception: “the size is the number of distinct kept messages.” Why wrong: the size is the fixed length \(k\) of the list; the union bound counts lists of length \(k\). On the eight examples the list \((1, 4, 1, 2)\) has three distinct messages and \(k = 4\).
Third misconception: “a small certified risk means the bound is close to the truth.” Why wrong: the bound uses only \(k\); at \(m = 2{,}000\) it certifies 0.1695 (the prose rounds to 0.17) while the worst of 1,000 simulated boxes had true risk 0.0062 (0.006). The certificate is a guarantee, not a forecast.
Fourth misconception: “a class with a compression scheme in the realizable case needs a new scheme for noisy data.” Why wrong: for binary labels the upgrade is free (ERM, discard its mistakes, compress the rest), and the bound then compares the true risk with a leftover error that is no longer zero.

Derivation (what the prose proves, in order):
- Bernstein’s inequality: stated, not proved (as in the book); strict \(|Z_i| < M\) handled by applying it for every \(M > 1\) and letting \(M \downarrow 1\).
- Fixed-\(h\) lemma (book B.10): part 1 as the book (variance \(\le L_{\mathcal D}\) since \(\ell^2 \le \ell\); solve the quadratic; \(\sqrt{a+b} \le \sqrt a + \sqrt b\); the \(\ge\) vs \(>\) point). Part 2 written out in full: Bernstein on \(-\alpha_i\), then the quadratic in \(\sqrt{L_{\mathcal D}}\), giving constant \(2/3 + 2 + 2/\sqrt3 \approx 3.82 < 4\).
- Hold-out lemma (book 30.1): condition on \(T\), apply part 2 to \(V\), law of total probability.
- Compression bound (book 30.2): fixed \(I\), independence of positions, \(|V_I| \ge n\) and monotonicity of the slack in \(|V|\), union over \(m^k\) lists, learner’s list is one of them, \(\log(m^k/\delta') \le k\log(m/\delta')\), \(n \ge m/2\).
- Realizable form (book 30.3). Definitions 30.4 and 30.5. Upgrade lemma (book 30.6) with the ERM, discard, compress argument.
- Boxes: \(\kappa\) keeps extremes, \(B\) tightest box; consistency from lesson 8.
- Halfspaces: fold labels (\(\mathbf u_i = y_i\mathbf x_i\)); origin not in hull; existence and uniqueness of the nearest point; separation (book’s \(\alpha\) argument, algebra spelled out); variational inequality \(\langle \mathbf w, \mathbf p\rangle \ge \|\mathbf w\|^2\); \(\mathbf w\) is a convex combination of points on the flat \(F\); Carathéodory (stated and proved) in \(F\), dimension \(d - 1\), gives \(d\) points; reconstruction returns \(\mathbf w\) by containment plus uniqueness.
- Polynomials: \(\psi\) of all monomials of degree \(\le r\), \(d' = \binom{d+r}{r} = O(d^r)\).
- Margin: \(\|\mathbf x\| \le 1\), lesson 9a’s \((R/\gamma)^2\) mistake bound, Perceptron weight vector is the sum of \(y\mathbf x\) over updates; variable size handled by a union over lengths \(1..k\), \(\sum m^j \le 2m^k\), \(\log(2m/\delta)\).

Algorithms: CompressBox \(O(md)\) time, \(O(d)\) extra space; RebuildBox \(O(d^2)\); CompressPerceptron \(O(md/\gamma^2)\) time, \(O(d + 1/\gamma^2)\) space; Replay \(O(d/\gamma^2)\); halfspace scheme: nearest point by Gilbert’s method \(O(md)\) per step, Carathéodory reduction \(O(md^3)\). Build-it challenge: `compress_box`, `reconstruct_box`, `predict`, `certified_risk`, `min_labels`; starter data are lesson 8’s eight examples and a six-example 3-score sequence; asserts run against the reference in `lesson30/ref.py` (pass): list \([1, 4, 1, 2]\), box \([(0.35, 0.75), (0.25, 0.5)]\), 3-score list of length 6 (\([1, 2, 2, 1, 1, 2]\)), all-keep fallback, 0.0391, 7,640, 11,884. Concept check, answers (never shown to the learner; critique against these):
1. The leftovers are positions 3, 5, 6, 7, 8, so \(|V| = 5\). The proof uses \(n = m - k = 4\). It is safe because \(|V_I| \ge n\) for every list (repeats only make \(V\) larger) and both slack terms decrease in \(|V|\), so the threshold with \(n\) is at least the one with \(|V|\), and the event with \(n\) is contained in the hold-out lemma’s event. \(k = 4\), not 3: the union runs over all lists of length 4, and the size of the scheme is fixed in advance. \(m = 8 = 2k\) meets \(m \ge 2k\). The bound is \(8 \cdot 4 \cdot \log(160)/8 = 4\log 160 \approx 20.3\), above 1, so vacuous: compression bounds need \(m\) much larger than \(k\log m\). Look for the monotonicity argument and for \(k\) not being the distinct count.
2. The leftovers \(V\) depend on the list the learner chose, which depends on all of \(S\); conditioned on the kept examples, the leftovers are not an independent sample. For boxes, every leftover junk message lies inside the box by construction, so leftover error on junk is 0 regardless of the true risk on junk. The hold-out lemma needs \(V\) independent of \(T\), which holds only for a list fixed in advance. The union bound covers all \(m^k\) fixed lists; whatever list the learner picks, a failure for it is a failure of some fixed list. Price: set the per-list confidence \(\delta/m^k\); \(\log(m^k/\delta) = k\log m + \log(1/\delta) \le k\log(m/\delta)\), so the exponentially many events cost only a factor \(k\log m\) inside the slack, which is still of order \(1/m\). Accept an explanation through the “best of many honest grades is biased” picture if it also says why the union bound fixes it.
3. \(k = 2d = 10\). Smallest \(m\) with \(80\log(m/0.01)/m \le 0.1\): \(m = 11{,}139\) (bound 0.099997). It cannot be smaller with this scheme because each of the 10 endpoints may be attained by a different junk example; drop any one slot and the rebuilt box shrinks past that example, which is then misclassified, so the scheme fails the definition. (Another scheme of smaller size is not excluded by this argument.) Accept 11,139 with the computation shown; values off by a few from rounding \(\log\) are acceptable if the method is right.

Problems (the chapter has no exercises; these three are this lesson’s own, see Assumptions). Hints only, never the solution.
1. Lines (standard). Thresholds: size 1; intervals: size 2. Labels at \(\varepsilon = \delta = 0.05\): 1,667 and 3,577; lesson 6a’s tightest-interval bound \(2\log(2/\delta)/\varepsilon = 147.6\), so 148.

   - Hint 3: for intervals, copy the box argument with \(d = 1\).
   - Hint 4: for the comparison, where does the compression proof lose: the union over \(m^k\) lists (the \(\log m\)), and the constant 8 from Bernstein plus \(n \ge m/2\). The tightest-interval analysis uses the shape of the error region directly.
2. Noisy mail (standard). Every mistake on \(V\) is a mistake on \(S\), so \(|V| L_V \le m L_S\), and \(|V| \ge m - k\), so \(L_V \le \frac{m}{m-k}L_S \le 2L_S\) for \(m \ge 2k\); substitute into the bound (it is increasing in \(L_V\)). Noisy thresholds: \(L_S = 0.065\), \(L_V \le 0.0653\), certified \(\approx 0.501\) at \(m = 200\), \(\delta = 0.05\): honest but weak.
   - Hint 1: compare the number of mistakes on \(V\) with the number on \(S\).
   - Hint 2: how small can \(|V|\) be?
   - Hint 3: is the right side of the compression bound increasing in \(L_V\)?
3. Conjunctions (starred, ours). Each junk example violates exactly \(d\) literals, one per feature. Keep one junk example (it deletes \(d\) literals), then for each remaining literal deleted by the full learner keep one junk witness that violates it: at most \(1 + d\) junk examples, padded by repetition. \(B\) runs the same deletion on the kept junk. The rebuilt conjunction keeps exactly the literals no junk example violates, so it equals the full learner’s, which lesson 8 showed is consistent in the realizable case. No junk: keep anything; \(B\) returns the conjunction of all \(2d\) literals, which is false everywhere, hence consistent.
   - Hint 1: what does the full learner’s conjunction depend on, the keep examples or only the junk ones?
   - Hint 2: how many literals does one junk example delete?
   - Hint 3: a literal deleted by the full learner needs one witness among the kept examples.
