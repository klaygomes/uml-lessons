The learner that gave up

Last time you gave the filter a real guarantee. You fixed a finite hypothesis class in advance and ran ERM on it: empirical risk minimization, which returns the hypothesis in the class with the fewest mistakes on the labelled examples. The finite-class bound told you how many labelled examples to ask for so that, with high probability, the returned hypothesis had small true risk, meaning a small chance of mislabelling the next message to arrive. You shipped it, and for a while the mail room was quiet.

Then two things happened in the same week.

First, the newsletters. The company’s design team signs up for a weekly digest that arrives with a link, from a sender nobody has in their contacts. Half the design team reads it every Monday. Most people in finance click “junk” the moment it lands. Same message, same four features, opposite labels. One person’s newsletter is another person’s junk.

Second, the honest mistakes. People label in a hurry. Somebody marks a “FREE” prize draw as keep because they were scrolling past it with their coffee in the other hand. Nobody is lying; they are just people.

The learner from last lesson doesn’t cope. Its proof leaned on one assumption, realizability: some hypothesis in the class has true risk 0, so it agrees with every example. Now no hypothesis does. Not one of the four one-feature hypotheses. Not even the memorizer: the same message turns up once as junk and once as keep, and a hypothesis has to give it one label. A learner written to return a consistent hypothesis, one with no mistakes on the examples, now returns nothing at all. A learner that breaks ties on its own returns something arbitrary.

So what should a learner return when the labels themselves disagree? And, the harder question, what could you possibly promise about it? This lesson answers both, and along the way it turns lesson 2’s single result into the definition everything after it is built on.

What the last learner promised

Before we break the old promise, let’s write it down carefully, because the new one will be built in its image.

Recall the setting of lesson 2. Messages arrive from a distribution D\mathcal DD over the domain set X\mathcal XX, the set of every possible message (16 of them in lesson 1’s four-feature world). A labelling function f:X→{0,1}f: \mathcal X \to \{0, 1\}f:X→{0,1} gives each message its true label. The learner sees a training sequence SSS of mmm examples drawn i.i.d. (independently, each from the same distribution), which we write S∼DmS \sim \mathcal D^mS∼Dm, and returns a hypothesis. (The tilde ∼\sim∼ reads “is drawn from”, and Dm\mathcal D^mDm means mmm independent draws from D\mathcal DD, one per example.) Its true risk is L(D,f)(h)L_{(\mathcal D, f)}(h)L(D,f)​(h), the probability that hhh disagrees with fff on a fresh message; the subscript (D,f)(\mathcal D, f)(D,f) names the two things that risk depends on, where messages come from and who labels them.

Lesson 2 proved a guarantee for ERM over a finite class H\mathcal HH when realizability holds. Pick a tolerated true risk, say 0.1, and a tolerated chance of failure, say 0.05. Once the number of examples passed a threshold, 44 for lesson 1’s four one-feature hypotheses, the returned hypothesis had true risk at most 0.1 on all but 5% of training sequences. The threshold grows slowly with the size of the class and quickly as the tolerance shrinks; its exact formula comes back in the next section. Call the tolerated true risk ε\varepsilonε (the Greek letter epsilon) and the tolerated failure chance δ\deltaδ (delta). Then the guarantee reads: true risk at most ε\varepsilonε, with probability at least 1−δ1 - \delta1−δ. That is two promises nested inside each other, and their names give the whole theory its title.

  • Approximately correct. The returned hypothesis is not required to be perfect, only to have true risk at most ε\varepsilonε. The number ε\varepsilonε is the accuracy parameter.
  • Probably. Even that is not promised every time, only with probability at least 1−δ1 - \delta1−δ over the random draw of the training sequence. The number δ\deltaδ is the confidence parameter. (Lesson 2 gave that name to 1−δ1 - \delta1−δ, the chance of success. From here on it goes to δ\deltaδ, the allowed chance of failure. The promise is the same either way, and this lesson always states it in full: with probability at least 1−δ1 - \delta1−δ.)

Probably approximately correct: PAC.

To turn that into a definition, we need one more ingredient: a recipe that, told only ε\varepsilonε and δ\deltaδ, answers “how many examples?”. For the one-feature class, the recipe answers 44 when you ask for ε=0.1\varepsilon = 0.1ε=0.1 and δ=0.05\delta = 0.05δ=0.05. In plain words: there is a recipe that turns any accuracy and confidence you ask for into a number of examples, and with that many examples the learner is, on all but a δ\deltaδ share of training sequences, within ε\varepsilonε of perfect, whatever D\mathcal DD and fff turn out to be. With ε=0.1\varepsilon = 0.1ε=0.1 and δ=0.05\delta = 0.05δ=0.05, the recipe for lesson 1’s one-feature class says 44 examples, and the promise reads: at least 95 runs in 100 return a hypothesis whose true risk is at most 0.1. The simulation in the next section runs exactly that test, line by line: misses counts the runs where the true risk exceeds ε\varepsilonε, and the final assert checks that their share stays at or below δ\deltaδ.

Read the quantifiers slowly, because they are where the strength lives. The function mHm_{\mathcal H}mH​ gets to see ε\varepsilonε and δ\deltaδ, and nothing else. It is chosen before anyone tells you what D\mathcal DD is or which fff labels the messages, and it has to work for all of them. That is the distribution-free ambition from lesson 1, now written as a definition.

Why two parameters, and not just “the learner returns the right hypothesis”? There are two reasons, and you have already watched both happen. The training sequence is random, so there is always a small chance it is useless. Imagine a training sequence that contains only one message, drawn over and over again. No learner can do anything with that, which is why δ\deltaδ can never be zero. And even when the training sequence does reflect D\mathcal DD faithfully, it is finite, so some fine details of D\mathcal DD will be missing from it. The accuracy parameter forgives the learner for small mistakes on those details, which is why ε\varepsilonε can never be zero either.

Sample complexity

The function mHm_{\mathcal H}mH​ is the sample complexity of learning H\mathcal HH: how many examples guarantee a probably approximately correct answer. It depends on ε\varepsilonε, on δ\deltaδ, and on properties of the class. If one function works, any larger function works too, so there are many valid choices. We pin it down by taking the minimal one. For each ε\varepsilonε and δ\deltaδ, mH(ε,δ)m_{\mathcal H}(\varepsilon, \delta)mH​(ε,δ) is the smallest integer that meets the requirements of PAC learning with that accuracy and confidence.

With that, lesson 2’s result becomes a one-line statement.

The finite-class guarantee. Every finite hypothesis class is PAC learnable with sample complexity

mH(ε,δ)≤⌈log⁡(∣H∣/δ)ε⌉.m_{\mathcal H}(\varepsilon, \delta) \le \left\lceil \frac{\log(|\mathcal H|/\delta)}{\varepsilon} \right\rceil.mH​(ε,δ)≤⌈εlog(∣H∣/δ)​⌉.

Read it from the inside out.

  • ∣H∣|\mathcal H|∣H∣ is how many hypotheses the class has: the vertical bars around a set mean “how many elements”.
  • ∣H∣/δ|\mathcal H|/\delta∣H∣/δ divides that count by the allowed failure probability. A smaller δ\deltaδ makes this ratio bigger.
  • log⁡\loglog is the natural logarithm, the power you must raise e≈2.718e \approx 2.718e≈2.718 to in order to get its input. It is what keeps the bound gentle: multiplying the ratio by 10 only adds about 2.3 to its log.
  • Dividing by ε\varepsilonε scales that up: asking for ten times the accuracy costs ten times the examples.
  • The brackets ⌈⋅⌉\lceil \cdot \rceil⌈⋅⌉ are the ceiling, “round up to the next whole number”, because you cannot collect 43.8 examples.
  • ≤\le≤ says this is an upper bound on the minimal sample complexity, not its exact value.

In plain words: the number of examples you need is at most the log of “class size over failure chance”, divided by the accuracy, rounded up. With ∣H∣=4|\mathcal H| = 4∣H∣=4, δ=0.05\delta = 0.05δ=0.05 and ε=0.1\varepsilon = 0.1ε=0.1: the ratio is 4/0.05=804/0.05 = 804/0.05=80, its log is about 4.38, divided by 0.1 that is 43.8, and the ceiling makes it 44. Put numbers in. In lesson 1’s tiny world, the class of one-feature hypotheses has ∣H∣=4|\mathcal H| = 4∣H∣=4. Ask for ε=0.1\varepsilon = 0.1ε=0.1 and δ=0.05\delta = 0.05δ=0.05, and the bound says 44 labelled examples are enough. That gentle growth comes from the logarithm: doubling the class adds a fixed number of examples instead of doubling them.

Here is the bound, and then the claim itself, simulated in lesson 1’s world. There are four features, all messages are equally likely, and a message is junk exactly when the subject says “FREE”. ERM over the one-feature class picks one of its consistent hypotheses at random, as lesson 1’s ConsistentFeature did. A miss is a run whose true risk is above ε\varepsilonε. An impostor, as in lesson 1, is a wrong feature that happens to agree with every example; it has true risk 0.5, so every impostor is a miss.

import math, random
eps, delta = 0.1, 0.05
for H in (4, 30):
    print(H, math.ceil(math.log(H / delta) / eps))
rng = random.Random(1)
def erm(S):
    H = [j for j in range(4) if all(x[j] == y for x, y in S)]
    return rng.choice(H)
for m in (6, 44):
    misses = 0
    for _ in range(20000):
        xs = [rng.choices((0, 1), k=4) for _ in range(m)]
        S = [(x, x[0]) for x in xs]
        misses += (0.0 if erm(S) == 0 else 0.5) > eps
    print(m, round(misses / 20000, 3))
assert misses / 20000 <= delta

It prints 44 and 64. Then come the miss rates over 20,000 runs: 0.023 (about 2.3%) with only 6 examples, and 0.0 with the 44 the bound asks for, well under δ=0.05\delta = 0.05δ=0.05. The bound is safe, and here it is generous. A wrong feature agrees with the true label on a random message half the time, so it survives 44 examples with chance 1/21/21/2 multiplied by itself 44 times, written 2−442^{-44}2−44 (about one in 18 trillion). There are 3 wrong features, so an impostor survives with probability at most 3⋅2−443 \cdot 2^{-44}3⋅2−44.

Finite classes are not the only learnable ones. Some infinite classes are PAC learnable too, such as circles centred at the origin, where a hypothesis says 1 inside the circle. What really decides learnability is not finiteness but a combinatorial measure called the VC dimension, which arrives in lesson 6.

Labels from a distribution

Where do these labels come from, if not from a single function fff? The answer is to let the label be random too.

Picture each example as a two-step draw. First a message arrives, drawn from a distribution over X\mathcal XX. Then someone labels it, and the label is itself a draw: for the newsletter, junk for seven readers in ten and keep for the other three. What gets drawn is a pair, a message together with its label, like (0110, junk). With Y={0,1}\mathcal Y = \{0, 1\}Y={0,1} the set of labels, the set of all such pairs is written X×Y\mathcal X \times \mathcal YX×Y; the ×\times× reads “paired with”, so here there are 16⋅2=3216 \cdot 2 = 3216⋅2=32 pairs. Formally, from now on D\mathcal DD is a probability distribution over X×Y\mathcal X \times \mathcal YX×Y, a joint distribution over messages and labels. It is also called the data-labels generating distribution. It has two parts, one for each step of the draw:

  • the marginal distribution of unlabelled messages, written Dx\mathcal D_xDx​: how often each message arrives, so Dx(x)\mathcal D_x(x)Dx​(x) is the probability that the next message is xxx;
  • the conditional probability of the label given the message, D((x,y)∣x)\mathcal D((x, y) \mid x)D((x,y)∣x): how likely a message xxx is to be labelled junk. The bar ∣\mid∣ reads “given”: we only look at draws where this particular message arrived, and ask about its label.

For binary labels we’ll shorten the second part to one number per message, the share of labellers who call it junk, 0.7 for the newsletter:

  • y=1y = 1y=1 is the event “the label is junk”.
  • The bar makes it y=1∣xy = 1 \mid xy=1∣x: “the label is junk, given that the message is xxx”.
  • (x,y)∼D(x, y) \sim \mathcal D(x,y)∼D says the pair is drawn from the joint distribution, message and label together; ∼\sim∼ reads “drawn from”, as before.

The true risk needs a new definition, because there is no fff to compare with. We redefine it as the probability that a hypothesis disagrees with the label of a fresh example, message and label drawn together. The best anyone can do

Here is a question the realizable world never raised. In the noisy mail room, even a perfect learner, one that somehow knew D\mathcal DD exactly, could not get every label right. The newsletter is junk for seven readers and keep for three; whatever you say, someone disagrees. So what is the smallest true risk that any hypothesis can reach?

Think about one message at a time. For the newsletter 0110, saying junk is wrong with probability 1−0.7=0.31 - 0.7 = 0.31−0.7=0.3, and saying keep is wrong with probability 0.70.70.7. A promise you can keep

So what should the new promise say? Two natural candidates fail before we land on the right one.

“True risk at most ε\varepsilonε” is impossible. In the noisy mail room every hypothesis has true risk at least 0.125, so asking for ε=0.1\varepsilon = 0.1ε=0.1 asks for the impossible, however many examples arrive.

“True risk within ε\varepsilonε of the Bayes risk” is the next idea, and it is also out of reach. Once we make no prior assumptions about D\mathcal DD, no algorithm can be guaranteed to find a hypothesis as good as the Bayes optimal one. The proof is the No-Free-Lunch theorem of lesson 5, the formal version of lesson 1’s lesson that learning needs a bias.

What is left is to compare the learner with a benchmark it could actually hope to match: the best hypothesis in a class you fixed in advance. In the noisy mail room, the best one-feature hypothesis is hFh_FhF​, with true risk 0.175. With ε=0.05\varepsilon = 0.05ε=0.05, the learner would be asked to land at 0.175+0.05=0.2250.175 + 0.05 = 0.2250.175+0.05=0.225 or below, on all but a δ\deltaδ share of training sequences. The definition says exactly that, for every distribution at once.

Definition (Agnostic PAC learnability). A hypothesis class H\mathcal HH is agnostic PAC learnable if there exist a function mH:(0,1)2→Nm_{\mathcal H}: (0, 1)^2 \to \mathbb NmH​:(0,1)2→N and a learning algorithm with the following property.   • h′h'h′ (“h prime”) is a second name for a hypothesis, so it doesn’t clash with hhh, the one the learner returned.
  • LD(h′)L_{\mathcal D}(h')LD​(h′) is the true risk of h′h'h′.
  • min⁡h′∈H\min_{h' \in \mathcal H}minh′∈H​ means “the smallest value as h′h'h′ runs over every hypothesis in H\mathcal HH”: the true risk of the best in the class.

Two words in the name are doing work. “Agnostic” because the learner no longer assumes the class contains a perfect hypothesis; it doesn’t need to believe anything about D\mathcal DD. And the guarantee is relative: success means “not much worse than the best in H\mathcal HH”, not “small in absolute terms”. Be candid about what that means: the strength of such a promise depends entirely on the choice of the class.

Beyond junk and keep

So far a hypothesis has answered one yes/no question. The same framework covers far more, once we stop hard-wiring “count the wrong labels” into the definition of risk.

Multiclass classification. Suppose the mail room adds folders: keep, junk, newsletters, invoices. Now Y\mathcal YY is a set of several labels. Sorting documents by topic (news, sports, biology, medicine) is the same kind of problem, with features such as counts of key words, the document’s size or its origin. Nothing else in the framework changes. An example is still a pair (x,y)(x, y)(x,y), a hypothesis is still a function from X\mathcal XX to Y\mathcal YY, and success is still measured by the probability of a wrong label.

Regression. Now suppose the mail room wants to predict a number, say how many minutes a message will sit unread. The labels are real numbers, and in this case Y\mathcal YY is often called the target set. Other examples are tomorrow’s temperature at 9 p.m., or a baby’s birth weight predicted from three ultrasound measurements, where X⊆R3\mathcal X \subseteq \mathbb R^3X⊆R3. Here “wrong or right” is the wrong question, since a prediction of 41 minutes for a message read after 40 is nearly right. Instead, square the gap: 41 against 40 costs 12=11^2 = 112=1, and 41 against 50 costs 92=819^2 = 8192=81. Squaring makes every gap count as positive and makes big misses cost much more than small ones.

The label is random, though. Say the same message sits 40 minutes for half its readers and 50 for the other half. Then the cost of predicting 41 is 1 half the time and 81 the other half, and its expected value, the average you would see over many draws with each outcome weighted by its probability, is 0.5⋅1+0.5⋅81=410.5 \cdot 1 + 0.5 \cdot 81 = 410.5⋅1+0.5⋅81=41. So we measure a hypothesis by its expected squared difference from the label:

Read it from the inside out.

  • h(x)−yh(x) - yh(x)−y is the gap between the prediction and the actual label.
  • The exponent 2^22 squares it.
  • E(x,y)∼D[⋅]\mathbb E_{(x, y) \sim \mathcal D}[\cdot]E(x,y)∼D​[⋅] is the expected value of what is inside, over pairs drawn from D\mathcal DD: each possible pair’s squared gap, weighted by that pair’s probability, all added up.

In plain words: the average squared miss, over messages and their labels as they actually arrive. For the half-40, half-50 message, predicting 41 scores 41, and predicting 45, right in the middle, scores 0.5⋅25+0.5⋅25=250.5 \cdot 25 + 0.5 \cdot 25 = 250.5⋅25+0.5⋅25=25, which is better.

Two tasks, two ways to score a hypothesis. So we generalize the scoring itself.

Loss functions. The idea is a price tag. Hand it a hypothesis and one example, and it says how much it cost to use that hypothesis on that example. For the junk filter the price is 1 for a wrong label and 0 for a right one, so hFh_FhF​ on row 7 of the table (1100, labelled keep) costs 1. For regression it is the squared gap, so predicting 41 for a message read after 50 costs 81. Formally, take a set H\mathcal HH (our hypotheses) and a domain ZZZ of examples. The learner as code

What is a PAC learner, as a piece of software? Two things shipped together: an algorithm AAA that maps a training sequence to a hypothesis, and a function mH(ε,δ)m_{\mathcal H}(\varepsilon, \delta)mH​(ε,δ) that tells you how many examples to collect. In use, the order is: choose ε\varepsilonε and δ\deltaδ, compute m=mH(ε,δ)m = m_{\mathcal H}(\varepsilon, \delta)m=mH​(ε,δ), have people label mmm messages drawn as they arrive, run AAA, ship the hypothesis. Notice that AAA never sees ε\varepsilonε or δ\deltaδ. They only decide how much data it gets.

For a finite class the algorithm is ERM, now with a general loss. It walks through the class and keeps the hypothesis with the smallest empirical risk.

ERM(H, loss, S):
  best ← +infinity
  for h in H:
    total ← 0
    for i = 1..m:
      total ← total + loss(h, z_i)
    if total < best:
      best ← total
      h_best ← h
  return h_best

Dividing by mmm doesn’t change which hypothesis wins, so the code compares totals. Ties go to the first hypothesis found, which is one legal choice: ERM may return any member of the argmin, the set of hypotheses tied for the smallest empirical risk.

What people get wrong

“Agnostic PAC with ε=0.01\varepsilon = 0.01ε=0.01 means the filter is wrong at most 1% of the time.” It means the filter’s true risk is at most 0.01 above the best hypothesis in the class. In the noisy mail room, with the one-feature class, that is up to 18.5%. The promise is relative to the class, and the class may be weak.

“If I had enough data, I could reach zero true risk.” Not when labels disagree. The Bayes risk is a floor under every hypothesis, and in the noisy mail room it is 0.125. Three readers in ten will always call the newsletter keep, and one hurried click in ten will always go the wrong way.

“Minimizing empirical risk is the goal.” It is the method, not the goal. ERM over all 65,536 hypotheses reached the smallest possible empirical risk on our twelve examples, 1/12, and had true risk 0.35, double that of hFh_FhF​. The goal is a small true risk. Empirical risk is only useful as long as it tracks true risk, and that is exactly what the next lesson must show.

“PAC learnable means the learner finds the best hypothesis.” It finds a hypothesis that is probably approximately as good. Both words stay in. Neither ε\varepsilonε nor δ\deltaδ can be zero, for the two reasons we met at the start. A training sequence can be useless with small but positive probability, and a finite one always misses fine details.

Where these definitions come from

PAC learning was introduced by Valiant (1984), who won the 2010 Turing Award for it. Valiant’s definition required the sample complexity to be polynomial in 1/ε1/\varepsilon1/ε, 1/δ1/\delta1/δ and the size of the hypotheses’ representation, and the runtime to be polynomial too. Lesson 6 will show that whenever a problem is PAC learnable at all, its sample complexity depends polynomially on 1/ε1/\varepsilon1/ε and log⁡(1/δ)\log(1/\delta)log(1/δ). The agnostic formalization is due to Haussler (1992), and the general setting with loss functions follows Vapnik and Chervonenkis (1971) and Vapnik’s later work. From here on, “PAC learning” means the most general model, agnostic with a general loss, and the loss is understood from context. When the realizable case is meant, we will say so. Lesson 7 discusses other notions of learnability.
