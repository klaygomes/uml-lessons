The junk nobody had a feature for

Your invoice filter from lesson 19 works, and it comes with a certificate. But remember what it stood on: two scores that somebody invented, picked out of thirty that somebody else designed. Look back over the whole course and the pattern is the same. The one-feature hypotheses of lesson 1 looked at bits someone chose (“FREE”, a link, an unknown sender). The halfspaces of lesson 9a weighed word counts. The trees of lesson 18 asked questions about features a person had written down. Every filter so far is only as good as the imagination of whoever built its features.

This week that imagination ran out. A new wave of junk is arriving, and every filter you own lets it through. You pull a few hundred of the messages and stare at them. No word gives them away. No sender list catches them. Then a colleague plots two numbers the mail server records for every message anyway, before anyone designs anything: the time of day it was sent and its length. We’ll call these raw signals, and scale each one to lie between −1 and 1, so a message becomes a point u=(u1,u2)u = (u_1, u_2)u=(u1​,u2​) in a square. Here u1u_1u1​ is the send time (−1 is midnight at the start of the day, 1 is midnight at the end) and u2u_2u2​ is the length (−1 is the shortest message, 1 the longest).

On that plot the new junk sits in a slanted triangle, with corners at (−0.6,−0.6)(-0.6, -0.6)(−0.6,−0.6), (0.6,−0.2)(0.6, -0.2)(0.6,−0.2) and (−0.2,0.6)(-0.2, 0.6)(−0.2,0.6). It covers 16% of the square. Inside the triangle, everything is junk; outside, everything is keep, apart from the 2% of messages your users label carelessly.

Why does every old filter miss it? A hand-made feature such as “sent in the morning” or “longer than average” cuts the square with a vertical or horizontal line. A halfspace on the two raw signals, lesson 9a’s tool, cuts it with one slanted line. A triangle is neither: it is the region inside three lines at once. The filter you would need has to invent three new features, “on the correct side of line 1”, “of line 2”, “of line 3”, and then combine them. Nobody thought of those features, and next month’s junk will need different ones.

So here is this lesson’s question. Could a filter build its own features, straight from the raw signals, and learn the junk rule on top of them, all from labelled examples? That is what a neural network does. By the end you will know what such a network can express, how many labels it costs, why training it is hard in the worst case, and how to train it anyway with stochastic gradient descent and an algorithm called backpropagation.

Features that build themselves

Let’s build the triangle detector by hand first, with real numbers, and only then give the parts their names.

Start with one of the three lines. The first edge of the triangle is where 3u1−u2+1.23u_1 - u_2 + 1.23u1​−u2​+1.2 equals 0, and the triangle lies on the side where that number is positive. So here is a tiny machine: take the two raw signals, multiply them by 3 and −1, add 1.2, and answer +1 if the total is positive and −1 if it is not. On the message u=(0.3,−0.1)u = (0.3, -0.1)u=(0.3,−0.1), the total is 3⋅0.3−(−0.1)+1.2=0.9+0.1+1.2=2.23 \cdot 0.3 - (-0.1) + 1.2 = 0.9 + 0.1 + 1.2 = 2.23⋅0.3−(−0.1)+1.2=0.9+0.1+1.2=2.2, which is positive, so the machine says +1. That machine is a halfspace, exactly as in lesson 9a, and its answer is a brand new feature of the message: “on the inner side of edge 1”.

Build two more machines for the other two edges, with weights (−1,3)(-1, 3)(−1,3) and a constant 1.2, and weights (−1,−1)(-1, -1)(−1,−1) and a constant 0.4. Now each message has three new features, each +1 or −1. A message is in the triangle exactly when all three are +1. One more machine finishes the job: add the three features, subtract 2, and answer by the sign. If all three are +1 the total is 3−2=13 - 2 = 13−2=1, positive. If even one is −1, the total is at most 1+1−1−2=−11 + 1 - 1 - 2 = -11+1−1−2=−1, negative. So the last machine computes “all three are +1”, the logical AND.

Before you run it, predict the answer for (0.5,0.5)(0.5, 0.5)(0.5,0.5): a message sent in the afternoon, a bit longer than average. Which of the three features does it fail?

def sign(a):
    return 1 if a > 0 else -1

hidden = [(3, -1, 1.2), (-1, 3, 1.2), (-1, -1, 0.4)]

def network(u1, u2):
    o = [sign(w1 * u1 + w2 * u2 + b) for w1, w2, b in hidden]
    return o, sign(sum(o) - 2)

Notice what happened. Four tiny machines, each doing nothing smarter than “weigh, add, take the sign”, did what no single halfspace can. The first three turned two raw signals into three useful features, and the last one combined them. Nobody designed “inner side of edge 1” as a mail feature. It came out of the weights. And if the weights could be learned from labelled messages, the features would build themselves.

A network as a hypothesis class

Once the graph (V,E)(V, E)(V,E), the activation σ\sigmaσ and the weights www are fixed, the network is a function: it takes the nnn signals of the input layer (that is ∣V0∣−1|V_0| - 1∣V0​∣−1 numbers, since one input neuron is the constant) and returns the outputs of the top layer. Write that function hV,E,σ,wh_{V,E,\sigma,w}hV,E,σ,w​.

The triangle detector and an “either” detector can share a graph. Keep the three feature machines, but give the last machine the weight +2 on the constant instead of −2. Now the total is o1+o2+o3+2o_1 + o_2 + o_3 + 2o1​+o2​+o3​+2, which is positive unless all three features are −1. Same wires, different weights, different hypothesis. That is the whole idea of learning a network: the wiring is fixed in advance, and learning chooses the weights.

The triple (V,E,σ)(V, E, \sigma)(V,E,σ) is called the architecture of the network. Each architecture defines a hypothesis class (lesson 1’s word for the set of hypotheses a learner may return, fixed before it sees data):

HV,E,σ={hV,E,σ,w  :  w is a mapping from E to R}.\mathcal H_{V,E,\sigma} = \{ h_{V,E,\sigma,w} \;:\; w \text{ is a mapping from } E \text{ to } \mathbb R \}.HV,E,σ​={hV,E,σ,w​:w is a mapping from E to R}.

Reading it: the braces with a colon are set-builder notation, “the set of all hV,E,σ,wh_{V,E,\sigma,w}hV,E,σ,w​ such that www is a mapping from EEE to R\mathbb RR”. The subscripts V,E,σV, E, \sigmaV,E,σ are fixed for the whole class; only www varies. In plain words: the class of a network is every function you can get from one fixed wiring and one fixed activation by choosing the weights. The triangle architecture has 9+4=139 + 4 = 139+4=13 edges (three wires into each of three feature neurons, four into the output), so a hypothesis in its class is named by 13 real numbers.

The rest of the lesson takes them in that order.

What a network can express

Let’s start with the most generous question: which Boolean functions can a sign network compute? A Boolean function here is any rule that takes nnn bits, each written as +1 or −1, and returns +1 or −1: a function from {±1}n\{\pm 1\}^n{±1}n to {±1}\{\pm 1\}{±1}. There are 2n2^n2n possible inputs, and a function picks one of two outputs for each, so there are 22n2^{2^n}22n such functions: 256 when n=3n = 3n=3. This is not as narrow as it sounds. A computer stores every real number in bbb bits, so whatever it computes from nnn real numbers is really a Boolean-valued function of nbnbnb bits.

def sign(a):
    return 1 if a > 0 else -1

n = 3
cube = list(itertools.product([-1, 1], repeat=n))

def depth_two(f):
    us = [u for u in cube if f[u] == 1]

AND, OR and NOT are the gates of a Boolean circuit, a network of logic gates, and every program can be unrolled into one. That gives a striking fact about efficient programs. We state it without proof, because the proof is a standard result about circuits rather than about learning.

Fact (efficient programs are small networks). If a function on nnn bits can be computed by a Turing machine (the formal model of a computer program) in at most T(n)T(n)T(n) steps, then it can be computed by a sign network whose size is at most c T(n)2+bc\,T(n)^2 + bcT(n)2+b, for constants bbb and ccc that do not depend on nnn.

The intuition: lay out the computer’s memory as a layer of neurons, one layer per time step; each step is a simple operation on the memory, and the lemma says such operations are a layer of AND, OR and NOT neurons. So every predictor that runs in polynomial time lives in some network class of polynomial size. Hold on to this. It means networks of modest size have almost no approximation error for anything we could hope to compute. The catch comes later.

Seeing it in the plane

Back to the square of raw signals, where the pictures live. In a depth 2 sign network, each hidden neuron is a halfspace: it says +1 on one side of a line. If the output neuron computes the AND of the hidden features, the network says +1 exactly on the region inside every one of those lines. Such a region, an intersection of finitely many halfspaces, is a convex polytope (in the plane, a convex polygon). The triangle is a polytope with three faces. With kkk feature neurons plus the constant, a depth 2 network can carve any convex polytope with kkk faces.

What if the junk sits in two separate triangles, or a shape with a dent? Add one more layer. Let layer 1 hold the lines, layer 2 hold one AND neuron per polytope, and the output neuron take the OR of those. Now the network says +1 on a union of polytopes, which can approximate any shape you can draw.

Here is a depth 3 network for the union of the triangle and its mirror image through the centre of the square (the mirror triangle uses the same three lines with the signal weights negated). We compare it with the true region on a grid of 201×201201 \times 201201×201 messages:

How many labels a network needs

Lesson 6a answered “how many labels” for any class through one number. Recall that a class shatters a set of mmm points when it produces all 2m2^m2m labellings of them, and its VC dimension is the size of the largest set it shatters. The fundamental theorem of lesson 6a says that a class of binary hypotheses is learnable exactly when its VC dimension is finite, and that the number of labels grows in proportion to it. So we want the VC dimension of a sign network with a single output neuron.

Sigmoid networks and the bits of a weight

The VC proof used, in Step 3, that every neuron outputs one of two values. A sigmoid neuron outputs any number between 0 and 1, so the proof does not apply, and the answer really is different: there are sigmoid networks whose VC dimension grows like ∣E∣2|E|^2∣E∣2, the square of the number of weights. The best known upper bound is of order ∣V∣2∣E∣2|V|^2 |E|^2∣V∣2∣E∣2, with a proof beyond this course. Real numbers can hide a lot of information.

But your computer never stores a real number. Lesson 4’s discretization trick says: a class described by numbers that each take one of finitely many values is a finite class. The price: training is hard

So far networks look like the perfect class. Polynomial-size networks express everything that can be computed efficiently, so their approximation error is as small as one could hope, and their label cost grows with the number of edges. What is the catch?

The catch is computation. Recall from lesson 8 that a problem is NP-hard when a polynomial-time algorithm for it would give polynomial-time algorithms for every problem in NP, which almost everyone believes impossible. And recall that ERM, empirical risk minimization, means returning a hypothesis in the class with the smallest empirical risk.

Fact (ERM for small networks is NP-hard). Let k≥3k \ge 3k≥3. For every nnn, let (V,E)(V, E)(V,E) be the layered graph with nnn input neurons (plus the constant), one hidden layer of k+1k + 1k+1 neurons, one of them the constant, and a single output neuron. Implementing the ERM rule for HV,E,sign⁡\mathcal H_{V,E,\operatorname{sign}}HV,E,sign​ is NP-hard.

The third problem at the end of the lesson asks you to prove it. The key is a class you met in lesson 8’s problems: intersections of kkk halfspaces. By the AND lemma, this network contains every intersection of kkk halfspaces, and fitting those exactly encodes colouring a graph with kkk colours, a classic NP-hard problem. Notice how small the network is: three feature neurons, the triangle architecture itself.

Could we escape? Two natural ideas, and the evidence against each:

  • Settle for nearly the best weights. Even finding weights whose empirical risk is close to the minimum is known to be computationally infeasible.
  • Use a bigger network, or another activation. Lesson 8 showed that a hard class can sometimes be learned through a larger, easier one. But under a widely believed cryptographic assumption, intersections of halfspaces cannot be learned efficiently by any learner, whatever hypotheses it outputs. Any network class that contains them inherits the hardness.

So no efficient algorithm is guaranteed to train a network well in the worst case. What people do instead is run stochastic gradient descent and hope. Lesson 14a proved that SGD succeeds when the loss is convex; for networks it is far from convex, so there is no such guarantee. Yet on many practical tasks it finds good weights. The certificate of the last section is what lets us use the result safely: we do not need SGD to be optimal, only to land on a network whose training error is small, and then the certificate tells us what that is worth on fresh mail.

Training by stochastic gradient descent

Gradient descent needs a loss that changes smoothly with the weights. The sign activation is no good: nudge a weight a little and the output either does not change at all or jumps. So for training we switch to the sigmoid, its smooth cousin, and to a smooth loss.

SGD from lesson 14a, in one breath: start somewhere, then repeatedly draw one example, compute the gradient of the loss on it, and take a small step against it. For networks four things change.

First, start at random, near zero, not at zero. If every weight starts at 0 in a fully connected layered network, all the hidden neurons of a layer compute the same function, receive the same gradient, and stay identical forever: the layer behaves like a single neuron. Random starting weights break the tie. Here is the trap, run on three hidden neurons, with the label kept short: junk when the message is inside the long edge of the triangle.

import math, random

def sigma(a):
    return 1 / (1 + math.exp(-a))

def sgd(W0, W1, steps, rng, eta=0.5):
    for _ in range(steps):
        u = [rng.uniform(-1, 1), rng.uniform(-1, 1)]
        y = float(-u[0] - u[1] + 0.4 > 0)
        o0 = u + [1.0]
        a1 = [sum(w * o for w, o in zip(r, o0)) for r in W0]
        o1 = [sigma(a) for a in a1] + [1.0]
        o2 = sigma(sum(w * o for w, o in zip(W1, o1)))
        d2 = (o2 - y) * o2 * (1 - o2)
        for i in range(len(W0)):
            d1 = W1[i] * d2 * o1[i] * (1 - o1[i])
            W0[i] = [w - eta * d1 * o for w, o in zip(W0[i], o0)]
        W1[:] = [w - eta * d2 * o for w, o in zip(W1, o1)]
    return W0

rng = random.Random(0)
zero = sgd([[0.0] * 3 for _ in range(3)], [0.0] * 4, 2000, rng)
print([[round(w, 3) for w in r] for r in zero])

Let’s first do it by hand, on the small network and the message (0.5,−0.5)(0.5, -0.5)(0.5,−0.5) labelled junk, y=1y = 1y=1. We want to know, for each weight, how fast the loss changes when that weight moves.

  o_0 ← x
  for t = 1..T:
    for i = 1..k_t:
      a_{t,i} ← Σ_j W_{t-1,i,j} · o_{t-1,j}
      o_{t,i} ← σ(a_{t,i})

  δ_T ← o_T − y
  for t = T−1 down to 1:

      δ_{t,i} ← Σ_j W_{t,j,i} · δ_{t+1,j} · σ'(a_{t+1,j})
  for each edge (v_{t-1,j}, v_{t,i}) in E:
    partial derivative ← δ_{t,i} · σ'(a_{t,i}) · o_{t-1,j}
  return the vector of partial derivatives

SGDForNetworks(S, (V, E), σ, τ, η_1..η_τ, λ):
  w^(1) ← random, close to 0
  for i = 1..τ:

What people get wrong

“A network with one hidden layer can compute anything, so depth and size don’t matter.” It can compute every Boolean function, but the size that takes is exponential: at least about 2n/n2^n/n2n/n edges for nnn bits, by the VC bound. What polynomial-size networks can compute is everything that runs in polynomial time, which is plenty, but it is a statement about size.

“The label cost of a network grows with the number of neurons.” It grows with the number of edges, the weights. For sign networks the VC dimension is O(∣E∣log⁡∣E∣)O(|E| \log |E|)O(∣E∣log∣E∣). Two networks with the same number of neurons can differ enormously in edges.

“The VC bound covers sigmoid networks too.” Its proof needs each neuron’s output to take finitely many values. Sigmoid networks with real weights can have VC dimension of order ∣E∣2|E|^2∣E∣2. What rescues the engineer is the discretization trick: bbb-bit weights give at most 2b∣E∣2^{b|E|}2b∣E∣ networks and VC dimension at most b∣E∣b|E|b∣E∣.

“Gradient descent finds the best weights.” Lesson 14a’s guarantee needs a convex loss, and the loss of a network is not convex. Worse, finding the ERM weights is NP-hard even for three feature neurons, and is believed hard even approximately. SGD is a heuristic that often works; the certificate is what tells you what the result is worth.

“Start every weight at zero, as lesson 14a did.” In a fully connected layer, zero starts make all neurons of a layer identical forever, so the layer acts like one neuron. Start at small random values.

“Choosing the best of several runs is cheating.” Not when the guarantee is uniform over the class: the certificate bounds the true risk of every network in the class at once, including the one you picked.
