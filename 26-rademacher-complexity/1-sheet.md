# Rademacher Complexity

Objective: after this, you can define the Rademacher complexity of a set of vectors and of a loss class on a training sequence, compute it exactly for small sets and by Monte Carlo for large ones, prove the symmetrization lemma and McDiarmid’s inequality, state and prove the three Rademacher generalization bounds, use the four calculus rules (shift and scale, convex hull, Massart, contraction), bound the complexity of \(\ell_2\)- and \(\ell_1\)-bounded linear classes, and derive the norm-based SVM, hard-SVM, learned-norm and \(\ell_1\) bounds, none of which depend on the number of features.

Terms:
- Rademacher complexity of \(A \subset \mathbb R^m\): \(R(A) = \frac1m\mathbb E_\sigma[\sup_{\mathbf a\in A}\sum_i\sigma_ia_i]\), \(\sigma\) uniform on \(\{\pm1\}^m\).
- Loss class on \(S\): \(\mathcal F\circ S = \{(f(z_1),\dots,f(z_m)) : f\in\mathcal F\}\); its complexity \(R(\mathcal F\circ S)\).
- Representativeness: \(\mathrm{Rep}_{\mathcal D}(\mathcal F,S) = \sup_f(L_{\mathcal D}(f) - L_S(f))\), one-sided.
- Bounded differences with constant \(c\): changing one input moves \(f\) by at most \(c\).
- McDiarmid: \(\mathbb P[f - \mathbb Ef \ge t] \le e^{-2t^2/(mc^2)}\); two-sided radius \(c\sqrt{m\log(2/\delta)/2}\).
- Data-dependent bound: complexity term measured on the same \(S\) used for training.
- \(\rho\)-Lipschitz: \(|\phi(\alpha)-\phi(\beta)| \le \rho|\alpha-\beta|\).
- \(\mathcal H_1, \mathcal H_2\): linear scores with \(\|\mathbf w\|_1 \le 1\), \(\|\mathbf w\|_2 \le 1\).

Misconception: “the Rademacher complexity is a property of the class alone”. Why wrong: it is defined on a training sequence; the same 50,000-word halfspaces have VC dimension 50,000 and measured \(\ell_2\)-ball complexity about 0.19 on 400 short messages.
Second: “smallest training risk gives the best certificate”. Why wrong: act 2’s \(B = 4\) has ramp risk 0.008 but certificate 0.2021; \(B = 2\) certifies 0.1396.
Third: “choosing among classes after looking costs nothing”. Why wrong: the bound is for a fixed class; a menu of eight needs \(\delta/8\) each and a union bound (same device as the learned-norm theorem and lesson 7’s SRM).
Fourth: “\(\ell_1\) always wins for sparse predictors”. Why wrong: it trades \(\|\mathbf x\|_2\) for \(\|\mathbf x\|_\infty\sqrt{2\log 2d}\); for 25-word messages and 10 key words the \(\ell_2\) term is 0.0707 vs 0.2146.

Derivations (all in the prose, complete):
- Half-split gap equals \(\frac2m\sum_i\sigma_ia_i\) (Eqs. 26.2 to 26.3).
- Symmetrization (Lemma 26.2) in five steps: ghost sample, swap invariance, random sign per index, split the sup, \(-\sigma \sim \sigma\).
- ERM in expectation and the Markov \(1/\delta\) version (Theorem 26.3).
- McDiarmid’s inequality, full proof (Doob martingale, conditional mean zero, interval of length \(c\), Hoeffding’s lemma, Markov, optimal \(\lambda = 4t/(mc^2)\), union for two sides). Not proved in the book.
- Theorem 26.5, all three parts, with the bounded-differences constant \(2c/m\) for both Rep and \(R(\ell\circ\mathcal H\circ S)\).
- Lemmas 26.6 (with equality), 26.7, 26.8 (with Lemma A.6 proved by power series and Jensen for log), 26.9 (coordinate by coordinate, absolute value removal by pair symmetry), 26.10, 26.11 (Hölder proved), Theorems 26.12 to 26.15.

Concept check, answers (never shown to the learner; critique against these):
1. (a) One hypothesis: \(\mathcal F\circ S\) has one vector, so \(R = 0\) (each \(\sigma_i\) has mean 0). Part 1 gives \(L_{\mathcal D}(h) - L_S(h) \le c\sqrt{2\log(2/\delta)/m}\) with c = 1, i.e. \(\sqrt{2\log(2/\delta)/m}\), twice Hoeffding’s \(\sqrt{\log(2/\delta)/(2m)}\): the theorem assumes \(|\ell| \le c\) (range \(2c\)) and uses a bounded-difference constant \(2c/m\), while the 0-1 loss has range 1. Accept any correct account of the factor 2. (b) Every labelling of m distinct messages produces every 0/1 loss vector, so \(R = 1/2\); part 1 gives \(1 + \sqrt{2\log 40/1000} = 1.0859\), vacuous, as it must be: by the No-Free-Lunch argument (lesson 5) and lesson 1’s Proposition 1, such a class cannot be learned from m examples.
2. For each pair, \(|a_1 - a'_1| + G(\mathbf a) + G(\mathbf a')\) equals the no-absolute-value expression at either \((\mathbf a, \mathbf a')\) or \((\mathbf a', \mathbf a)\), and the no-absolute-value expression never exceeds it, so the suprema over pairs coincide. This needs the swapped pair to be admissible (both range over the same A) and the rest of the expression to be symmetric (\(G(\mathbf a) + G(\mathbf a')\) uses the same \(\sigma_2, \dots, \sigma_m\) in both terms, which holds because both suprema came from the same draw of those signs when \(\sigma_1\) was averaged). Watch for: claiming \(\phi\) is monotone (not assumed).
3. \(0.035 + 20/\sqrt m + \sqrt{2\log 40/m} \le 0.10\), i.e. \((20 + 2.7162)/\sqrt m \le 0.065\): smallest m = 122,137 (by script; certificate 0.0999998 at 122,137 and 0.10000008 at 122,136). Accept 122,137 or 122,138 with a stated rounding.

Problems (written for this lesson; Chapter 26 has no exercises; presented in the prose as the lesson’s own; hints one at a time, never a solution):
- Problem 1 (thresholds by Massart).
  1. Sort the m scores. Between two consecutive scores, does the loss vector change as the threshold moves?
  2. So there are at most m + 1 distinct loss vectors (one per gap, including the two ends). Apply Massart with N = m + 1.
  3. Every entry is in [0, 1], and so is the mean’s; bound \(\|\mathbf a - \bar{\mathbf a}\|\) by \(\sqrt m\).
  4. At m = 1,000: \(\sqrt{2\log 1001/1000} = 0.1175\).
- Problem 2 (one-sided confidence).
  1. Which direction of deviation does part 1 actually use?
  2. Rerun Step 5 of McDiarmid’s proof for \(f - \mathbb Ef\) only, and set \(e^{-2t^2/(mc^2)} = \delta\).
  3. With constant \(2c/m\), \(t = c\sqrt{2\log(1/\delta)/m}\).
- Problem 3 (the Euclidean bound is tight).
  1. Write \(\sum_i\sigma_i\mathbf e_i\) explicitly. What is its length for every sign vector?
  2. It is \(\sqrt m\) for every σ, so \(R = \sqrt m/m = 1/\sqrt m\), exactly the bound: the lemma cannot be improved in general.
